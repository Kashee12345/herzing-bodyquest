TOPIC 1 📚 Overview of Anatomy & Physiology MUST KNOW▼
🔬 Connects to Lab: Body Structure & Organization — Help Identify a Potentially Failing Organ System

Anatomy = study of body structure; physiology = study of body function. Form follows function: alveoli walls are thin so gases diffuse easily.

Levels of anatomy study

  • Gross (visible) · Microscopic (cytology & histology) · Regional · Systemic.

The 11 organ systems

SystemFunction
IntegumentaryProtection, temperature, vitamin D
SkeletalSupport, protection, blood cell production
MuscularMovement, posture, heat
NervousFast control via electrical signals
EndocrineSlow control via hormones
CardiovascularTransport blood, O₂, nutrients, wastes
Lymphatic / ImmuneFluid balance & defense
RespiratoryGas exchange
DigestiveBreakdown & absorption of nutrients
UrinaryWaste, fluid, pH balance
ReproductiveProduction of offspring

Levels of organization (visualized)

💡 Mnemonic for life characteristics: "Old Mr. Robert's Magic Donkey Runs" — Organization, Metabolism, Responsiveness, Movement, Development, Reproduction.
⚠️ Common confusion: Don't mix up cytology (study of cells) with histology (study of tissues). Both are microscopic anatomy.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Define anatomy and physiology and explain "form follows function."
    Show answer
    Anatomy = structure; physiology = function. Form follows function: alveoli's thin walls allow easy gas exchange.
  2. List the 11 organ systems and one function of each.
    Show answer
    Integumentary (protection), skeletal (support), muscular (movement), nervous (rapid control), endocrine (hormonal), cardiovascular (transport), lymphatic/immune (defense), respiratory (gas exchange), digestive (nutrients), urinary (waste/fluid), reproductive (offspring).
  3. Difference between cytology and histology?
    Show answer
    Cytology = cells; histology = tissues.
  4. Name the 6 characteristics of life.
    Show answer
    Organization, metabolism, responsiveness, movement, development, reproduction.
  5. List 5 environmental survival needs.
    Show answer
    Oxygen, nutrients, water, normal body temperature, atmospheric pressure.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Which of the following best describes physiology?
    A) Naming bones · B) Studying cell structure · C) Studying how organs work · D) Dissecting cadavers
    Show answer
    C. Physiology = study of function.
  2. The smallest level of organization that is considered "alive" is:
    A) Atom · B) Molecule · C) Cell · D) Tissue
    Show answer
    C. The cell is the smallest unit of life.
  3. Which system is responsible for the rapid, electrical control of body functions?
    A) Endocrine · B) Nervous · C) Lymphatic · D) Cardiovascular
    Show answer
    B. The nervous system uses fast electrical signals; the endocrine system is slower (hormones).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A 78-year-old patient presents with poor wound healing, brittle bones, and decreased lung capacity. Which levels of organization are likely involved, and which two organ systems must be evaluated together to address all three symptoms?
Show answer
Multiple levels are affected (cellular → tissue → organ → system). Wound healing implicates the integumentary & immune systems; bone fragility implicates skeletal; lung capacity implicates respiratory. The cardiovascular system links them all because tissue repair, bone remodeling, and oxygen delivery all depend on circulation. Best paired evaluation: cardiovascular + the affected systems (integumentary, skeletal, respiratory).

  • I can list and order the 6 levels of organization.
  • I can list the 6 characteristics of life and 5 survival needs.
  • I can distinguish cytology, histology, gross, and microscopic anatomy.

TOPIC 2 📍 Anatomical Terminology MUST KNOW▼
🔬 Connects to Lab: Body Planes & Sections — Locate a Chimpanzee's Injuries

Anatomical position

Body upright, feet slightly apart, arms at sides, palms forward, head & eyes forward. All directional terms assume this position.

Body planes (visualized)

Directional terms

TermMeaningExample
Superior / InferiorAbove / BelowHead is superior to neck.
Anterior / PosteriorFront / BackSternum is anterior to heart.
Medial / LateralToward / Away from midlineNose is medial to ears.
Proximal / DistalCloser / Farther from trunkElbow is proximal to wrist.
Superficial / DeepNear surface / Toward interiorSkin is superficial to muscle.

Body cavities & abdominal quadrants

Dorsal: cranial + vertebral. Ventral: thoracic + abdominopelvic (separated by diaphragm). The abdomen is divided into 4 quadrants (RUQ, LUQ, RLQ, LLQ) for clinical use.

💡 Tip: The patient's right is the opposite of yours when facing them. Always chart from the patient's perspective.
⚠️ Common confusion: "Anterior" and "ventral" are synonyms in humans, but they are NOT the same as "superior." Many students confuse these because all three sound like "front/up." Anterior = front; superior = above.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Describe the anatomical position and why it matters.
    Show answer
    Body upright, feet slightly apart, arms at sides, palms forward. Universal reference point.
  2. Use directional terms to describe the heart relative to lungs, stomach, spine.
    Show answer
    Medial to lungs; superior to stomach; anterior to spine.
  3. Which plane shows a CT cross-section?
    Show answer
    Transverse (horizontal).
  4. What does the thoracic cavity contain? The abdominopelvic cavity?
    Show answer
    Thoracic: heart, lungs, major vessels. Abdominopelvic: stomach, intestines, liver, kidneys, reproductive organs, bladder.
  5. Appendicitis pain — which quadrant?
    Show answer
    Right Lower Quadrant (RLQ).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The wrist is _____ to the elbow.
    A) Superior · B) Proximal · C) Distal · D) Medial
    Show answer
    C. Distal — the wrist is farther from the trunk than the elbow.
  2. A midsagittal plane divides the body into:
    A) Equal left & right halves · B) Front & back · C) Top & bottom · D) Unequal left & right
    Show answer
    A. "Mid" makes them equal.
  3. The diaphragm separates which two cavities?
    A) Cranial & vertebral · B) Thoracic & abdominopelvic · C) Ventral & dorsal · D) Pleural & pericardial
    Show answer
    B. Diaphragm divides the ventral cavity into thoracic and abdominopelvic.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
You are charting a patient who fell off a ladder. They have a laceration "lateral and inferior to the right knee" and bruising "in the LUQ of the abdomen." Translate these into plain language for a colleague who didn't go to nursing school.
Show answer
A cut on the outside of the right lower leg, just below the knee, and bruising in the upper-left part of the belly (which sits over the spleen and stomach, so monitor for those organs).

  • I can use all 5 pairs of directional terms correctly.
  • I can name the 3 body planes and what each divides.
  • I can identify which cavity contains which organs.
  • I can locate the 4 abdominal quadrants and the major organs in each.

TOPIC 3 ⚖ Homeostasis MUST KNOW▼
🔬 Connects to Lab: Body Structure & Organization — Failing Organ System

Homeostasis = stable internal environment despite external change. Body temp ~98.6°F, blood pH 7.35–7.45, glucose, BP, fluid balance — all kept in narrow ranges.

The control loop (visualized)

Negative vs. positive feedback

TypeEffectExample
NegativeReverses change (most common)Body cools when overheated
PositiveAmplifies changeChildbirth, blood clotting
💡 Memory aid: Negative = "Normalize." Positive = "Push further." Most processes normalize.
⚠️ Common confusion: "Positive feedback" is NOT good and "negative" is NOT bad. The names refer to direction (amplify vs. reverse), not value.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Define homeostasis.
    Show answer
    Maintenance of a stable internal environment despite external changes.
  2. Identify receptor, control center, effector for falling body temperature.
    Show answer
    Receptor: thermoreceptors (skin/hypothalamus). Control: hypothalamus. Effectors: skeletal muscle (shivering), blood vessels (constrict).
  3. Why is negative feedback more common than positive?
    Show answer
    Most variables must stay near a set point; reversing change keeps things stable. Amplifying would push variables out of range.
  4. Give one positive feedback example and explain why amplification is appropriate.
    Show answer
    Childbirth: oxytocin → contractions → more oxytocin until delivery. Used because the event must run to completion.
  5. How does diabetes mellitus represent failed homeostasis?
    Show answer
    Insulin issues — blood glucose can't return to its set point.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Which is an example of positive feedback?
    A) Sweating to cool down · B) Insulin lowering blood sugar · C) Oxytocin release during labor · D) Vasoconstriction when cold
    Show answer
    C. Oxytocin in labor amplifies (positive feedback). The others reverse change (negative).
  2. The "control center" in most homeostatic loops is the:
    A) Skin · B) Liver · C) Brain · D) Pituitary
    Show answer
    C. Brain (especially the hypothalamus).
  3. Normal blood pH is approximately:
    A) 6.0 · B) 7.0 · C) 7.4 · D) 8.0
    Show answer
    C. 7.35–7.45.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with a fever of 102.4°F is shivering uncontrollably. Why is the patient shivering when they're already too hot? Identify the receptor, control center, and effector in this scenario.
Show answer
During fever, the hypothalamic set point is reset upward (e.g., 102°F) by pyrogens from the immune response. The body now "thinks" 102 is normal, so 98.6 feels too cold — receptors in skin send signals to the hypothalamus (control), which activates shivering (skeletal muscle effector) to raise temperature toward the new set point.
☑ Self-Assessment Checklist
  • I can define homeostasis and give 3 examples of variables it controls.
  • I can identify the 3 components of a feedback loop.
  • I can distinguish positive vs. negative feedback and give an example of each.
  • I can explain how disease (e.g., diabetes, fever) reflects failed homeostasis.

Atomic structure

  • Protons (+) in nucleus — element identity. Neutrons (0) — mass; isotopes. Electrons (−) orbit; valence shell determines bonding.

Chemical bonds

  • Ionic — electrons donated. NaCl.
  • Covalent — electrons shared. Polar (H₂O) or nonpolar (O₂).
  • Hydrogen — weak attraction; holds water and DNA together.

Reactions

Synthesis (anabolic) A+B→AB · Decomposition (catabolic) AB→A+B · Exchange parts swap.

💡 Mnemonic: "Oh Crazy Humans, Nitrogen!" — the 4 main elements.
⚠️ Common confusion: Isotopes vs. ions. Isotopes have different neutron counts (same element, different mass). Ions have different electron counts (charged).

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Name the 4 elements that make up ~96% of body mass.
    Show answer
    Oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen.
  2. Compare ionic, covalent, and hydrogen bonds.
    Show answer
    Ionic: electron transfer. Covalent: electron sharing (strongest in body). Hydrogen: weak attraction; vital for water and DNA shape.
  3. Why are polar covalent bonds important for water?
    Show answer
    Partial charges create cohesion, surface tension, high heat capacity, and solvent ability.
  4. Classify: amino acids forming protein; glycogen breaking down; food digestion.
    Show answer
    Synthesis; decomposition; decomposition.
  5. Where in atoms are protons, neutrons, electrons?
    Show answer
    Protons + neutrons in nucleus; electrons in orbital shells.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Sodium (Na) gives an electron to chlorine (Cl), forming NaCl. This is a(n):
    A) Hydrogen bond · B) Ionic bond · C) Covalent bond · D) Polar bond
    Show answer
    B. Electron transfer = ionic.
  2. Digestion is an example of which reaction?
    A) Synthesis · B) Decomposition · C) Dehydration synthesis · D) Anabolic
    Show answer
    B. Decomposition (catabolic) breaks large molecules into smaller ones.
  3. Which subatomic particle determines an atom's identity?
    A) Proton · B) Neutron · C) Electron · D) Nucleus
    Show answer
    A. # of protons = atomic number = element identity.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient receives a contrast dye containing radioactive iodine for a thyroid scan. Iodine-131 is an isotope of normal iodine. What does that mean chemically, and why does the body still take it up?
Show answer
Iodine-131 has the same number of protons as regular iodine (so chemically identical and the thyroid absorbs it the same way) but a different number of neutrons, making it heavier and unstable (radioactive). The thyroid can't tell the difference, so the radioisotope concentrates in thyroid tissue and emits radiation that can be imaged or used for treatment.

  • I can identify the 4 main elements in the body and their approximate proportions.
  • I can describe what protons, neutrons, and electrons do.
  • I can compare ionic, covalent, and hydrogen bonds.
  • I can classify a reaction as synthesis, decomposition, or exchange.
  • I can explain the difference between an isotope and an ion.

TOPIC 5 🧪 Acids & Bases MUST KNOW▼

The pH scale (0–14, logarithmic!)

0–1
strong acid		 2–3
acidic		 4–6
weak acid		 7
neutral		 8–10
weak base		 11–12
basic		 13–14
strong base

Lemon ~2 · Stomach ~1.5–3.5 · Water = 7 · Blood ~7.4 · Baking soda ~9 · Bleach ~12.

Acids vs. bases

  • Acid: donates H⁺ (HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻).
  • Base: accepts H⁺ or releases OH⁻ (NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻).

Why pH matters

Blood pH = 7.35–7.45. Acidosis = below 7.35. Alkalosis = above 7.45.

Buffers

Resist sudden pH change. The blood's main buffer is bicarbonate (H₂CO₃ / HCO₃⁻), aided by lungs (exhale CO₂) and kidneys (excrete H⁺).

💡 Don't forget: The pH scale is logarithmic — pH 3 is 10× more acidic than pH 4 and 100× more acidic than pH 5.
⚠️ Common confusion: Lower pH = MORE acidic, not less. The scale runs opposite to what students intuit.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What does pH measure?
    Show answer
    Concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in solution.
  2. If A is pH 3 and B is pH 5, how much more acidic is A?
    Show answer
    100× (logarithmic; 2 units = 10×10).
  3. Normal blood pH and what is acidosis?
    Show answer
    7.35–7.45. Acidosis = below 7.35.
  4. Define a buffer and name the body's main blood buffer.
    Show answer
    A buffer resists pH change. Bicarbonate buffer (H₂CO₃ / HCO₃⁻) is the main one.
  5. Which 2 organ systems pair with the bicarbonate buffer?
    Show answer
    Respiratory (lungs) and urinary (kidneys).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. A solution with pH 2 contains what relative to pH 4?
    A) 2× more H⁺ · B) 10× more H⁺ · C) 100× more H⁺ · D) Equal H⁺
    Show answer
    C. 2 units of pH = 100×.
  2. A patient's blood pH is 7.30. They are in:
    A) Alkalosis · B) Acidosis · C) Normal range · D) Critical alkalosis
    Show answer
    B. Below 7.35 = acidosis.
  3. Which pair works with the bicarbonate buffer to maintain blood pH?
    A) Liver & spleen · B) Lungs & kidneys · C) Heart & brain · D) Skin & thyroid
    Show answer
    B. Lungs (exhale CO₂) and kidneys (excrete H⁺).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with severe asthma is hyperventilating. Their blood pH measures 7.52. Identify the acid-base disorder, explain why hyperventilation caused it, and predict the kidneys' compensatory response.
Show answer
This is respiratory alkalosis. Hyperventilation blows off too much CO₂, shifting the bicarbonate equilibrium and lowering H⁺ (raising pH). Kidneys compensate by excreting more bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) and retaining H⁺ to bring pH back down toward 7.4.
☑ Self-Assessment Checklist
  • I can place common substances on the pH scale.
  • I can explain why pH is logarithmic.
  • I can define acidosis and alkalosis with their pH ranges.
  • I can describe how the bicarbonate buffer, lungs, and kidneys cooperate.

TOPIC 6 🫐 The Chemistry of Life MUST KNOW▼

Water: 4 essential properties

  • Universal solvent · high heat capacity · cohesion / surface tension · reactant in hydrolysis & dehydration synthesis.

The 4 organic macromolecules

MoleculeBuilding BlockMain Job
CarbohydratesMonosaccharidesQuick energy · glycogen
LipidsFatty acids + glycerolEnergy storage · membranes · hormones
ProteinsAmino acids (20)Structure, enzymes, transport
Nucleic acidsNucleotidesDNA · RNA · ATP

Two key reactions

Dehydration synthesis — joins monomers, removes water (builds). Hydrolysis — adds water, breaks polymers (digestion).

Enzymes

Protein catalysts; lock-and-key specific. Sensitive to temperature and pH — high fever or pH imbalance denatures them.

💡 Mnemonic: "Crazy Lions Push Nothing" — Carbs, Lipids, Proteins, Nucleic acids.
⚠️ Common confusion: Dehydration synthesis removes water; hydrolysis adds water. The "hydro" in hydrolysis means water is the splitter.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. List 4 properties of water.
    Show answer
    Universal solvent, high heat capacity, cohesion/surface tension, reactant in chemical reactions.
  2. Match each macromolecule to its monomer.
    Show answer
    Carbs → monosaccharide; lipids → fatty acid + glycerol; proteins → amino acids; nucleic acids → nucleotides.
  3. Compare dehydration synthesis and hydrolysis.
    Show answer
    Dehydration synthesis builds (removes water). Hydrolysis breaks down (adds water).
  4. What are enzymes and what do they do?
    Show answer
    Proteins. Catalysts that speed reactions without being consumed.
  5. Why do extreme temp/pH ruin enzymes?
    Show answer
    They denature it — change its 3-D shape so substrate no longer fits.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The monomer of a protein is:
    A) Glucose · B) Amino acid · C) Fatty acid · D) Nucleotide
    Show answer
    B. Amino acid.
  2. Which is mainly responsible for storing genetic information?
    A) Protein · B) Lipid · C) DNA · D) Glucose
    Show answer
    C. DNA, a nucleic acid.
  3. Phospholipids are most associated with:
    A) Energy storage · B) Cell membranes · C) Enzyme activity · D) DNA replication
    Show answer
    B. Cell membranes (phospholipid bilayer).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
An infant on a strict liquid-only diet is showing slow muscle growth, frequent infections, and poor wound healing. Which macromolecule is most likely deficient and why?
Show answer
Most likely protein deficiency. Proteins are needed for muscle growth (structural), antibody production (immune defense), and tissue repair (wound healing — collagen). All three findings point to inadequate amino-acid supply.

  • I can list 4 properties of water and why they matter.
  • I can match the 4 macromolecules to their monomers and main jobs.
  • I can describe dehydration synthesis vs. hydrolysis.
  • I can explain what an enzyme is and what denatures it.

TOPIC 7 🍳 The Cell Membrane MUST KNOW▼
🔬 Connects to Lab: Cell Structure — Cell Theory & Internal Organelles

Phospholipid bilayer (visualized)

Two layers of phospholipids — hydrophilic heads outward, hydrophobic tails inward. Embedded with proteins, cholesterol, carbs. Called the fluid mosaic model.

Passive transport (no ATP)

  • Simple diffusion — small/nonpolar (O₂, CO₂).
  • Facilitated diffusion — via channels (glucose).
  • Osmosis — water.

Active transport (uses ATP)

  • Primary: Na/K pump.
  • Secondary: uses gradients.
  • Endocytosis in · exocytosis out.

Tonicity

ISOTONIC
Equal solute. Cell stays same.		 HYPOTONIC
Less solute outside. Water in — cell swells/lyses.		 HYPERTONIC
More solute outside. Water out — cell shrinks/crenates.
💡 Memory aid: "Hyper" = HIGH (solute high outside → water leaves). "Hypo" = LOW (solute low outside → water comes in).
⚠️ Common confusion: Water moves toward HIGHER solute, not lower. So in a hypertonic environment (more solute outside), water leaves the cell.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Describe the fluid mosaic model.
    Show answer
    Phospholipid bilayer with proteins, cholesterol, and carbs that drift like a "fluid mosaic"; selectively permeable.
  2. Three forms of passive transport with examples.
    Show answer
    Simple diffusion (O₂); facilitated (glucose via GLUT); osmosis (water).
  3. Why does active transport need ATP?
    Show answer
    Moves substances against their gradient, which requires energy.
  4. RBC in hypotonic vs hypertonic?
    Show answer
    Hypotonic: swells/lyses. Hypertonic: shrinks/crenates.
  5. Compare endocytosis & exocytosis.
    Show answer
    Endocytosis brings IN; exocytosis releases OUT.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. A red blood cell placed in pure water will:
    A) Shrink · B) Swell and lyse · C) Stay the same · D) Crenate
    Show answer
    B. Pure water = hypotonic; water rushes in.
  2. Which is an example of active transport?
    A) O₂ entering blood · B) Glucose via GLUT · C) Sodium-potassium pump · D) Osmosis
    Show answer
    C. Na/K pump uses ATP directly (primary active transport).
  3. White blood cells engulf bacteria via:
    A) Diffusion · B) Phagocytosis · C) Pinocytosis · D) Exocytosis
    Show answer
    B. Phagocytosis = "cell eating" of solids.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient is dehydrated and the nurse considers IV fluids. Why must the nurse never administer pure (hypotonic) sterile water IV, even though the patient is "low on water"?
Show answer
Pure water would be extremely hypotonic relative to plasma. RBCs would absorb water rapidly and lyse (hemolysis). Instead, isotonic fluids (e.g., 0.9% normal saline) are used so cell volume stays stable while overall body water is replaced.
☑ Self-Assessment Checklist
  • I can describe the fluid mosaic model and its components.
  • I can list 3 passive and 3 active transport mechanisms with examples.
  • I can predict cell behavior in iso/hypo/hypertonic solutions.
  • I can explain why active transport requires ATP.

⚠️ Common confusion: Cytoplasm ≠ cytosol. Cytoplasm = cytosol + organelles. Cytosol is just the fluid.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Cytoplasm vs. cytosol?
    Show answer
    Cytoplasm = cytosol + organelles + cytoskeleton. Cytosol = fluid only.
  2. Trace a protein from creation to export.
    Show answer
    Nucleus → ribosome on rough ER → rough ER → vesicle → Golgi → vesicle → cell membrane → exocytosis.
  3. Why are muscle/liver cells packed with mitochondria?
    Show answer
    High ATP demand.
  4. Rough vs. smooth ER?
    Show answer
    Rough has ribosomes; makes proteins. Smooth: lipids & detox.
  5. State 3 principles of cell theory.
    Show answer
    All living things made of cells; cell = basic unit of life; cells from existing cells.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The "powerhouse of the cell" is the:
    A) Nucleus · B) Lysosome · C) Mitochondrion · D) Golgi
    Show answer
    C. Mitochondrion makes ATP.
  2. Ribosomes attach to which organelle for protein synthesis?
    A) Smooth ER · B) Rough ER · C) Golgi · D) Lysosome
    Show answer
    B. Rough ER (which is why it looks "rough").
  3. The Golgi apparatus is best described as the cell's:
    A) Power plant · B) Library · C) Post office · D) Recycling center
    Show answer
    C. Post office — modifies, sorts, packages.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A child is diagnosed with Tay-Sachs disease, a lysosomal storage disorder where lysosomal enzymes can't break down certain lipids. Predict what happens to neurons over time and why this disease causes severe neurological decline.
Show answer
Without functioning lysosomes, undigested lipids (gangliosides) accumulate inside neurons. This swelling damages cells. Because neurons cannot easily replace themselves, the buildup leads to progressive loss of nervous system function — muscle weakness, seizures, and developmental decline.

TOPIC 9 🪫 Tissues: Epithelial & Connective MUST KNOW▼

The 4 primary tissue types: epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous (last two in Topic 10).

Epithelial tissue

Covers, lines, secretes. Apical (free) & basal (anchored to basement membrane). Avascular, well innervated. Functions: protection, absorption, secretion, filtration, sensation.

Naming = layers + shape: simple/stratified/pseudostratified + squamous/cuboidal/columnar.

TypeWhere
Simple squamousAlveoli, capillaries
Simple cuboidalKidney tubules, glands
Simple columnarStomach, intestines
Stratified squamousEpidermis, mouth
Pseudostratified ciliatedTrachea
TransitionalBladder (stretches)

Connective tissue

Most diverse and abundant. Cells in an extracellular matrix (ground substance + fibers: collagen, elastic, reticular). Mostly vascular (cartilage = exception). Functions: bind, support, protect, insulate, transport, store energy.

  • Loose CT: areolar, adipose, reticular.
  • Dense CT: regular (tendons, ligaments), irregular (dermis), elastic.
  • Cartilage: hyaline, elastic, fibrocartilage.
  • Bone (osseous) · Blood (liquid CT)
💡 Tendon vs. Ligament: "Tendons connect muscle to bone." Ligaments connect bone to bone.
⚠️ Common confusion: Pseudostratified epithelium looks layered but is actually one layer of cells of varying heights. The basal surface anchors all cells.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. List the 4 primary tissue types.
    Show answer
    Epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous.
  2. Why is epithelial avascular and how does it eat?
    Show answer
    No blood vessels. Nutrients diffuse from underlying CT through the basement membrane.
  3. Match: simple squamous / columnar / stratified squamous / transitional.
    Show answer
    Squamous → alveoli/capillaries; columnar → intestine; stratified squamous → epidermis/mouth; transitional → bladder.
  4. 3 components of the extracellular matrix?
    Show answer
    Cells, ground substance, fibers (collagen, elastic, reticular).
  5. Compare tendons and ligaments.
    Show answer
    Both = dense regular CT. Tendons: muscle to bone. Ligaments: bone to bone.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Which tissue type is best for diffusion?
    A) Stratified squamous · B) Simple squamous · C) Cuboidal · D) Columnar
    Show answer
    B. Thin and single-layered — ideal for diffusion in alveoli.
  2. Adipose tissue is classified as:
    A) Epithelial · B) Loose CT · C) Dense CT · D) Cartilage
    Show answer
    B. Loose connective tissue.
  3. Blood is considered which type of tissue?
    A) Epithelial · B) Muscular · C) Connective · D) Nervous
    Show answer
    C. Connective (liquid CT with plasma matrix).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient tears their ACL during a basketball game. Which tissue type was injured, why does it heal slowly, and how does this differ from a torn quadriceps tendon?
Show answer
The ACL is a ligament (dense regular connective tissue connecting bone to bone). It has poor blood supply, which slows healing — cells can't repair quickly without circulation. A quadriceps tendon (muscle-to-bone) is also dense regular CT but has better blood supply because it's intimately connected to the well-vascularized muscle. Both are similar tissue but heal at different rates.

Muscle functions: movement, posture, heat, joint stability, moves substances through hollow organs.

Nervous tissue

  • Neurons — send signals. Dendrites receive, cell body processes, axon sends.
  • Neuroglia — support and protect; far more numerous.

Two key properties: excitability (generate signal) and conductivity (pass it on).

💡 "Skeletal = Stripey & Strong-willed (voluntary)." Cardiac and smooth = involuntary.
⚠️ Common confusion: Both skeletal AND cardiac muscle are striated, but only skeletal is voluntary. Cardiac has the unique intercalated discs.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Compare the 3 muscle types.
    Show answer
    Skeletal: bones / voluntary / striated multinucleated. Cardiac: heart / involuntary / striated, branched, intercalated discs. Smooth: walls / involuntary / non-striated, spindle.
  2. Why is cardiac involuntary, and what feature lets it act as a unit?
    Show answer
    Autonomic control. Intercalated discs spread electrical signals across the entire heart so it contracts together.
  3. 3 parts of a neuron.
    Show answer
    Dendrites (receive), cell body (processes), axon (sends).
  4. What do neuroglia do?
    Show answer
    Support, insulate, protect neurons. Outnumber neurons.
  5. 4 muscle functions.
    Show answer
    Movement, posture, heat, joint stabilization (also moves substances through hollow organs).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Intercalated discs are unique to:
    A) Skeletal muscle · B) Smooth muscle · C) Cardiac muscle · D) Tendons
    Show answer
    C. Cardiac muscle.
  2. The part of the neuron that carries signals AWAY from the cell body is the:
    A) Dendrite · B) Axon · C) Soma · D) Synapse
    Show answer
    B. Axon.
  3. Smooth muscle is found in all of these EXCEPT:
    A) Stomach wall · B) Bladder · C) Biceps · D) Blood vessels
    Show answer
    C. Biceps = skeletal muscle.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A 65-year-old has a stroke that damages part of the cerebral cortex. Despite physical therapy, the affected muscles remain weak years later. Why don't neurons regenerate the way most other cells do?
Show answer
Most mature neurons cannot divide — they are highly specialized for excitability and conductivity. When destroyed (e.g., by stroke), they generally aren't replaced. Some recovery occurs through plasticity, where surviving neurons form new connections, but lost cells aren't regrown the way muscle or epithelial tissue is.

TOPIC 11 🪹 The Integumentary System MUST KNOW▼
🔬 Connects to Lab: Skin Layers & Organ Anatomy — Follow a Skin Cell's Journey

Skin cross-section (visualized)

Skin + accessory structures (hair, nails, sweat & oil glands). Largest organ — ~22 sq. ft., ~10 lbs.

Layers

Epidermis — stratified squamous epithelium; avascular. Sublayers (deep→out): basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum (thick skin only), corneum.

Dermis — dense irregular CT; vessels, nerves, hair follicles, glands.

Hypodermis — subcutaneous; adipose; not technically skin but anchors it.

Skin color — 3 pigments

  • Melanin — brown/black; UV protection.
  • Carotene — yellow-orange; from diet.
  • Hemoglobin — red, in dermal blood vessels.

Accessory structures

Hair (protection, sensation), nails (fingertip protection), sebaceous glands (sebum, waterproofing), sweat glands — eccrine (cooling, watery) vs. apocrine (axilla/groin, puberty, body odor).

💡 Mnemonic for epidermis layers (deep→out): "Come, Let's Get Sun Burned" backward — Basale, Spinosum, Granulosum, Lucidum, Corneum.
⚠️ Common confusion: Stratum lucidum exists ONLY in thick skin (palms, soles). Thin skin (everywhere else) has 4 layers.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Name the 3 layers and tissue type each.
    Show answer
    Epidermis (stratified squamous epithelium); dermis (dense irregular CT); hypodermis (mostly adipose).
  2. 5 epidermal layers deep-to-superficial. Which is missing in thin skin?
    Show answer
    Basale → spinosum → granulosum → lucidum → corneum. Lucidum missing in thin skin.
  3. 3 pigments and source of each.
    Show answer
    Melanin (melanocytes; UV protection); carotene (diet); hemoglobin (blood, in dermis).
  4. Eccrine vs apocrine sweat glands.
    Show answer
    Eccrine: most of body, watery, cooling. Apocrine: axilla/groin, thicker, puberty, body odor.
  5. How does the epidermis get nutrients?
    Show answer
    No blood vessels — nutrients diffuse from the dermis below.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. New keratinocytes are produced in the:
    A) Stratum corneum · B) Stratum lucidum · C) Stratum basale · D) Dermis
    Show answer
    C. Stratum basale.
  2. Which gland is responsible for cooling the body?
    A) Sebaceous · B) Apocrine · C) Eccrine · D) Mammary
    Show answer
    C. Eccrine sweat glands.
  3. The hypodermis is composed mostly of:
    A) Stratified squamous epithelium · B) Adipose tissue · C) Bone · D) Cartilage
    Show answer
    B. Adipose.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient has chronic kidney disease and uses a "tunneled" central line that passes through the skin into a major vein. The exit site keeps getting infected. Which skin layer normally provides the barrier and what's compromised by this line?
Show answer
The epidermis (especially the stratum corneum) is the body's first physical barrier against pathogens. A central line creates a permanent break in that barrier, giving bacteria a direct path from skin surface into the bloodstream. That's why daily site care, sterile dressings, and aseptic technique are crucial.

The 6 functions

🛡 1. Protection
Barrier vs. abrasion, UV, pathogens, water loss.		 🌡️ 2. Thermoregulation

~5% of blood volume; can be redirected.

  1. List the 6 functions of the integumentary system.
    Show answer
    Protection, thermoregulation, sensation, vitamin D synthesis, excretion, blood reservoir.
  2. Skin response to high vs low temperature.
    Show answer
    Hot: vessels dilate, sweat. Cold: vessels constrict, arrector pili contract (goosebumps).
  3. Why is vitamin D important to bones?
    Show answer
    Vit D enables calcium absorption from the gut; calcium is essential for bone strength.
  4. Which burn is least painful at the site, and why?
    Show answer
    3rd-degree — nerve endings destroyed.
  5. What is ABCDE for?
    Show answer
    Identifying possible melanoma. Asymmetry, Border, Color, Diameter, Evolving.

    A) Vitamin C · B) Vitamin D · C) Calcium · D) Melanin only
    Show answer
    B. Vitamin D₃.
  2. A patient has a sunburn that is red and painful but has no blisters. This is:
    A) 1st-degree · B) 2nd-degree · C) 3rd-degree · D) 4th-degree
    Show answer
    A. 1st-degree (superficial; epidermis only).
  3. The MOST dangerous skin cancer is:
    A) Basal cell · B) Squamous cell · C) Melanoma · D) Actinic keratosis
    Show answer
    C. Melanoma metastasizes early.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A 5-year-old patient has rickets (soft, weak bones). On exam, you note the child rarely plays outside, lives in a cloudy northern city, and follows a strict vegan diet without fortified milk. Connect the integumentary, skeletal, and digestive systems to explain how this happened.
Show answer
Without sun exposure, the integumentary system can't synthesize vitamin D from cholesterol. Without dietary D, the digestive system can't absorb calcium from food. Without calcium, the skeletal system can't mineralize new bone properly — resulting in rickets. Treatment combines vit D supplementation, dietary calcium, and (safely) more sun exposure.

📚 Quick Glossary

Foundations & Terminology
  • Anatomy — study of body structure.
  • Physiology — study of body function.
  • Homeostasis — stable internal environment.
  • Anatomical position — standard reference: standing, palms forward.
  • Sagittal/Frontal/Transverse — left-right / front-back / top-bottom planes.
  • Negative feedback — reverses change. Positive feedback — amplifies.
Chemistry
  • Element — pure substance. Isotope — same element, different neutrons. Ion — charged atom.
  • Ionic / Covalent / Hydrogen bond — transfer / share / weak attraction.
  • pH — H⁺ concentration (logarithmic).
  • Buffer — resists pH change.
  • Macromolecules — carbs, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids.
  • Enzyme — protein catalyst. Denatured — lost shape/function.
  • Hydrolysis / Dehydration synthesis — break with water / build by removing water.
Cells
  • Phospholipid bilayer / Fluid mosaic model — cell membrane.
  • Diffusion / Osmosis / Facilitated diffusion — passive transport.
  • Active transport / Endocytosis / Exocytosis — ATP-using.
  • Iso/Hypo/Hypertonic — equal/less/more solute.
  • Mitochondria — ATP. Lysosome — recycler. Ribosome — protein synthesis.
  • Cytoplasm vs cytosol — whole interior vs. fluid only.
Tissues
  • Epithelial tissue — covers/lines; avascular.
  • Connective tissue — cells in extracellular matrix.
  • Tendon vs ligament — muscle to bone vs bone to bone.
  • Skeletal/Cardiac/Smooth muscle — voluntary,striated / involuntary,intercalated discs / involuntary,non-striated.
  • Neuron — dendrites, cell body, axon. Neuroglia — supporting cells.
Integumentary System
  • Epidermis / Dermis / Hypodermis — outer / middle / fat anchor.
  • Keratin — tough epidermal protein.
  • Melanin / Carotene / Hemoglobin — the 3 skin-color pigments.
  • Eccrine vs apocrine — cooling sweat vs pubertal/odor sweat.
  • Sebaceous gland — oil. Arrector pili — goosebumps.
  • Burns 1/2/3 — epidermis / partial / full thickness.
  • ABCDE — melanoma warning signs.

ⓘ What you'll learn in this unit

The reproductive system is the one body system not needed to keep you alive — its job is to produce the next generation. Both sexes share the same plan: gonads make gametes (sperm or eggs) and secrete sex hormones, while accessory organs (ducts, glands, external genitalia) support delivery, fertilization, and (in females) pregnancy. This guide covers the male system, the female system, the cell-division processes that make gametes, the hormonal cycles, and how biological sex is determined.

🧬 The Big Picture: Same Plan, Two Designs

TOPIC 1 ♂️ Reproductive Overview & the Testes MUST KNOW▼

The Shared Plan (both sexes)

Every reproductive system has the same two-part design:

MaleFemale
Gonads (make gametes + hormones)Testes → sperm + testosteroneOvaries → ova (eggs) + estrogen & progesterone
Gametes (sex cells)SpermOva (eggs)
Accessory organsDucts, glands, external genitaliaDucts, glands, external genitalia

The overall function: to produce offspring.

The Testes — Sperm Factories

  • Seminiferous tubules — the "sperm factories" coiled inside each testis where sperm are made.
  • Spermatogenic cells — give rise to sperm.
  • Interstitial cells (of Leydig) — sit between the tubules and produce androgens (testosterone).

The Scrotum & Temperature Control

Viable sperm cannot be made at core body temperature (37°C) — they need about 34°C. That's why the testes hang outside the body in the scrotum. Two muscles fine-tune the temperature:

MuscleAction
DartosSmooth muscle that wrinkles the scrotal skin (reduces surface area when cold).
CremasterSmooth muscle that raises/lowers the testes — pulls them close when cold, lets them descend when warm.
💡 Mnemonic: "Gonads make Gametes" — Testes/sperm/testosterone, Ovaries/eggs/estrogen. Sperm like it cool (~34°C, not 37). Cremaster Carries the testes up; Dartos Draws the skin tight. Interstitial cells = testosterone ("the in-between cells make the hormone").
⚠️ Common confusion: Two different cell jobs in the testis — spermatogenic cells make sperm (inside the seminiferous tubules), while interstitial cells make testosterone (between the tubules). Don't mix them up.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What two things do gonads produce?
    Show answer
    Gametes (sperm or eggs) and sex hormones.
  2. Why are the testes located outside the body cavity?
    Show answer
    Sperm production requires a cooler temperature (~34°C) than core body temperature (37°C); the scrotum keeps the testes cooler.
  3. Which testicular cells make testosterone, and where are they?
    Show answer
    Interstitial (Leydig) cells, located between the seminiferous tubules.

🩺 Clinical Scenario
A condition called cryptorchidism means a testis fails to descend and stays inside the warm abdomen. Why does this threaten fertility?
Show answer
Sperm production needs the cooler scrotal temperature (~34°C). An undescended testis sits at core body temperature (37°C), which impairs or prevents viable sperm production — and also raises the risk of testicular cancer. This is why it's surgically corrected in childhood.

TOPIC 2 😎 Male Ducts & External Genitalia MUST KNOW▼

After sperm are made in the testes, they travel through a series of ducts to reach the outside. Learn the order — it's a common exam question.

The Duct Pathway

  • Epididymis — immature, nonmotile sperm leave the testis and enter here to mature and gain motility. Its muscles contract to expel sperm during ejaculation.
  • Ductus (vas) deferens — runs up as part of the spermatic cord through the inguinal canal into the pelvis; its end (ampulla) joins the seminal vesicle's duct to form the ejaculatory duct. (This is the tube cut in a vasectomy.)
  • Ejaculatory duct — where sperm + seminal fluid mix before entering the urethra.
  • Urethra — the terminal duct; carries both urine and semen (but never at the same time).

External Genitalia

  • Penis — delivers sperm to the female reproductive tract; contains erectile tissue.
  • Scrotum — the skin sac holding the testes (Topic 1).
💡 Mnemonic: Duct order = "SEVE(N) UP" → Seminiferous tubules, Epididymis, Vas deferens, Ejaculatory duct, (Nothing), Urethra, Penis. Epididymis = sperm "Earn their Energy/maturity."
⚠️ Common confusion: Sperm are made in the seminiferous tubules but mature and gain motility in the epididymis — they leave the testis still immature. A vasectomy cuts the vas deferens, blocking sperm but not hormones or fluid.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. List the duct pathway sperm travel from production to exit.
    Show answer
    Seminiferous tubules → epididymis → ductus (vas) deferens → ejaculatory duct → urethra → out the penis.
  2. What happens to sperm in the epididymis?
    Show answer
    They mature and gain the ability to swim (motility); the epididymis also stores them and expels them during ejaculation.
  3. What two fluids does the male urethra carry?
    Show answer
    Urine and semen (at different times).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Sperm mature and become motile in the:
    A) Seminiferous tubules · B) Epididymis · C) Prostate · D) Urethra
    Show answer
    B. The epididymis.
  2. A vasectomy cuts which structure?
    A) Urethra · B) Epididymis · C) Ductus (vas) deferens · D) Ejaculatory duct
    Show answer
    C. The ductus (vas) deferens.
  3. The ductus deferens travels to the pelvis as part of the:
    A) Spermatic cord · B) Inguinal ligament · C) Corpus luteum · D) Broad ligament
    Show answer
    A. The spermatic cord.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A man has a vasectomy. He's reassured that his testosterone, sex drive, and ejaculate volume will be essentially unchanged. Using the anatomy, explain why — and what is different.
Show answer
A vasectomy only cuts the vas deferens, blocking sperm from reaching the ejaculatory duct. The testes still make testosterone (interstitial cells — hormones go into blood, not the duct), and the accessory glands still produce ~95% of semen volume. So hormones, libido, and ejaculate look the same — the ejaculate just contains no sperm, making him infertile.
☑ Self-check: Can you recite the duct order, explain epididymal maturation, and say what a vasectomy cuts? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 3 💧 Accessory Glands & Semen MUST KNOW▼

Sperm are only about 5% of semen — the rest is fluid from three accessory glands that nourish, protect, and transport sperm.

The 3 Accessory Glands

GlandSecretion & role
Seminal vesiclesYellowish viscous fluid — ~60% of semen. Contains fructose (fuel for sperm), prostaglandins, clotting factors.
Prostate glandMilky, slightly acidic fluid with enzymes & citrate; activates sperm. (~30% of semen.)
Bulbourethral glandsThick clear mucus that neutralizes acidic urine in the urethra before ejaculation and lubricates.

Semen — Composition & Job

Semen is white, milky, somewhat sticky: a mix of sperm (~5%), testicular fluid, and accessory gland secretions. It's the transport medium and nutrient source for sperm. Key features:

  • Fructose — provides nearly all the fuel for sperm ATP synthesis.
  • Slightly alkaline — neutralizes the acidic male urethra and vagina to protect sperm.
  • Prostaglandins — stimulate reverse peristalsis of the uterine tubes (help sperm move toward the egg).
  • Clotting factors — coagulate semen just after ejaculation so it sticks in the vagina; fibrinolysin then liquefies it so sperm can swim.
  • Seminalplasmin — an antibiotic protein.
  • Volume per ejaculation: 2–5 mL, with 20–150 million sperm/mL.
💡 Mnemonic: Glands: "Seminal vesicles = Sugar (fructose), 60%"; "Prostate = Plenty of enzymes, milky"; "Bulbourethral = Buffers/lubricates (neutralizes acid)." Semen is alkaline to protect sperm from acid. Sperm = only ~5% of the volume.
⚠️ Common confusion: The seminal vesicles contribute the most fluid (~60%) and the fructose that fuels sperm — not the prostate. Semen is alkaline (basic), which is the opposite of the acidic environments it must protect sperm from.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Which gland contributes most of the semen volume, and what key sugar does it add?
    Show answer
    The seminal vesicles (~60%); they add fructose, the main fuel for sperm.
  2. Why is semen slightly alkaline?
    Show answer
    To neutralize the acidic environments of the male urethra and the vagina, protecting the sperm.
  3. What do clotting factors and fibrinolysin each do to semen?
    Show answer
    Clotting factors coagulate semen right after ejaculation (so it stays in the vagina); fibrinolysin then liquefies it so sperm can swim.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Sperm make up about what fraction of semen?
    A) 5% · B) 30% · C) 60% · D) 95%
    Show answer
    A. About 5%.
  2. The energy source for sperm in semen is:
    A) Glucose · B) Fructose · C) Citrate · D) Lactose
    Show answer
    B. Fructose (from the seminal vesicles).
  3. Which gland's secretion neutralizes acidic urine in the urethra?
    A) Prostate · B) Seminal vesicle · C) Bulbourethral gland · D) Epididymis
    Show answer
    C. The bulbourethral glands.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A man's fertility workup shows normal sperm count but very low semen fructose. Which gland is likely involved, and why could this affect fertility?
Show answer
Fructose comes from the seminal vesicles, so low fructose suggests a blockage or problem there. Without fructose, sperm lack their main fuel for ATP, reducing motility and the ability to reach/fertilize an egg — so fertility can suffer even with a normal count.
☑ Self-check: Can you match the 3 glands to their secretions, explain why semen is alkaline, and recall fructose's role? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 4 🧾 Spermatogenesis & Sperm Structure MUST KNOW▼

Spermatogenesis is sperm formation. It begins at puberty and continues throughout life. From an immature spermatogonium to a finished spermatozoon takes about 64 days, and the testes crank out 100–300 million sperm per day.

The Steps (mitosis then meiosis)

  • A spermatogonium undergoes mitosis: one daughter stays a stem cell (feeder for future divisions), one becomes a primary spermatocyte.
  • The primary spermatocyte goes through meiosis I → two secondary spermatocytes.
  • Each secondary spermatocyte completes meiosis II → spermatids (so 4 spermatids total).
  • Spermatids mature into spermatozoa (sperm). Net result: 1 stem cell → 4 functional sperm.

Sperm Structure

PartContents & job
HeadThe nucleus (DNA), capped by the acrosome — enzymes (lysozyme-type) that help the sperm penetrate the egg.
MidpiecePacked with mitochondria — the energy/ATP source for swimming.
Tail (flagellum)Made of cytoskeleton; propels the sperm.
💡 Mnemonic: "1 spermatogonium → 4 sperm" (males waste nothing — unlike oogenesis, Topic 9). Sperm parts: "Head holds DNA + acrosome drill; Midpiece = Motor (mitochondria); Tail = Travel." Acrosome = "ax" that cuts into the egg.
⚠️ Common confusion: The acrosome (on the head) carries egg-penetrating enzymes; the midpiece (mitochondria) makes the energy. Spermatogenesis yields 4 sperm from each precursor — this is different from oogenesis, which yields only 1 egg.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. How many functional sperm result from one spermatogonium's full process?
    Show answer
    Four functional spermatozoa.
  2. What does the acrosome do?
    Show answer
    It caps the sperm head and contains enzymes that help the sperm penetrate the egg.
  3. Why is the midpiece packed with mitochondria?
    Show answer
    To produce the ATP energy the tail needs to swim toward the egg.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Spermatogenesis begins at:
    A) Birth · B) Puberty · C) Age 30 · D) Fertilization
    Show answer
    B. Puberty (and continues throughout life).
  2. The part of the sperm that provides energy for movement is the:
    A) Head · B) Acrosome · C) Midpiece · D) Tail
    Show answer
    C. The midpiece (mitochondria).
  3. Meiosis I converts a primary spermatocyte into:
    A) Four spermatids · B) Two secondary spermatocytes · C) One ovum · D) A spermatogonium
    Show answer
    B. Two secondary spermatocytes.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A man undergoing chemotherapy is warned it may temporarily lower his sperm count, and is offered sperm banking beforehand. Using spermatogenesis, explain why rapidly dividing sperm precursors are vulnerable.
Show answer
Spermatogenesis relies on constant rapid cell division (mitosis + meiosis) of spermatogonia — producing 100–300 million sperm/day. Chemotherapy targets rapidly dividing cells, so it hits these precursors hard, lowering sperm count. Banking sperm before treatment preserves fertility options.
☑ Self-check: Can you trace spermatogonium → 4 sperm, label the 3 sperm parts, and explain the acrosome and midpiece? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 5 ⚡ Male Sexual Response MUST KNOW▼

The male sexual response has two main events, and each is run by a different branch of the autonomic nervous system — this is the classic exam point.

Erection vs. Ejaculation

EventControlled byWhat happens
ErectionParasympathetic NSErectile bodies flood with blood (arterioles dilate). No arousal → arterioles stay constricted, tissue stays flaccid.
EjaculationSympathetic NSBladder sphincter closes; ducts & glands contract; a spinal reflex makes the bulbospongiosus muscle contract to propel semen.

Orgasm (climax) is the entire ejaculatory event. The bladder sphincter closing is why urine and semen don't exit together.

💡 Mnemonic: "Point and Shoot" — Parasympathetic = Point (erection); Sympathetic = Shoot (ejaculation). Parasympathetic is "rest-and-digest" (relaxed, blood flows in); sympathetic is the "action" reflex.
⚠️ Common confusion: It feels backwards, but erection = parasympathetic (the "calm" division opens the blood vessels) and ejaculation = sympathetic. "Point (Para), Shoot (Sympathetic)" keeps it straight.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Which ANS division controls erection, and what physically happens?
    Show answer
    Parasympathetic; erectile tissues fill with blood as their arterioles dilate.
  2. Which ANS division controls ejaculation?
    Show answer
    Sympathetic.
  3. Why don't urine and semen leave the body at the same time?
    Show answer
    During ejaculation the bladder sphincter constricts, preventing urine from entering the urethra.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Erection is primarily controlled by the:
    A) Sympathetic NS · B) Parasympathetic NS · C) Somatic NS · D) Endocrine system
    Show answer
    B. Parasympathetic ("Point").
  2. Ejaculation is controlled by the:
    A) Parasympathetic NS · B) Sympathetic NS · C) Pituitary · D) Cerebellum
    Show answer
    B. Sympathetic ("Shoot").
  3. The term for the entire ejaculatory event is:
    A) Erection · B) Emission · C) Orgasm · D) Latency
    Show answer
    C. Orgasm (climax).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
Some medications for high blood pressure or anxiety affect the autonomic nervous system and can cause erectile difficulty. Using the control of erection, explain the connection.
Show answer
Erection depends on parasympathetic signals dilating the penile arterioles to let blood fill the erectile tissue. Drugs that blunt parasympathetic activity (or boost sympathetic tone, which constricts those vessels) can reduce that blood inflow, making erections harder to achieve — a common medication side effect.
☑ Self-check: Can you assign erection and ejaculation to the right ANS divisions ("Point and Shoot") and explain the bladder sphincter's role? If yes, you've finished the male system — on to the female system!
TOPIC 6 ♀️ Ovaries & Internal Genitalia MUST KNOW▼

The Ovaries — Primary Female Organ

  • Produce gametes (ova/eggs) and sex hormones (estrogen & progesterone).
  • Contain ovarian follicles — each holds an immature egg called an oocyte.
  • Egg number is set at birth: ~7 million oocytes before birth, down to ~250,000 by puberty (and they keep declining). Unlike sperm, no new eggs are made after birth.

The Uterine (Fallopian) Tubes

Also called oviducts, they receive the ovulated oocyte — but here's the surprise: they don't actually touch the ovary. Instead, finger-like fimbriae create currents in the peritoneal fluid that sweep the oocyte into the tube. (This is also where fertilization usually happens.) Nonciliated cells secrete fluid to keep the oocyte and any sperm moist and nourished.

Key oddity: there's a tiny gap between the ovary and the uterine tube — the egg is "caught," not handed off directly.
💡 Mnemonic: "Females are born with all their eggs" (7 million → 250k by puberty) — opposite of males who make sperm continuously. Fimbriae = fingers that "fish" the egg into the tube across a gap. Fertilization usually happens in the uterine tube, not the uterus.
⚠️ Common confusion: The uterine tube does not connect directly to the ovary — the fimbriae sweep the egg across a small gap. This is why an egg (or fertilized egg) can occasionally implant in the wrong place (ectopic pregnancy). And egg supply is fixed at birth; it isn't replenished.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What does the ovary produce?
    Show answer
    Ova (eggs) and the hormones estrogen and progesterone.
  2. How does the oocyte get into the uterine tube if the tube doesn't touch the ovary?
    Show answer
    The fimbriae create currents in the peritoneal fluid that sweep the ovulated oocyte into the tube across a small gap.
  3. How does egg supply change from birth to puberty?
    Show answer
    From ~7 million oocytes before birth down to ~250,000 by puberty; no new eggs are made after birth.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Fertilization most commonly occurs in the:
    A) Ovary · B) Uterine (fallopian) tube · C) Uterus · D) Vagina
    Show answer
    B. The uterine tube.
  2. The finger-like projections that sweep the oocyte into the tube are the:
    A) Cilia · B) Fimbriae · C) Villi · D) Rugae
    Show answer
    B. Fimbriae.
  3. An immature egg within a follicle is called a(n):
    A) Ovum · B) Oocyte · C) Zygote · D) Polar body
    Show answer
    B. An oocyte.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient has an ectopic pregnancy, where a fertilized egg implanted in the uterine tube instead of the uterus. Using the anatomy of egg pickup, explain how this can happen.
Show answer
Because the egg is swept into and fertilized within the uterine tube (and the tube isn't directly attached to the ovary), if scarring or slow tubal transport delays the fertilized egg's journey, it can implant in the tube wall instead of reaching the uterus — an ectopic (tubal) pregnancy, which is a medical emergency.

TOPIC 7 🍁 Uterus & Vagina MUST KNOW▼

The Uterus

A hollow, thick-walled, muscular organ. Its job: to receive, retain, and nourish a fertilized ovum through pregnancy. It has three wall layers:

Layer (out → in)What it is & does
PerimetriumOuter serous layer (covering).
MyometriumMiddle smooth muscle layer — powerfully contracts to expel the baby during birth.
EndometriumInner mucosal lining — where a fertilized egg implants; this is the layer shed during menstruation.

The Vagina

The birth canal. Two functions: a passageway for delivery of the infant and for menstrual flow (and it receives the penis during intercourse). Resident bacteria keep the vagina acidic, which keeps it healthy and free from infection.

💡 Mnemonic: Uterus layers (out→in): "Perimeter, Muscle (Myo), Mucosa (Endo)." Myo = Muscle = Moves the baby out; Endo = the lining that's shed. Vagina = acidic for protection (opposite of alkaline semen — that's why semen is alkaline, to survive!).
⚠️ Common confusion: The endometrium (inner lining) is what builds up and sheds each cycle — not the myometrium (muscle) or perimetrium (covering). The myometrium is the muscle that contracts during labor.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Name the 3 uterine layers from outer to inner and the job of each.
    Show answer
    Perimetrium (outer serous cover), myometrium (smooth muscle that expels the baby), endometrium (mucosal lining where implantation occurs and which sheds in menstruation).
  2. What are the two main functions of the vagina?
    Show answer
    Passageway for delivery of the infant (birth canal) and for menstrual flow.
  3. Why is the vagina acidic?
    Show answer
    Resident bacteria make it acidic, which keeps it healthy and helps prevent infection.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The uterine layer that contracts during childbirth is the:
    A) Perimetrium · B) Myometrium · C) Endometrium · D) Peritoneum
    Show answer
    B. The myometrium (smooth muscle).
  2. The uterine lining shed during menstruation is the:
    A) Myometrium · B) Endometrium · C) Perimetrium · D) Cervix
    Show answer
    B. The endometrium.
  3. The birth canal is the:
    A) Uterine tube · B) Cervix · C) Vagina · D) Vestibule
    Show answer

🩺 Clinical Scenario
After taking broad-spectrum antibiotics, a patient develops a vaginal yeast infection. Using the vagina's normal environment, explain why.
Show answer
Resident bacteria normally keep the vagina acidic, which suppresses overgrowth of yeast and other microbes. Broad-spectrum antibiotics kill these protective bacteria, raising the pH and allowing yeast to overgrow — leading to a yeast infection.

TOPIC 8 🌸 External Genitalia & Mammary Glands ▼

The Vulva (External Female Genitalia)

The collective term for the external structures is the vulva:

StructureWhat it is
Mons pubisFatty, rounded area over the pubic bone.
Labia majoraOuter folds of skin (the "larger lips").
Labia minoraInner, smaller folds.
VestibuleRecessed area containing the external openings of the urethra and vagina, plus the greater vestibular glands (release lubricating mucus) and the clitoris (largely erectile tissue, homologous to the penis).

Mammary Glands

Mammary glands produce milk for a newborn (lactation). They are present in both males and females, but normally only develop and function in females (under hormonal control after pregnancy). They are modified sweat glands.

💡 Mnemonic: "Vulva = the Visible external parts." Majora = Major (outer, larger); minora = minor (inner). The vestibule is the "lobby" — it holds the urethral and vaginal openings + glands + clitoris. Clitoris ↔ penis (both erectile, same embryonic origin).
⚠️ Common confusion: The vulva is the external genitalia (vagina is internal). The vestibule contains both the urethral and vaginal openings — in females these are separate (unlike the male urethra, which is shared by urine and semen).

TOPIC 9 🥚 Oogenesis MUST KNOW▼

Oogenesis is egg formation. It begins before birth (oocytes start meiosis, then pause) and resumes one at a time from puberty to menopause. The big contrast with sperm: oogenesis makes one functional egg (not four), saving all the cytoplasm for that one cell.

The Steps

  • A primary oocyte divides in meiosis I into a large secondary oocyte (keeps nearly all the cytoplasm) and a tiny first polar body (discarded).
  • The secondary oocyte pauses at metaphase II — and is what's actually ovulated.
  • If a sperm penetrates it, it quickly completes meiosis II → one large ovum (+ another polar body). If not fertilized, it deteriorates.

So fertilization is what finishes the egg — an unfertilized egg never completes meiosis.

Oogenesis vs. Spermatogenesis

Oogenesis (female)Spermatogenesis (male)
Functional gametes per precursor1 ovum (+ polar bodies that die)4 sperm
When it startsBefore birth (then pauses)Puberty
DurationPuberty → menopause (finite eggs)Puberty → lifelong
💡 Mnemonic: "Oogenesis: One egg; Spermatogenesis: Several (4)." The egg hoards the cytoplasm (polar bodies get almost nothing) so the future embryo has nutrients. The egg waits at metaphase II — "frozen until a sperm wakes it up."
⚠️ Common confusion: What's ovulated is a secondary oocyte (paused at metaphase II), NOT a finished ovum. It only completes meiosis II if a sperm fertilizes it. The unequal division (polar bodies) is unique to oogenesis — spermatogenesis splits evenly into 4.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. How many functional eggs result from one primary oocyte, and what happens to the rest?
    Show answer
    One functional ovum; the others become polar bodies that get almost no cytoplasm and deteriorate.
  2. At what stage is the egg when it's ovulated, and what finishes meiosis?
    Show answer
    A secondary oocyte paused at metaphase II; fertilization (sperm penetration) triggers completion of meiosis II.
  3. Give two ways oogenesis differs from spermatogenesis.
    Show answer
    Oogenesis makes 1 functional gamete (vs 4), starts before birth (vs puberty), and is finite/ends at menopause (vs lifelong).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The cell released at ovulation is a:
    A) Primary oocyte · B) Secondary oocyte · C) Mature ovum · D) Polar body
    Show answer
    B. A secondary oocyte (paused at metaphase II).
  2. Oogenesis produces how many functional eggs per primary oocyte?
    A) 1 · B) 2 · C) 4 · D) 8
    Show answer
    A. One.
  3. What triggers the secondary oocyte to complete meiosis II?
    A) Ovulation · B) Menstruation · C) Fertilization by a sperm · D) Puberty
    Show answer
    C. Fertilization by a sperm.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
Older maternal age is associated with higher rates of chromosomal abnormalities. Using oogenesis timing, suggest why eggs ovulated later in life carry more risk.
Show answer
A woman's oocytes begin meiosis before birth and stay paused for years to decades until ovulated. The longer an oocyte sits arrested, the more time for errors in chromosome separation (nondisjunction) when meiosis finally completes — raising the chance of chromosomal abnormalities (e.g., trisomy) with advancing maternal age.

TOPIC 10 🔄 The Ovarian Cycle MUST KNOW▼

The ovarian cycle is the monthly series of events in the ovary. It has two phases separated by ovulation.

The Two Phases

  • Follicular phase — a follicle matures through stages: primordial → primary → secondary → late secondary → vesicular (mature) follicle. Estrogen rises.
  • Ovulation — the mature follicle ruptures and releases the secondary oocyte. This ends the follicular phase and starts the luteal phase (roughly mid-cycle, ~day 14).
  • Luteal phase — the leftover follicle (granulosa cells) becomes the corpus luteum, an endocrine structure that secretes progesterone (and some estrogen) to prepare the body for possible pregnancy.

If Pregnancy Doesn't Happen

The corpus luteum degenerates, so progesterone and estrogen fall — which triggers the uterine lining (endometrium) to shed (menstruation) and a new cycle to begin. If pregnancy does occur, the corpus luteum is maintained to keep hormone levels up early on.

Pituitary hormones drive the whole thing: FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) grows the follicle, and a surge of LH (luteinizing hormone) triggers ovulation and forms the corpus luteum.

💡 Mnemonic: "Follicular → Ovulation → Luteal." Follicular = estrogen builds; Luteal = corpus Luteum makes progesterone ("L for Luteum and progesterone Leads the second half"). Pituitary: "FSH grows the Follicle; LH Launches ovulation." No pregnancy → corpus luteum dies → period.
⚠️ Common confusion: Ovulation marks the switch — it ENDS the follicular phase and STARTS the luteal phase. The corpus luteum (luteal phase) makes mostly progesterone; the follicular phase is dominated by estrogen. Don't confuse the LH surge (triggers ovulation) with FSH (grows the follicle).

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Name the two phases of the ovarian cycle and what separates them.
    Show answer
    Follicular phase and luteal phase, separated by ovulation.
  2. What is the corpus luteum and what does it secrete?
    Show answer
    An endocrine structure formed from the ruptured follicle's granulosa cells; it secretes progesterone and some estrogen.
  3. What happens to the corpus luteum if there's no pregnancy?
    Show answer
    It degenerates, so progesterone/estrogen fall, triggering shedding of the endometrium (menstruation).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Ovulation marks the:
    A) Start of the follicular phase · B) End of the follicular phase / start of luteal · C) End of the cycle · D) Start of menstruation
    Show answer
    B. The end of the follicular phase and start of the luteal phase.
  2. The corpus luteum primarily secretes:
    A) Testosterone · B) FSH · C) Progesterone · D) Oxytocin
    Show answer
    C. Progesterone (and some estrogen).
  3. Which hormone surge triggers ovulation?
    A) FSH · B) LH · C) Progesterone · D) Estrogen
    Show answer
    B. A surge of LH (luteinizing hormone).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
Birth control pills supply steady estrogen and progesterone. Using the cycle's hormone feedback, explain how this prevents ovulation.
Show answer
Steady high estrogen and progesterone provide negative feedback to the pituitary, suppressing FSH and (critically) preventing the mid-cycle LH surge. Without the LH surge, the follicle doesn't rupture — so ovulation doesn't occur, and without a released egg, pregnancy can't happen.

TOPIC 11 🌿 Female Sexual Response & Menopause ▼

Female Sexual Response

Broadly similar to the male response — arousal causes engorgement of erectile tissue (clitoris) and increased secretions — with a few differences:

  • No ejaculation (no equivalent expulsion event).
  • No latent (refractory) period like males have, so the response can repeat without a forced pause.
  • Orgasm is not required for reproduction — fertilization can occur without it.

Menopause

  • Female fertility peaks in the later 20s, declines slowly until ~35, then drops more rapidly.
  • Menopause is officially reached after a full year without menstruation (menses).
  • It's caused by declining estrogen and progesterone as the ovaries run out of responsive follicles.
  • Lower estrogen drives the symptoms (hot flashes, mood changes, etc.) and longer-term health effects (e.g., bone density loss), since estrogen affects many body systems.
💡 Mnemonic: Female response = "like male, minus ejaculation & minus the refractory pause." Menopause = "men-o-pause" = menses pause — confirmed after 12 months with no period; driven by falling estrogen.
⚠️ Common confusion: Menopause is only confirmed after a full 12 months without menses (not just irregular periods). It's caused by declining ovarian estrogen/progesterone — the ovaries, not the pituitary, are running out of follicles.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Give two ways the female sexual response differs from the male.
    Show answer
    No ejaculation and no latent/refractory period; also, orgasm isn't required for reproduction.
  2. How is menopause defined?
    Show answer
    A full year (12 months) without menstruation.
  3. What hormone changes cause menopausal symptoms?
    Show answer
    Declining estrogen and progesterone as the ovaries stop responding/run out of follicles.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Menopause is officially diagnosed after:
    A) One missed period · B) Six months without menses · C) Twelve months without menses · D) Age 50
    Show answer
    C. Twelve consecutive months without menstruation.
  2. Unlike males, the female sexual response lacks a:
    A) Arousal phase · B) Latent (refractory) period · C) Erectile tissue · D) Orgasm
    Show answer
    B. A latent/refractory period.
  3. Menopausal symptoms are primarily due to declining:
    A) Testosterone · B) Estrogen · C) FSH · D) Oxytocin
    Show answer
    B. Estrogen.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A postmenopausal patient is told she's at increased risk for osteoporosis. Using the role of estrogen, explain the connection.
Show answer
Estrogen helps maintain bone density by limiting bone breakdown. After menopause, estrogen drops sharply, so bone resorption outpaces formation and bones lose density — raising the risk of osteoporosis and fractures. This is why bone health is emphasized after menopause.

TOPIC 12 🧬 Sexual Determination & Differentiation MUST KNOW▼

Genetic Determination of Sex

Of the 23 chromosome pairs, one pair is the sex chromosomes (X and Y):

CombinationResult
XX (X from mom + X from dad)Female
XY (X from mom + Y from dad)Male

Since the mother always contributes an X, the father's sperm (X or Y) determines the sex.

The Master Switch: the SRY Gene

The Y chromosome carries the SRY gene (Sex-determining Region of the Y). It's the master switch that initiates testes development. With SRY → testes form → male development. Without SRY (XX) → ovaries form → female development.

Sexual Differentiation Timeline

  • Weeks 5–6: the embryo is sexually indifferent — male and female look identical.
  • Weeks 7–8: XY individuals begin developing male structures (driven by SRY/testosterone).
  • Weeks 8–9: XX individuals begin developing female structures.
💡 Mnemonic: "Dad decides" — mom always gives X, so the father's X or Y sets the sex. SRY = "Sex-determining Region Y" = the switch for testes. Everyone starts sexually indifferent, then "boys first (7–8 wk), then girls (8–9 wk)."
⚠️ Common confusion: The SRY gene on the Y chromosome is the trigger for testes/male development — the "default" path without it is female. And the father's sperm determines sex (X = female, Y = male); the mother always supplies an X.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Which parent's gamete determines the offspring's sex, and why?
    Show answer
    The father's sperm — it can carry an X (female) or Y (male), while the mother's egg always carries an X.
  2. What does the SRY gene do?
    Show answer
    It's the sex-determining region on the Y chromosome — the master switch that initiates testes development (male path).
  3. When is the embryo sexually indifferent?
    Show answer
    Around weeks 5–6, before male (7–8 wk) or female (8–9 wk) structures begin to form.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The chromosome combination for a female is:
    A) XY · B) XX · C) YY · D) XO
    Show answer
    B. XX.
  2. The gene that triggers testes development is:
    A) BRCA · B) SRY · C) FSH · D) HOX
    Show answer
    B. SRY (on the Y chromosome).
  3. The sex of a baby is determined by:
    A) The mother's egg · B) The father's sperm · C) The uterus · D) Hormones at birth
    Show answer
    B. The father's sperm (X or Y).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
In rare cases, a person with XY chromosomes develops along the female pathway because the SRY gene is missing or nonfunctional. Using sexual determination, explain why.
Show answer
The SRY gene is the master switch that initiates testes development. If SRY is absent or nonfunctional, the "default" female pathway proceeds even with a Y chromosome — ovaries (or undifferentiated gonads) form and female structures develop. This shows that it's the SRY gene, not just the Y chromosome's presence, that drives male development.

🎯 Exam Cheat Sheet

♂️ MALE SYSTEM
  • Gonad: testes → sperm + testosterone. Seminiferous tubules make sperm; interstitial cells make testosterone.
  • Scrotum keeps testes ~34°C (cooler than 37). Dartos wrinkles skin, cremaster raises testes.
  • Duct path: seminiferous tubules → epididymis (sperm mature) → vas deferens → ejaculatory duct → urethra. Vasectomy cuts the vas deferens.
  • Glands: seminal vesicles (~60%, fructose fuel), prostate (milky, enzymes), bulbourethral (neutralizes acid). Sperm = ~5% of semen.
  • Semen is alkaline (protects sperm from acid). Fructose = sperm fuel.
  • Spermatogenesis: 1 spermatogonium → 4 sperm; starts at puberty, lifelong; ~64 days. Sperm = head (nucleus + acrosome), midpiece (mitochondria), tail.
  • Response: "Point & Shoot" — erection = Parasympathetic, ejaculation = Sympathetic.
♀️ FEMALE SYSTEM
  • Gonad: ovaries → ova + estrogen & progesterone. Eggs set at birth (7M → 250k by puberty).
  • Uterine tubes don't touch the ovary — fimbriae sweep the egg in; fertilization happens here.
  • Uterus layers: Perimetrium (outer), Myometrium (muscle, labor), Endometrium (lining, sheds). Vagina = birth canal, acidic.
  • Vulva (external): mons pubis, labia majora/minora, vestibule (urethra + vaginal openings, glands, clitoris). Mammary glands in both sexes.
  • Oogenesis: 1 primary oocyte → 1 ovum (+ polar bodies die). Ovulated as secondary oocyte paused at metaphase II; fertilization completes meiosis II.
  • Ovarian cycle: Follicular (estrogen) → OVULATION → Luteal (corpus luteum → progesterone). No pregnancy → corpus luteum dies → menses. FSH grows follicle, LH surge triggers ovulation.
  • Response: like male but no ejaculation, no refractory period. Menopause = 12 months no menses; falling estrogen.
🧬 SEXUAL DETERMINATION
  • XX = female, XY = male. The father's sperm (X or Y) determines sex (mom always gives X).
  • SRY gene on the Y chromosome = master switch that starts testes development. No SRY → female path.
  • Timeline: weeks 5–6 sexually indifferent → weeks 7–8 male parts (XY) → weeks 8–9 female parts (XX).

📚 Glossary

Male Terms
  • Gonad / Gamete — sex organ (testis) / sex cell (sperm).
  • Seminiferous tubules — site of sperm production.
  • Interstitial (Leydig) cells — produce testosterone.
  • Epididymis — where sperm mature and gain motility.
  • Ductus (vas) deferens — carries sperm toward the urethra (cut in vasectomy).
  • Seminal vesicles — add ~60% of semen + fructose.
  • Acrosome — enzyme cap on the sperm head that penetrates the egg.
  • Spermatogenesis — sperm formation (1 precursor → 4 sperm).
Female Terms
  • Oocyte / Ovum — immature egg / mature egg.
  • Follicle — ovarian structure housing a developing egg.
  • Fimbriae — finger-like projections that sweep the egg into the uterine tube.
  • Endometrium — uterine lining that implants/sheds; myometrium — labor muscle.
  • Vulva — external female genitalia; vestibule holds urethral/vaginal openings.
  • Oogenesis — egg formation (1 precursor → 1 ovum).
  • Corpus luteum — post-ovulation structure secreting progesterone.
  • Ovulation — release of the secondary oocyte.
  • Menopause — 12 months without menses.
Shared / Genetic Terms
  • Sex chromosomes — X and Y (XX = female, XY = male).
  • SRY gene — sex-determining region on the Y; triggers testes.
  • FSH / LH — pituitary hormones that grow the follicle / trigger ovulation.
  • Estrogen / Progesterone / Testosterone — the main sex hormones.
  • Meiosis — cell division that halves chromosome number to make gametes.

  • Learn the two systems in parallel — same plan (gonad → gamete + hormone → ducts/glands), different details. The comparison tables do half the work.
  • Memorize the ordered pathways: the male duct route (tubules→epididymis→vas→ejaculatory→urethra) and the ovarian cycle (follicular→ovulation→luteal).
  • Lock in the "opposites": spermatogenesis = 4 sperm/lifelong vs oogenesis = 1 egg/finite; semen alkaline vs vagina acidic; erection (parasympathetic) vs ejaculation (sympathetic).

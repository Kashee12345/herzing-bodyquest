TOPIC 1 🧠 Nervous System Overview & Functions MUST KNOW▼

The nervous system is the body's master controlling and communicating system. Every thought, sensation, and movement runs through it.

The 3 Core Functions

👀 1. Sensory Input
Monitors stimuli inside and outside the body (touch, light, temperature, pain, blood pH, etc.).		 🧠 2. Integration
Interprets the sensory input and decides what to do about it (CNS function).		 ⚡ 3. Motor Output
Sends signals to muscles or glands to respond (the "answer" to the input).
💡 Mnemonic for the 3 functions: "See, Interpret, Move" — Sensory, Integration, Motor.
⚠️ Common confusion: Integration happens in the CNS, not in peripheral nerves. The PNS just delivers messages back and forth.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. List the 3 core functions of the nervous system.
    Show answer
    Sensory input, integration, motor output.
  2. Where does integration occur?
    Show answer
    In the central nervous system (CNS = brain & spinal cord).
  3. Give an example of sensory input and its motor output.
    Show answer
    Touching a hot stove (sensory: heat/pain detected) → integration in spinal cord → motor output: pull hand away.
  4. Why is the nervous system called the "master" system?
    Show answer
    It controls and communicates with every other body system through fast electrical signals.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Sensing a hot stove without yet reacting is which function?
    A) Integration · B) Sensory input · C) Motor output · D) Reflex
    Show answer
    B. Sensory input.
  2. Integration takes place primarily in:
    A) PNS · B) CNS · C) Skin receptors · D) Muscles
    Show answer
    B. Central nervous system.
  3. Motor output produces a response in:
    A) Muscles and glands · B) Sensory receptors · C) Bone · D) Connective tissue
    Show answer
    A. Muscles and glands are the effectors.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with a spinal cord injury at the lumbar level retains feeling in the arms but loses sensation and movement in the legs. Which 2 of the 3 nervous-system functions are interrupted below the injury, and which one might still occur via reflexes?
Show answer
Sensory input from the legs is blocked from reaching the brain, and motor output from the brain can't reach the legs. Integration at the brain level is impaired for the lower limbs. Some spinal reflexes may still work because they integrate at the spinal cord BELOW the injury.

  • I can list the 3 core functions of the nervous system.
  • I can give a real-world example of each.
  • I can identify which body region performs integration.

Anatomical Divisions

DivisionIncludesRole
CNS (Central)Brain + Spinal CordIntegration & command center
PNS (Peripheral)Spinal nerves + Cranial nervesCarries messages to/from the CNS; links body to CNS

PNS — 2 Functional Divisions

DivisionDirectionWhat it carries
Sensory (Afferent)TOWARD CNSSomatic sensory = skin, muscles, joints. Visceral sensory = organs.
Motor (Efferent)AWAY from CNSSignals to muscles & glands (effectors)

Motor Division — 2 Subdivisions

SubdivisionControlTargets
Somatic NSVOLUNTARYSkeletal muscles (you tell your hand to wave)
Autonomic NS (ANS)INVOLUNTARYSmooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands. Splits into sympathetic ("fight or flight") and parasympathetic ("rest and digest").
💡 Memory aids:
  • Afferent = Arriving (at the CNS).
  • Efferent = Exiting (from the CNS).
  • Somatic = Skeletal muscle = conscious.
  • Autonomic = Automatic (unconscious).
⚠️ Common confusion: The PNS does NOT integrate — it just carries signals. All decision-making happens in the CNS.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What 2 structures make up the CNS?
    Show answer
    Brain and spinal cord.
  2. What is the difference between afferent and efferent fibers?
    Show answer
    Afferent = sensory, carries impulses TOWARD CNS. Efferent = motor, carries impulses AWAY from CNS.
  3. Compare somatic and autonomic divisions of the motor system.
    Show answer
    Somatic = voluntary, controls skeletal muscle. Autonomic = involuntary, controls smooth/cardiac muscle and glands.
  4. What are the 2 subdivisions of the ANS?
    Show answer
    Sympathetic (fight or flight) and parasympathetic (rest and digest).
  5. What's the difference between somatic and visceral sensory fibers?
    Show answer
    Somatic sensory = signals from skin, skeletal muscles, joints. Visceral sensory = signals from internal organs.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Spinal nerves are part of the:
    A) CNS · B) PNS · C) Brain · D) ANS only
    Show answer
    B. PNS — spinal & cranial nerves are peripheral.
  2. Conscious control of skeletal muscle uses the:
    A) Autonomic NS · B) Sympathetic NS · C) Somatic NS · D) Visceral NS
    Show answer
    C. Somatic = voluntary.
  3. An efferent fiber carries:
    A) Sensory signals to the brain · B) Motor signals from the CNS · C) Pain signals only · D) Hormones
    Show answer
    B. Efferent = "exits" the CNS.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient experiences sudden tachycardia, sweating, and rapid breathing right before a job interview. Which division of the nervous system is most responsible, and how is it different from the somatic NS?
Show answer
The sympathetic division of the autonomic NS is activated — "fight or flight" response. Heart rate ↑, sweating ↑, breathing ↑. This is INVOLUNTARY — the patient can't consciously stop it (unlike the somatic NS, which controls voluntary skeletal muscle).

TOPIC 3 🧴 Neuroglia (Supporting Cells) MUST KNOW▼

The 2 principal cell types in nervous tissue are neurons (excitable; transmit signals) and neuroglia / glial cells (support cells). Neuroglia support, insulate, and protect neurons and dramatically speed up signal conduction. There are 6 types: 4 in the CNS, 2 in the PNS.

CNS Neuroglia (4 types)

CellKey Functions
AstrocytesMost abundant & versatile. Highly branched. Support & anchor neurons to capillaries; control chemical environment.
MicrogliaSmall, ovoid cells with spiny processes. Act as macrophages — phagocytize microorganisms and dead neurons.
Ependymal cellsLine brain ventricles and spinal cord central canal. Have cilia to circulate CSF (cerebrospinal fluid).
OligodendrocytesForm the myelin sheath in the CNS. Each cell wraps multiple axons.

PNS Neuroglia (2 types)

CellKey Functions
Schwann cellsForm the myelin sheath around PNS axons (one Schwann cell per segment of one axon). Critical for regenerating damaged peripheral nerves.
Satellite cellsSurround neuron cell bodies in PNS ganglia. Function still partly unknown (likely structural support).
💡 Mnemonic for the 6 glia: CNS — "Animals Make Evening Omelettes" (Astrocytes, Microglia, Ependymal, Oligodendrocytes). PNS — "Schwann & Satellite are the Sidekicks."
⚠️ Common confusion: Both oligodendrocytes (CNS) and Schwann cells (PNS) make myelin — but in different locations. Each oligodendrocyte myelinates many axons; each Schwann cell myelinates one segment of one axon.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Name the 4 CNS and 2 PNS neuroglia.
    Show answer
    CNS: astrocytes, microglia, ependymal, oligodendrocytes. PNS: Schwann, satellite.
  2. Which glial cells produce myelin?
    Show answer
    Oligodendrocytes (CNS) and Schwann cells (PNS).
  3. Which glial cell acts as the immune defender (macrophage) of the CNS?
    Show answer
    Microglia.
  4. What role do ependymal cells play?
    Show answer
    Line ventricles & central canal; their cilia circulate CSF.
  5. Why are Schwann cells especially important?
    Show answer
    They myelinate PNS axons AND are essential for the regeneration of damaged peripheral nerve fibers.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Myelin in the CNS is made by:
    A) Schwann cells · B) Astrocytes · C) Oligodendrocytes · D) Microglia
    Show answer
    C. Oligodendrocytes (CNS).
  2. Which glial cell circulates CSF?
    A) Ependymal · B) Astrocyte · C) Microglia · D) Satellite
    Show answer
    A. Ependymal cells have cilia that move CSF.
  3. The most abundant glial cell type is:
    A) Microglia · B) Astrocytes · C) Oligodendrocytes · D) Schwann cells
    Show answer
    B. Astrocytes — most abundant & versatile.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with multiple sclerosis (MS) has lost the myelin around many CNS axons due to an autoimmune attack. Which glial cell type is the target, and why does this cause widespread symptoms like vision changes, weakness, and coordination problems?
Show answer
MS attacks oligodendrocytes and the myelin they produce. Without myelin, action potential conduction along CNS axons becomes slow, irregular, or fails entirely. Because demyelination can occur in many CNS locations, symptoms are widespread — optic nerve (vision), motor pathways (weakness), cerebellar tracts (coordination), etc.
☑ Self-Assessment Checklist
  • I can name all 6 neuroglial cell types and their locations.
  • I can identify which 2 cell types produce myelin and where.
  • I can describe the unique function of each (anchor, immunity, CSF, myelin, regeneration, support).

TOPIC 4 🧸 Neuron Anatomy & Myelin Sheath MUST KNOW▼

Neurons are the structural and functional units of the nervous system. They're long-lived (over 100 years), amitotic (don't divide), and have a high metabolic rate.

Neuron Structure (visualized)

The 3 Main Parts of a Neuron

PartFunction
Cell body (soma / perikaryon)Major biosynthetic center. Contains nucleus. Has the axon hillock (cone-shaped area where the axon starts and where action potentials begin).
DendritesReceptive region. Carry impulses TOWARD the soma. A neuron typically has many.
AxonCarries impulses AWAY from soma. Usually only one per neuron, but can branch. Ends in axon terminals that release neurotransmitter at a synapse.

Myelin Sheath

  • Made by Schwann cells (PNS) and oligodendrocytes (CNS).
  • 3 jobs: protects axon, electrically insulates it, increases AP conduction speed.
  • Nodes of Ranvier — gaps between myelin segments. Action potentials "jump" node to node (saltatory conduction; see Topic 9).
  • Axon terminals (synaptic end bulbs) — release neurotransmitter into the synapse.

Vocabulary You'll Need

TermCNS EquivalentPNS Equivalent
Group of cell bodiesNucleus (a confusing name)Ganglion
Bundle of axons (processes)TractNerve
💡 Direction trick: "Dendrite = Delivers IN." "Axon = Away out." And one cell body has many dendrites but usually just one axon.
⚠️ Common confusion: The word "nucleus" has 2 meanings in nervous tissue. (1) The organelle inside a cell. (2) A cluster of neuron cell bodies in the CNS. Context tells you which.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. List the 3 main parts of a neuron and their functions.
    Show answer
    Cell body (soma) = biosynthetic center. Dendrites = receive signals. Axon = sends signals away.
  2. What is the axon hillock and what happens there?
    Show answer
    A cone-shaped region where the axon emerges from the soma. Action potentials are generated here.
  3. List the 3 jobs of the myelin sheath.
    Show answer
    Protects the axon, electrically insulates it, increases conduction speed.
  4. What is a node of Ranvier?
    Show answer
    A gap between adjacent myelin sheath segments. Site of voltage-gated Na+ channels for saltatory conduction.
  5. Compare these pairs: nucleus vs ganglion; tract vs nerve.
    Show answer
    Nucleus (CNS) and ganglion (PNS) are both groups of cell bodies. Tract (CNS) and nerve (PNS) are both bundles of axons.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Signals are RECEIVED by which part of a neuron?
    A) Axon · B) Axon terminals · C) Dendrites · D) Myelin
    Show answer
    C. Dendrites are the receptive region.
  2. A bundle of axons in the PNS is called a:
    A) Tract · B) Ganglion · C) Nucleus · D) Nerve
    Show answer
    D. Nerve.
  3. Neurons are described as amitotic, meaning:
    A) They divide rapidly · B) They don't normally divide · C) They have no nucleus · D) They die young
    Show answer
    B. Don't divide — why nervous system damage often doesn't heal.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient suffers a lacerated peripheral nerve in their forearm. Will it regenerate? Why or why not, and which cell helps?
Show answer
Peripheral nerve fibers CAN regenerate — slowly — if the cell body survives. Schwann cells form regeneration "tubes" that guide the regrowing axon. CNS axons (myelinated by oligodendrocytes) generally do NOT regenerate after injury, which is why spinal cord injuries are often permanent.

  • I can label a diagram of a neuron with all parts.
  • I can describe the 3 jobs of the myelin sheath.
  • I can identify the node of Ranvier and the axon hillock.
  • I can compare nucleus/ganglion and tract/nerve terminology.

TOPIC 5 🌈 White vs. Gray Matter GOOD TO KNOW▼

The brain and spinal cord look distinctly white or gray depending on what dominates the tissue.

⚫ WHITE MATTER
Dense collections of myelinated axons (the myelin gives it its white sheen). Appears as white areas in cross-sections of the CNS.		 ⚪ GRAY MATTER
Mostly cell bodies (soma) and unmyelinated fibers. Appears gray. Where integration and processing happen.

Where to Find Each

  • Brain: Gray matter on the OUTSIDE (cerebral cortex). White matter on the INSIDE.
  • Spinal cord: Gray matter on the INSIDE (looks like a butterfly or H). White matter on the OUTSIDE.
💡 Memory aid: WHITE = WIRES (myelinated axons carrying signals). GRAY = "GRAY" matter where the GRAY thinking happens (cell bodies process).
⚠️ Common confusion: The location of gray matter is OPPOSITE in brain vs spinal cord. Brain = gray outside, white inside. Spinal cord = gray inside, white outside.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What gives white matter its color?
    Show answer
    Myelin around axons.
  2. What dominates gray matter?
    Show answer
    Cell bodies (soma) and unmyelinated fibers.
  3. Compare gray/white matter location in the brain vs. spinal cord.
    Show answer
    Brain: gray outside (cortex), white inside. Spinal cord: gray inside (H shape), white outside.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. White matter is mostly:
    A) Cell bodies · B) Myelinated axons · C) Glia only · D) Synapses
    Show answer
    B. Myelinated axons.
  2. The cerebral cortex (outer brain layer) is:
    A) White matter · B) Gray matter · C) CSF · D) Meninges
    Show answer
    B. Gray matter on the outside of the brain.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
An MRI shows white matter lesions consistent with MS. Why does damage to white matter affect signal speed but not necessarily processing?
Show answer
White matter = the "wires" (myelinated axons connecting regions). Damage slows or blocks signals between regions. Gray matter (cell bodies) is where processing happens — that may be intact even if signaling between regions is impaired.
☑ Self-Assessment Checklist
  • I can describe what makes white matter white.
  • I can identify what makes up gray matter.
  • I can locate each in the brain vs. spinal cord.

🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with retinitis pigmentosa progressively loses vision because of damage to retinal neurons. Which structural class of neurons is most directly affected, and what visual function depends on them?
Show answer
Retinal bipolar neurons connect photoreceptors (rods & cones) to ganglion cells, which form the optic nerve. Damage interrupts the visual signal from photoreceptors to brain, causing progressive vision loss.

TOPIC 7 ⚡ Action Potential — The 4 Phases MUST KNOW▼

An action potential (AP) is a nerve impulse — a brief reversal of membrane potential that travels down an axon. APs are the way neurons communicate. Only neurons and muscle cells can generate them, and they don't lose strength over distance.

Action Potential Trace (visualized)

The 4 Phases

PhaseWhat's happeningChannels
1. RestingMembrane at −70 mV. Na/K pump maintains gradient.Na⁺ and K⁺ channels CLOSED
2. DepolarizationStimulus reaches threshold; Na⁺ rushes IN; membrane becomes positive (+30 mV).Na⁺ channels OPEN
3. RepolarizationK⁺ rushes OUT; membrane voltage drops back toward rest.K⁺ channels OPEN
4. HyperpolarizationSome K⁺ channels stay open too long; voltage dips slightly below rest. Then channels reset.Na⁺ resets; K⁺ closes slowly
💡 Memory aid: "Na rushes in, K kicks out." Depolarization = Na+ IN. Repolarization = K+ OUT. Both ions flow DOWN their gradients through voltage-gated channels.
⚠️ Common confusion: Resting membrane potential is NEGATIVE inside (−70 mV). "Depolarization" means becoming LESS negative (closer to 0 or positive). Don't think "more polarized = more negative."

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. List the 4 phases of an action potential.
    Show answer
    Resting, depolarization, repolarization, hyperpolarization.
  2. Which ion rushes in during depolarization? Which rushes out during repolarization?
    Show answer
    Na⁺ in (depolarization). K⁺ out (repolarization).
  3. Why does hyperpolarization happen?
    Show answer
    Some K⁺ channels stay open slightly too long, causing voltage to dip below resting potential before stabilizing.
  4. What is the normal resting membrane potential?
    Show answer
    About −70 mV (inside negative compared to outside).
  5. Which cell types can generate APs?
    Show answer
    Only neurons and muscle cells.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Depolarization is caused by:
    A) K⁺ leaving the cell · B) Na⁺ entering the cell · C) Cl− entering · D) Ca²⁺ leaving
    Show answer
    B. Na⁺ entering.
  2. An AP does NOT lose strength as it travels because:
    A) It's always regenerated at the next site · B) The myelin holds it in · C) Sodium pumps prevent loss · D) Distance doesn't apply
    Show answer
    A. The signal is regenerated as it propagates — that's why it doesn't decrease in strength.
  3. Hyperpolarization brings the membrane voltage:
    A) Above resting · B) To zero · C) Below resting (more negative) · D) Above +30 mV
    Show answer
    C. Below resting — more negative than −70 mV briefly.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
Lidocaine is a local anesthetic that blocks voltage-gated Na⁺ channels. How does this prevent pain, and which phase of the AP is most affected?
Show answer
Lidocaine blocks Na⁺ channels, preventing depolarization. Without depolarization, no AP can be generated, so pain signals from sensory neurons can't travel to the CNS. The patient feels nothing in that area until the drug wears off.

  • I can list and order the 4 phases of an AP.
  • I can identify which ion moves and which way during depolarization and repolarization.
  • I can explain why APs don't decrease over distance.
  • I can state the resting membrane potential.

TOPIC 8 🎯 Threshold, All-or-None & Coding Intensity MUST KNOW▼

Threshold

The membrane must depolarize by about 15–20 mV from rest (to roughly −55 mV) to trigger an AP.

  • Subthreshold stimuli (weak) → NO action potential.
  • Threshold (or stronger) stimuli → AP fires.

The All-or-None Principle

An action potential either happens completely, or it doesn't happen at all. There's no "half" AP.

  • Every AP in a given neuron has the same size and speed.
  • Stronger stimuli don't make bigger APs.

How the Brain Codes Stimulus Intensity

If all APs are the same, how do you tell a soft poke from a hard pinch?

  • Frequency: stronger stimuli generate APs MORE OFTEN. The brain reads frequency (more APs per second = stronger stimulus).
  • Recruitment: stronger stimuli also activate more neurons.
💡 Light-switch analogy: A light switch is all-or-none (on or off, no in-between). To get "more light," you switch on more lights (frequency/recruitment) — you don't make one bulb brighter.
⚠️ Common confusion: "Stronger" stimulus does NOT produce a "bigger" AP. It produces MORE APs (and recruits MORE neurons).

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What is threshold in an action potential?
    Show answer
    The membrane voltage (about −55 mV) that must be reached to trigger an AP.
  2. State the all-or-none principle.
    Show answer
    An AP either occurs completely or not at all. There are no partial APs.
  3. How does the CNS know whether a stimulus is strong or weak?
    Show answer
    By the FREQUENCY of incoming APs (and by how many neurons are activated).
  4. What happens to subthreshold stimuli?
    Show answer
    No AP is generated — the signal dies out.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. A stronger stimulus produces:
    A) A bigger AP · B) A longer AP · C) More APs per second · D) A slower AP
    Show answer
    C. More APs per second — frequency coding.
  2. A subthreshold stimulus:
    A) Produces a partial AP · B) Produces no AP · C) Produces a small AP · D) Reverses the membrane
    Show answer
    B. No AP — all-or-none.
  3. Threshold is reached when membrane depolarizes by approximately:
    A) 5 mV · B) 15–20 mV · C) 70 mV · D) 100 mV
    Show answer
    B. 15–20 mV (to about −55 mV).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient steps on a tack. Why do they feel pain more intensely than light pressure, even though every individual AP is the same size in either case?
Show answer
A tack triggers many more APs per second in many more pain neurons. The brain interprets high frequency + many neurons firing simultaneously as more intense pain. Each AP is identical — it's frequency and recruitment that code intensity.
☑ Self-Assessment Checklist
  • I can define threshold and approximate the voltage.
  • I can explain the all-or-none principle.
  • I can describe how the CNS codes stimulus intensity.

TOPIC 9 🚀 Saltatory Conduction & Nerve Fiber Types MUST KNOW▼

What Determines AP Speed

  • Axon diameter — larger diameter = faster conduction (like a wider pipe).
  • Myelination — myelin dramatically speeds up conduction (~30× faster).

Saltatory Conduction (visualized)

How Saltatory Conduction Works

Saltatory comes from Latin "to leap." The AP only happens at the nodes of Ranvier because voltage-gated Na+ channels are concentrated there. The signal seems to "jump" from node to node, skipping the myelinated regions.

  • Myelinated axons: saltatory conduction, very fast.
  • Unmyelinated axons: continuous conduction, slow.

Nerve Fiber Classification by Speed

GroupDiameter / MyelinSpeedRole
Group ALarge & thickly myelinated~150 m/s (~300 mph)Somatic sensory & motor (skeletal muscle, touch)
Group BIntermediate diameter, lightly myelinated~15 m/s (~40 mph)ANS motor fibers
Group CSmall, unmyelinated~1 m/s (~2 mph)ANS motor fibers (slow pain, temperature)
💡 Memory hook: Bigger and more myelin = faster. A is the freeway. C is the dirt road.
⚠️ Common confusion: The AP itself isn't moving "faster" through myelin — the AP simply isn't generated under the myelin. It only fires at the nodes, so the SIGNAL effectively skips ahead.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What 2 features determine AP conduction speed?
    Show answer
    Axon diameter (bigger = faster) and degree of myelination (myelinated = faster).
  2. Define saltatory conduction.
    Show answer
    AP propagation that jumps from node to node along myelinated axons, much faster than continuous conduction.
  3. Where are voltage-gated Na+ channels concentrated?
    Show answer
    At the nodes of Ranvier.
  4. Compare A, B, and C fiber speeds and what each carries.
    Show answer
    A: ~150 m/s, large & thick myelin, somatic sensory/motor. B: ~15 m/s, intermediate, ANS motor. C: ~1 m/s, small & unmyelinated, ANS motor / slow pain.
  5. How much faster is saltatory vs continuous conduction?
    Show answer
    About 30× faster.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The fastest nerve fibers are:
    A) Group A · B) Group B · C) Group C · D) Equal across groups
    Show answer
    A. Group A — large, thickly myelinated.
  2. Saltatory conduction occurs:
    A) On all axons · B) Only on myelinated axons · C) Only on unmyelinated axons · D) Only in the brain
    Show answer
    B. Only on myelinated axons.
  3. If an axon is demyelinated:
    A) Conduction speeds up · B) Conduction slows or fails · C) Threshold disappears · D) The cell dies immediately
    Show answer
    B. Slows or fails — the basis of MS and Guillain-Barré symptoms.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with Guillain-Barré syndrome has progressive muscle weakness. Antibodies are attacking the myelin around their PERIPHERAL nerves. Which glial cell is damaged, and how does this connect to the weakness?
Show answer
Schwann cells are damaged (PNS myelin). Without myelin, motor neuron APs can't conduct saltatory — they slow dramatically or fail. Signals to skeletal muscle weaken or stop, causing ascending paralysis. Good news: Schwann cells can regenerate, so many patients recover with treatment.

  • I can describe saltatory conduction and why it's faster.
  • I can compare A, B, and C fiber types and their roles.
  • I can predict the impact of demyelination on conduction.

TOPIC 10 💬 The Synapse — Chemical Communication MUST KNOW▼

A synapse is where one neuron passes a signal to its target (another neuron, muscle cell, or gland). Most are chemical synapses — they use neurotransmitter to bridge the gap.

Synapse Anatomy (visualized)

The 5-Step Synapse Process

  1. AP arrives at the axon terminal of the presynaptic neuron.
  2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open. Ca²⁺ floods in.
  3. Ca²⁺ triggers synaptic vesicles to fuse with the membrane and release neurotransmitter by exocytosis.
  4. Neurotransmitter crosses the synaptic cleft and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
  5. Receptor binding changes the postsynaptic membrane's permeability → either excitatory or inhibitory effect (see Topic 11).

How Neurotransmitter Effect Ends

Once the signal is delivered, NT must be removed to stop the signal. 3 ways:

  1. Diffusion — NT drifts out of the cleft.
  2. Reuptake — presynaptic terminal reabsorbs the NT (target of many antidepressants).
  3. Enzymatic degradation — an enzyme breaks the NT down. Example: acetylcholinesterase (AChE) degrades ACh.
💡 Synapse trick: Chemical synapses are UNIDIRECTIONAL — vesicles are only on the presynaptic side, receptors only on the postsynaptic side. Signal can only go one way.
⚠️ Common confusion: The synaptic cleft is NOT a void — it's full of interstitial fluid. Neurotransmitter diffuses across it; it's not "shot" across.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What ion triggers neurotransmitter release at the axon terminal?
    Show answer
    Calcium (Ca²⁺).
  2. Describe the 5 steps at a chemical synapse.
    Show answer
    AP arrives → Ca²⁺ enters terminal → vesicles release NT by exocytosis → NT crosses cleft & binds receptors → postsynaptic membrane changes (EPSP or IPSP).
  3. List the 3 ways neurotransmitter is removed from the cleft.
    Show answer
    Diffusion away, reuptake by presynaptic terminal, enzymatic degradation (e.g., AChE breaks down ACh).
  4. Why is the chemical synapse unidirectional?
    Show answer
    Vesicles & NT release happen only on the presynaptic side; receptors are only on the postsynaptic side.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Synaptic vesicles release their contents by:
    A) Diffusion · B) Active transport · C) Exocytosis · D) Phagocytosis
    Show answer
    C. Exocytosis.
  2. Acetylcholine is degraded in the synaptic cleft by:
    A) Reuptake transporters · B) Acetylcholinesterase · C) MAO · D) Diffusion only
    Show answer
    B. Acetylcholinesterase (AChE).
  3. Drugs that block reuptake of serotonin (SSRIs) work by:
    A) Increasing serotonin levels in synapses · B) Decreasing serotonin levels · C) Destroying receptors · D) Speeding up degradation
    Show answer
    A. Blocking reuptake leaves more serotonin in the synapse, enhancing its effect.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
Organophosphate pesticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase. Predict the effect on neuromuscular junctions and explain the symptoms (muscle twitching, drooling, sweating).
Show answer
Without AChE, ACh accumulates in the cleft and keeps stimulating receptors. Skeletal muscle keeps firing (twitching, weakness). Parasympathetic (ANS) targets also use ACh, so drooling, sweating, GI cramps, miosis (small pupils) all occur. Treatment includes atropine to block ACh receptors and pralidoxime to reactivate AChE.

  • I can list the 5 steps at a chemical synapse.
  • I can name the ion that triggers NT release.
  • I can describe the 3 ways NT is removed from the cleft.
  • I can explain why chemical synapses are unidirectional.

TOPIC 11 ➕➖ EPSPs, IPSPs & Summation MUST KNOW▼

When neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron, it produces one of 2 effects, depending on which receptor and which NT:

EPSP vs. IPSP

TypeEffect on MembraneResult
EPSP — ExcitatoryDepolarizes (makes less negative)PROMOTES an action potential
IPSP — InhibitoryHyperpolarizes (makes MORE negative)PREVENTS an action potential

Note: A single EPSP is usually NOT enough to fire an AP. Multiple EPSPs must add together — this is called summation.

The 2 Types of Summation

TypeHow it Adds Up
Temporal summationOne presynaptic neuron fires rapidly — signals stack in TIME before the first fades.
Spatial summationMany presynaptic neurons fire at the same time onto one postsynaptic neuron — signals stack in SPACE.
💡 Summation memory aids:
  • Temporal = Time: same neuron firing fast.
  • Spatial = Space: many neurons firing together.
  • EPSPs add up to fire APs; IPSPs cancel them out. The neuron is a constant tug-of-war.
⚠️ Common confusion: EPSPs and IPSPs are graded potentials — not all-or-none. They CAN have different strengths, and they fade with distance. The all-or-none rule applies to the AP that they trigger, not to themselves.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Compare EPSPs and IPSPs.
    Show answer
    EPSP = depolarizes the postsynaptic membrane, promotes AP. IPSP = hyperpolarizes, prevents AP.
  2. Why must EPSPs summate?
    Show answer
    A single EPSP isn't strong enough to reach threshold. Multiple EPSPs must add together (temporally or spatially) to depolarize the membrane to threshold.
  3. Compare temporal and spatial summation.
    Show answer
    Temporal: one neuron fires rapidly so EPSPs stack in time. Spatial: many neurons fire at once onto one postsynaptic neuron.
  4. Are EPSPs and IPSPs all-or-none?
    Show answer
    No — they are graded potentials of varying strength.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. An IPSP makes the postsynaptic neuron:
    A) More likely to fire · B) Less likely to fire · C) Depolarized · D) Excited
    Show answer
    B. Less likely — hyperpolarization moves voltage away from threshold.
  2. A single neuron firing in rapid succession produces:
    A) Temporal summation · B) Spatial summation · C) Saltatory conduction · D) An IPSP
    Show answer
    A. Temporal summation.
  3. EPSPs are best described as:
    A) All-or-none · B) Graded potentials that promote APs · C) Inhibitory only · D) Independent of neurotransmitter type
    Show answer
    B. Graded depolarizations that promote APs when they summate to threshold.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
Benzodiazepines (like diazepam) increase the effect of GABA — the brain's main inhibitory neurotransmitter. Predict how this affects EPSP/IPSP balance and why benzos cause sedation and reduce seizures.
Show answer
More GABA effect = more IPSPs → neurons hyperpolarize more → APs are less likely to fire. Result: less neural activity overall → sedation, muscle relaxation, anxiety reduction, and stopping seizure activity (which is uncontrolled neural firing).
☑ Self-Assessment Checklist
  • I can compare EPSP and IPSP effects on a postsynaptic neuron.
  • I can define temporal and spatial summation.
  • I can explain why a single EPSP usually doesn't fire an AP.
  • I can distinguish graded potentials from action potentials.

TOPIC 12 🧪 Neurotransmitters MUST KNOW▼

Neurotransmitters (NTs) are chemical messengers used at chemical synapses. Over 50 different NTs have been identified, classified both chemically and functionally.

Chemical Classes

ClassExamples
Acetylcholine (ACh)The classic NT — only one in its own class
Biogenic aminesCatecholamines (dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine) and indolamines (serotonin, histamine)
Amino acidsGABA (inhibitory), glutamate (excitatory), glycine
PeptidesEndorphins, substance P
Dissolved gasesNitric oxide (NO), carbon monoxide (CO)

Acetylcholine — The First & Best-Known NT

  • Released at the neuromuscular junction (stimulates skeletal muscle).
  • Also released by some neurons in the autonomic NS.
  • Synthesized and stored in synaptic vesicles.
  • Broken down in the synaptic cleft by acetylcholinesterase (AChE).

Biogenic Amines

  • Widely distributed in the brain.
  • Major roles in emotion, mood, biological clock.
  • Examples: dopamine (reward, movement), norepinephrine (alertness, stress), serotonin (mood, sleep), histamine (alertness, allergy).

Functional Classification

TypeEffect
ExcitatoryCause depolarization (EPSP). Example: glutamate.
InhibitoryCause hyperpolarization (IPSP). Example: GABA.

Important twist: Some NTs can be EITHER excitatory or inhibitory depending on the receptor type on the postsynaptic cell. Example: ACh excites skeletal muscle at NMJs but INHIBITS cardiac muscle (slowing the heart) via different receptors.

💡 Mood-related NT cheat sheet:
  • Dopamine — reward, motivation, movement. Low in Parkinson's, dysregulated in addiction.
  • Serotonin — mood, sleep, appetite. Targeted by SSRIs for depression.
  • Norepinephrine — alertness, stress response.
  • GABA — main inhibitory NT. Targeted by benzos and alcohol.
  • Glutamate — main excitatory NT. Involved in learning.
⚠️ Common confusion: Whether a neurotransmitter is excitatory or inhibitory depends on the receptor, not the NT itself. ACh excites skeletal muscle but inhibits cardiac muscle.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Name the chemical classes of neurotransmitters.
    Show answer
    Acetylcholine, biogenic amines, amino acids, peptides, dissolved gases.
  2. Where is ACh released? What breaks it down?
    Show answer
    Released at the neuromuscular junction and in parts of the ANS. Broken down by acetylcholinesterase (AChE).
  3. What's the difference between an excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitter?
    Show answer
    Excitatory causes depolarization (EPSP); inhibitory causes hyperpolarization (IPSP).
  4. Why does ACh excite skeletal muscle but inhibit cardiac muscle?
    Show answer
    Because the postsynaptic receptors are different. The NT effect is determined by the receptor type.
  5. Name an example of a biogenic amine and what it does.
    Show answer
    Dopamine (reward/movement), serotonin (mood/sleep), norepinephrine (alertness/stress), epinephrine (fight or flight), histamine (alertness/allergy).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The main excitatory neurotransmitter in the CNS is:
    A) GABA · B) Glutamate · C) Dopamine · D) Acetylcholine
    Show answer
    B. Glutamate.
  2. Dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin are:
    A) Amino acids · B) Peptides · C) Biogenic amines · D) Gases
    Show answer
    C. Biogenic amines.
  3. The main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain is:
    A) Glutamate · B) GABA · C) ACh · D) Glycine in spinal cord
    Show answer
    B. GABA (glycine plays this role mainly in the spinal cord).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with Parkinson's disease shows resting tremor, rigidity, and slowed movement. Which neurotransmitter is deficient, and which part of the brain is affected?
Show answer
Dopamine is deficient due to loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra (a midbrain region). Dopamine is needed for smooth, controlled movement. Treatment usually involves L-DOPA (a dopamine precursor) which can cross the blood-brain barrier and be converted to dopamine in the brain.

📚 Quick Glossary

Overview & Organization
  • CNS / PNS — brain + cord / nerves outside CNS.
  • Afferent — sensory, arrives at CNS. Efferent — motor, exits CNS.
  • Somatic NS — voluntary skeletal muscle.
  • Autonomic NS — involuntary; sympathetic (fight/flight) + parasympathetic (rest/digest).
Cells & Anatomy
  • Neuron — structural/functional unit; amitotic.
  • Neuroglia — support cells. CNS: astrocytes, microglia, ependymal, oligodendrocytes. PNS: Schwann, satellite.
  • Soma — cell body.
  • Dendrite — receives signals.
  • Axon — sends signals; one per neuron.
  • Axon hillock — where APs start.
  • Myelin sheath — insulating wrap; speeds AP conduction.
  • Node of Ranvier — gap in myelin where Na+ channels concentrate.
  • Nucleus (CNS) / Ganglion (PNS) — cluster of cell bodies.
  • Tract (CNS) / Nerve (PNS) — bundle of axons.
  • White matter — myelinated axons. Gray matter — cell bodies.
Neuron Classification
  • Unipolar / Bipolar / Multipolar — 1 / 2 / many processes.
  • Sensory / Motor / Interneuron — toward CNS / away from CNS / within CNS.
Action Potential
  • Resting potential — ~−70 mV.
  • Threshold — ~−55 mV.
  • Depolarization — Na+ in.
  • Repolarization — K+ out.
  • Hyperpolarization — brief dip below resting.
  • All-or-none — AP fires fully or not at all.
  • Saltatory conduction — AP jumps node to node in myelinated axons.
  • Fiber types A/B/C — fastest / intermediate / slowest.
Synapse & Signaling
  • Synapse — junction where signal passes.
  • Synaptic cleft — fluid-filled space between pre- and postsynaptic membranes.
  • Neurotransmitter (NT) — chemical messenger.
  • Ca²⁺ — triggers NT release at axon terminal.
  • EPSP / IPSP — excitatory / inhibitory postsynaptic potentials.
  • Temporal summation — one neuron firing rapidly.
  • Spatial summation — many neurons firing at once.
  • AChE — acetylcholinesterase; breaks down ACh.
Neurotransmitters
  • ACh — NMJ; excites skeletal muscle, inhibits heart.
  • Glutamate — main excitatory NT in CNS.
  • GABA — main inhibitory NT in CNS.
  • Dopamine — reward, movement; low in Parkinson's.
  • Serotonin — mood, sleep; SSRI target.
  • Norepinephrine / Epinephrine — alertness, fight-or-flight.
  • Histamine — alertness, allergy.
  • Biogenic amines — catecholamines + indolamines.

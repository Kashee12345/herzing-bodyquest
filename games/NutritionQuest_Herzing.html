🔍 Nutritional Deficiency Quick Reference ▼
Clinical Pearl: Serum ferritin is the most sensitive early marker for iron deficiency — it falls before hemoglobin changes. Pre-albumin (half-life 2 days) is the best marker for acute nutritional intervention monitoring.

🔢 Key Formulas and Calculations ▼
BMI
BMI = Weight (kg) ÷ Height (m)²
Underweight <18.5 · Normal 18.5–24.9 · Overweight 25–29.9 · Obese ≥30. Does NOT measure body composition — waist circumference adds cardiovascular risk data.
TPN Caloric Content
Total kcal = (Dextrose g × 3.4) + (Amino acids g × 4) + (Lipids g × 9)
⚠️ IV dextrose yields 3.4 kcal/g (monohydrate form) — NOT 4 kcal/g like oral carbohydrates. This is a high-yield test distinction.
Nitrogen Balance
N balance = (Protein intake g ÷ 6.25) − (UUN g/24hr + 4)
Positive = anabolism (building). Negative = catabolism (breaking down). +4 accounts for non-urea nitrogen losses (skin, feces, respiration). Goal: positive balance in wound healing.
Protein Goal Calculation
Protein (g/day) = Weight (kg) × g/kg factor
Healthy adults: 0.8 g/kg · Post-surgery/illness: 1.2–1.5 g/kg · Wound healing/burns: 1.5–2.0 g/kg · Severe burns/critical: 2.0–2.5 g/kg
Daily Carbohydrate Goal
Carbs (g) = (Total kcal × carb%) ÷ 4
Recommended range: 45–65% of total calories. For 2,000 kcal at 50%: (2,000 × 0.5) ÷ 4 = 250g/day.
Sodium: mg to mEq Conversion
1 g sodium = 43 mEq · 2,000 mg/day = 87 mEq/day
Also useful: 1 teaspoon table salt ≈ 2,300 mg sodium. Hidden sodium sources: canned foods, deli meats, condiments, bread.
%DV Interpretation
≤5% DV = LOW · ≥20% DV = HIGH
Based on a 2,000 kcal reference diet. Use for patient label-reading education: "5 is low, 20 is high" is the rule to memorize.
🧪 Key Lab Values in Clinical Nutrition ▼
Lab ValueNormal RangeHalf-lifeClinical Significance
Albumin3.5–5.0 g/dL~21 daysChronic nutritional status. Low <3.5 → increased infection, poor wound healing, altered drug binding. Also low in inflammation/liver disease (not just malnutrition).
Pre-albumin (Transthyretin)16–35 mg/dL~2 daysBEST marker for acute nutritional intervention monitoring. Responds within days. Also low in inflammation — pair with CRP to interpret.
Potassium (K⁺)3.5–5.0 mEq/L—Critical for cardiac function. <3.5 = hypokalemia (U waves, weakness, digoxin toxicity risk). >5.0 = hyperkalemia (peaked T waves, arrhythmia — dangerous in CKD).
Sodium (Na⁺)135–145 mEq/L—Hyponatremia common in CHF, SIADH. Hypernatremia → dehydration. Drives fluid balance via osmosis.
Magnesium1.7–2.2 mg/dL—Most missed electrolyte. Low → refractory hypocalcemia, refractory hypokalemia, arrhythmia. Check when K and Ca won't correct.
Calcium (total)8.5–10.5 mg/dL—Correct for albumin: add 0.8 mg/dL per 1 g/dL albumin below 4.0. Low → tetany, Chvostek's, Trousseau's, QT prolongation.
Phosphorus2.5–4.5 mg/dL—High in CKD (restrict diet + phosphate binders). Low in refeeding syndrome (glucose shifts P intracellularly).
HemoglobinM: 13.5–17.5 g/dL · F: 12–15.5 g/dL~120 days (RBC)Late marker for iron deficiency — ferritin falls first. Low in iron, B12, and folate deficiency.
Serum FerritinM: 20–500 ng/mL · F: 12–150 ng/mL—EARLIEST and most sensitive marker for iron deficiency. Drops before hemoglobin falls. Can be falsely elevated in inflammation (acute phase reactant).
HbA1cNormal <5.7% · Pre-DM 5.7–6.4% · DM ≥6.5%~3 months (RBC)Reflects average blood glucose over 2–3 months. Target for most T2DM: <7%. Not useful for monitoring short-term dietary changes.
BUN (Blood Urea Nitrogen)7–20 mg/dL—Elevated in high protein intake, dehydration, or GI bleeding. Used in nitrogen balance calculations.
25-OH Vitamin D30–100 ng/mL (optimal)—Deficient <20 ng/mL · Insufficient 20–29 ng/mL. This is the correct test (not 1,25-OH Vit D which is active form and often normal even in deficiency).

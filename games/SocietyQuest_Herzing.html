Émile Durkheim
1858–1917 · French Sociologist
Social Facts · Anomie · Collective Conscience · Suicide (1897)
Father of sociology as a scientific discipline. Argued social facts exist outside individuals and constrain behavior. Studied suicide rates to prove sociology can explain individual acts through social forces. Showed suicide varied by religion and social integration — not just personal psychology.
"Man cannot become attached to higher aims and submit to a rule if he sees nothing above him to which he belongs."
Karl Marx
1818–1883 · German Philosopher/Economist
Conflict Theory · Class Struggle · Base/Superstructure · False Consciousness
Argued society is shaped by economic conflict between the bourgeoisie (owners) and proletariat (workers). Social institutions — law, religion, education, healthcare — serve ruling class interests. The superstructure (ideas, culture) reflects and justifies the economic base.
"The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles."
Max Weber
1864–1920 · German Sociologist
Rationalization · Bureaucracy · Class + Status + Power · Protestant Ethic
Expanded Marx: three dimensions of stratification — class (economic), status (prestige), power (political). Theorized bureaucracy as the dominant form of modern organization. Argued capitalism arose partly from Protestant values of work as a calling.
"The fate of our times is characterized by rationalization and, above all, by the disenchantment of the world."
George Herbert Mead
1863–1931 · American Social Psychologist
Symbolic Interactionism · The Self (I and Me) · Generalized Other
The self develops through social interaction. We develop identity by imagining how others see us (the generalized other) — society as a whole becomes internalized as the "Me." Language and symbols are the medium through which we construct shared reality.
Charles Cooley
1864–1929 · American Sociologist
Looking-Glass Self · Primary Groups
We develop self-concept by: (1) imagining how we appear to others, (2) imagining their judgment, (3) feeling pride or shame based on that perceived judgment. The mirror is other people. Clinical implication: how a nurse sees and treats a patient shapes how that patient sees themselves.
"I am not what I think I am and I am not what you think I am; I am what I think you think I am."
Erving Goffman
1922–1982 · Canadian Sociologist
Dramaturgy · Stigma · Total Institutions · Impression Management
Social life is a theatrical performance. Front stage: public performance for an audience. Backstage: where we drop the performance. Stigma: a deeply discrediting attribute that reduces someone from a whole person to a tainted, discounted one. Hospitals are total institutions that strip personal identity.
"The stigmatized individual tends to hold the same beliefs about identity that we do; this is a pernicious aspect of their situation."
Talcott Parsons
1902–1979 · American Sociologist
Structural Functionalism · Sick Role · AGIL Schema
Major functionalist. The sick role: when ill, a person is (1) exempt from normal duties, (2) not blamed for being sick, but must (3) try to get well and (4) seek competent medical help. Illness is a form of sanctioned deviance — medicine manages it to restore social functioning.
Pierre Bourdieu
1930–2002 · French Sociologist
Cultural Capital · Social Reproduction · Field and Habitus
Inequality is reproduced through cultural knowledge — knowing how to speak, dress, navigate institutions. Schools reward those who already possess this capital. Three types: embodied (dispositions), objectified (cultural goods), institutionalized (credentials like degrees).
"Cultural capital is the best hidden form of hereditary transmission of capital."
Robert Merton
1910–2003 · American Sociologist
Strain Theory · Manifest/Latent Functions · Reference Groups
Refined functionalism: manifest functions (intended outcomes) vs latent functions (unintended). Strain theory: when society promotes culturally valued goals but blocks legitimate means, individuals adapt — through conformity, innovation (crime), ritualism, retreatism, or rebellion.
C. Wright Mills
1916–1962 · American Sociologist
Sociological Imagination · Power Elite · White Collar
The sociological imagination links personal troubles to public issues — connecting biography to history. The power elite: a small network of military, corporate, and political leaders who make decisions affecting everyone. His work on white-collar work showed how occupational life shapes identity and values.
"The sociological imagination enables its possessor to understand the larger historical scene in terms of its meaning for the inner life."
Structural Functionalism
Core question: How does this contribute to social stability?
Society is a system of interconnected parts, each serving a function to maintain equilibrium. Dysfunction — when a part fails its function — destabilizes the whole. Major thinkers: Durkheim, Parsons, Merton.
Nursing application: The sick role keeps patients cooperative and society stable. The hospital as an institution functions to return the ill to productive social roles.
Conflict Theory
Core question: Who benefits and who is harmed by this arrangement?
Society is structured by competition over scarce resources. Power and privilege shape all social institutions. Inequality is not accidental — it is actively maintained by those who benefit from it. Major thinkers: Marx, Engels, C. Wright Mills, modern critical theorists.
Nursing application: Black patients systematically undertreated for pain. Poor neighborhoods with fewer hospitals and pharmacies. Pharmaceutical companies pricing insulin at $300+ when it costs $6 to produce. All are conflict theory in action.
Symbolic Interactionism
Core question: What do people mean by this, and how do they construct that meaning?
Society is constructed through everyday interactions and shared symbols. People act based on the meaning things have for them — meanings that arise through social interaction and are subject to change. Major thinkers: Mead, Cooley, Blumer, Goffman.
Nursing application: What does "pain" mean to a patient raised to never show weakness? How does hearing the word "cancer" change how a patient sees themselves? Why does a patient ignore a doctor but trust their grandmother? These are symbolic interactionist questions.
Feminist Theory
Core question: How does gender — alone and intersecting with race, class, sexuality — shape this experience?
Examines how gender inequality is constructed and maintained across all social institutions. Goes beyond individual discrimination to structural analysis. Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989) shows how multiple systems of oppression combine and interact rather than simply adding together.
Nursing application: Women having heart attack symptoms dismissed as anxiety. Female nurses expected to perform emotional labor without compensation. The gender wage gap between male physicians and female nurses reflects structural, not individual, patterns.
Labeling Theory
Core question: How does the label shape what a person becomes?
Deviance is not a quality of the act but a consequence of applying the label. Once labeled (criminal, addict, mentally ill, drug-seeker), people are treated accordingly and often internalize the label. Primary deviance = the act. Secondary deviance = deviance that results from being labeled. Thinkers: Becker, Lemert, Goffman.
Nursing application: "Drug-seeking" entered in a patient chart changes how every subsequent healthcare provider treats that patient — for years. Psychiatric diagnoses follow patients across their entire medical history. Labels have clinical consequences.
Anomie
Durkheim: a state of normlessness when social norms break down and individuals lose shared purpose and regulation. Associated with rapid social change, economic collapse, divorce, retirement, or social isolation. Strong predictor of suicide and mental illness.
Social Capital
The networks, relationships, and social connections a person or community has access to. Bonding capital (within groups) vs bridging capital (between groups). Communities with high social capital have better health outcomes, lower crime, and stronger recovery from disasters.
Cultural Capital
Bourdieu: non-economic resources that confer social advantage — knowledge, skills, behaviors, credentials, ways of speaking. Knowing how to navigate hospitals, write professional emails, or advocate for oneself in medical settings is cultural capital with direct health implications.
Medicalization
The process by which non-medical problems come to be defined and treated as medical issues. Childbirth, grief, hyperactivity, shyness, addiction, aging — all have been medicalized over the past century. Conrad and Schneider: medicalization shifts moral judgments into medical frameworks.
Sick Role
Parsons: when ill, a person is exempt from normal duties and not blamed for illness, but must (1) try to get well and (2) seek competent medical help. Illness is a sanctioned form of deviance — medicine manages it to restore social functioning. Criticized for ignoring chronic illness and patient agency.
Social Determinants of Health
The conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age that shape health outcomes — income, education, neighborhood, race, housing, food access, environmental toxins. A person's zip code predicts their life expectancy more reliably than their genetic code.
Hidden Curriculum
The unofficial lessons schools teach alongside academic content: compliance, punctuality, deference to authority, appropriate emotional expression. Schools socialize children into the behaviors expected of workers in a stratified economy — often without anyone explicitly teaching these things.
Collective Efficacy
A neighborhood shared belief in its capacity for collective action and willingness to intervene for the common good. Sampson (1997): collective efficacy predicts lower crime rates more strongly than poverty alone. Communities with high collective efficacy have better health outcomes.
Intersectionality
Coined by Kimberlé Crenshaw (1989). The overlapping and interdependent systems of discrimination — race, gender, class, sexuality, disability — that interact to shape individual experience in ways that cannot be understood by examining each dimension separately.
Strain Theory
Merton: when society promotes culturally valued goals (wealth, success) but blocks legitimate means for certain groups, people adapt through: conformity, innovation (crime to reach the goal), ritualism (following rules without believing in them), retreatism (giving up), or rebellion.
Social Stratification
The hierarchical ranking of individuals and groups based on wealth, power, and prestige. All known societies have some form of stratification. Forms include caste (rigid, ascribed, like India), class (semi-permeable, achieved), estate (feudal Europe), and slavery.
Socialization
The lifelong process through which individuals learn the norms, values, behaviors, and social skills that allow them to participate in society. Primary socialization (family, early childhood) vs secondary socialization (school, peer groups, workplace). Resocialization occurs in total institutions.
Total Institution
Goffman: a closed social system that controls all aspects of residents lives — hospitals, prisons, military, monasteries, residential schools. Strip personal identity (uniforms, new names, loss of control), impose a collective identity, regulate all time and space.
Gentrification
The process by which wealthier residents move into lower-income neighborhoods, driving up property values, rents, and living costs — displacing long-term residents. Destroys social capital, breaks community ties, and eliminates the affordable housing stock that low-income families depend on.
Acculturation
Cultural change that occurs when individuals or groups from one culture come into sustained contact with another. Distinct from assimilation: acculturation is bidirectional and does not require abandoning original culture. Immigrant health paradox: recent immigrants often healthier than US-born counterparts until they acculturate.
Sociological Imagination
C. Wright Mills: the ability to see the connection between personal biography and larger historical and social forces. A personal trouble (one person unemployed) becomes a public issue (mass unemployment during a recession) when it affects large numbers and has structural causes.

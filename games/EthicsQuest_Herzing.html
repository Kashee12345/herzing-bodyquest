Reference
Philosopher's Guide
Socrates
470–399 BCE · Athens
The Examined Life · Socratic Method · Moral Intellectualism
Believed that virtue is knowledge — no one does wrong willingly, only out of ignorance. The Socratic method (elenchus) uses questioning to expose contradictions in beliefs and move toward truth. His claim that "the unexamined life is not worth living" established philosophical inquiry as a moral obligation.
Aristotle
384–322 BCE · Stagira / Athens
Virtue Ethics · Eudaimonia · The Golden Mean · Nicomachean Ethics
The goal of human life is eudaimonia (flourishing) achieved through virtuous activity. Virtues are the mean between two vices — excess and deficiency. Practical wisdom (phronesis) is the master virtue enabling correct moral judgment in context. Character is formed through habituation: we become courageous by practicing courageous acts.
"We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence is not an act but a habit."
Immanuel Kant
1724–1804 · Königsberg, Prussia
Deontological Ethics · Categorical Imperative · Duty · Autonomy
The only unconditionally good thing is a good will — acting from duty, not inclination. The Categorical Imperative: (1) act only on universalizable maxims, (2) treat persons always as ends never merely as means, (3) act as legislating for a kingdom of ends. Lying is always wrong because it cannot be universalized and treats persons as means.
"Act only according to that maxim by which you can at the same time will it should become a universal law."
Jeremy Bentham
1748–1832 · London
Utilitarianism · Hedonic Calculus · Greatest Happiness Principle
The right action produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. Developed the hedonic calculus — quantifying pleasures and pains by intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent. All pleasures are equal in kind; only quantity matters.
John Stuart Mill
1806–1873 · London
Utilitarianism · Higher and Lower Pleasures · Harm Principle · Liberty
Refined Bentham's utilitarianism by distinguishing higher (intellectual) from lower (physical) pleasures — qualitatively superior regardless of quantity. "Better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." Harm principle: the only legitimate limit on individual liberty is preventing harm to others.
Thomas Hobbes
1588–1679 · Malmesbury, England
Social Contract · State of Nature · Sovereign Authority · Leviathan
Without government, life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." Rational self-interest produces a war of all against all in the state of nature. We contract to surrender freedoms to a sovereign in exchange for security. The sovereign's near-absolute authority is justified by its function.
John Locke
1632–1704 · Wrington, England
Natural Rights · Limited Government · Social Contract · Two Treatises
Humans possess natural rights to life, liberty, and property that preexist government. The social contract forms government to protect these rights more effectively. A government that violates natural rights loses its legitimacy and may be resisted. Foundation of liberal democratic theory and the American Declaration of Independence.
Jean-Jacques Rousseau
1712–1778 · Geneva
Social Contract · General Will · Natural Goodness · Corruption by Society
Humans are naturally good but corrupted by society and private property. The social contract should replace natural freedom with civil freedom — freedom under laws we collectively will. The general will (volonté générale) represents what is genuinely good for the whole community, distinct from mere aggregate private interests.
John Rawls
1921–2002 · Cambridge, USA
Justice as Fairness · Veil of Ignorance · Two Principles · A Theory of Justice
Principles of justice should be chosen from behind a "veil of ignorance" — not knowing one's own position in society. Produces two principles: (1) equal basic liberties for all, (2) inequalities are just only if they benefit the least-advantaged (difference principle). Rules out pure meritocracy and pure egalitarianism.
"Justice is the first virtue of social institutions, as truth is of systems of thought."
Carol Gilligan
b. 1936 · New York
Care Ethics · In a Different Voice · Critique of Kohlberg
Challenged Kohlberg's model as male-biased. Women tend to reason morally through relationships, care, and context rather than abstract rules and justice. Care ethics centers relationships, interdependence, and responsiveness to particular others — not a lower form of reasoning but a different voice.
Nel Noddings
b. 1929 · New Jersey
Ethics of Care · Natural Caring · Ethical Caring · One-Caring / Cared-For
Natural caring (spontaneous response to those near us) is the foundation of ethics. Ethical caring extends this to others through moral effort. The caring relation — between one-caring and cared-for — is the basic moral unit. Good care must be received: if it does not reach the patient as a person, it fails.
Beauchamp & Childress
Principles of Biomedical Ethics, 1979
Four Principles · Principlism · Biomedical Ethics
The four-principles framework: Autonomy (respect persons' right to make decisions), Beneficence (do good), Non-maleficence (avoid harm), Justice (distribute benefits and burdens fairly). Not derived from any single ethical theory — draws on moral intuitions that different theories converge on. Dominant framework in clinical ethics worldwide.
Consequentialism / Utilitarianism
Central question: What produces the best outcomes?
The right action produces the greatest good for the greatest number. Consequences — not intentions or rules — determine moral rightness. Act utilitarianism evaluates each action individually. Rule utilitarianism follows rules that generally produce the best outcomes. Strengths: practical, aggregative, egalitarian. Weaknesses: can justify harming individuals for collective benefit; ignores rights.
Deontological Ethics (Kantian)
Central question: What is my duty?
Moral rightness is determined by adherence to duties and rules, independent of consequences. The Categorical Imperative: (1) universalizability, (2) humanity as end, (3) kingdom of ends. Perfect duties (never lie, never murder) admit no exceptions. Imperfect duties (help others, develop talents) allow discretion in how they are fulfilled.
Virtue Ethics
Central question: What kind of person should I be?
Focuses on character rather than rules or consequences. Virtues (courage, justice, honesty) are developed through habituation and constitute human excellence. Eudaimonia (flourishing) is achieved by living virtuously over a complete life. The golden mean: virtues are the midpoint between vices of excess and deficiency. Practical wisdom (phronesis) applies virtues appropriately in context.
Care Ethics
Central question: How do I respond to the needs of those in my care?
Moral reasoning centers on relationships, interdependence, and responsiveness to particular others. The caring relation (one-caring and cared-for) is morally foundational. Care ethics critiques impartiality — the demand to treat all persons equally — as missing the moral significance of particular relationships. Applied extensively in nursing ethics.
Social Contract Theory
Central question: What principles would rational persons agree to?
Moral and political obligations arise from agreements among rational persons. Hobbes: contract for security under near-absolute sovereign. Locke: contract to protect pre-existing natural rights; limited government; right of revolution. Rousseau: the general will represents the genuine common good. Rawls: justice principles chosen behind the veil of ignorance.
The Four Principles of Bioethics
Central question: Which principle applies, and how do they conflict?
Autonomy: respect persons' right to make informed decisions. Beneficence: act to benefit the patient. Non-maleficence: do no harm. Justice: distribute benefits and burdens fairly. These principles are prima facie — all carry weight and may conflict. Balancing them is the central task of clinical ethics. Beauchamp and Childress (1979).
Categorical Imperative
Kant's supreme principle of morality: an unconditional command applying to all rational beings. Three formulations: universalizability, humanity as end, kingdom of ends.
Eudaimonia
Aristotle: human flourishing — not a feeling but an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life. The ultimate goal toward which all human action aims.
Golden Mean
Aristotle: virtue is the midpoint between two vices — excess and deficiency. Courage lies between cowardice and rashness. Context determines where the mean lies.
Greatest Happiness Principle
Bentham and Mill: the morally right action produces the greatest amount of happiness for the greatest number. The foundation of utilitarian ethics.
Veil of Ignorance
Rawls: a thought experiment in which principles of justice are chosen without knowing one's own position in society — social class, race, sex, or natural talents. Ensures impartiality.
Autonomy
Self-governance — the capacity of a person to make rational decisions according to their own values. In bioethics: the obligation to respect patients' informed choices about their own care.
Beneficence
The duty to act for the benefit of the patient. Positive duty to promote well-being. Distinct from and sometimes in tension with autonomy.
Non-maleficence
Primum non nocere — first, do no harm. The obligation to avoid causing harm. Often in tension with beneficence when beneficial treatments carry risks.
Justice (Distributive)
The fair distribution of benefits and burdens. Formal principle: treat equals equally. Substantive criteria: to each according to need, contribution, or legal entitlement.
Prima Facie Duty
W.D. Ross: a duty that is binding unless overridden by a stronger competing duty. Multiple prima facie duties exist and may conflict. Judgment determines which takes precedence.
Moral Relativism
The view that moral judgments are relative to the individual or culture making them — there are no universal moral truths. Descriptive relativism notes that cultures differ; normative relativism claims all such views are equally valid.
Hedonic Calculus
Bentham's method for quantifying pleasures and pains using seven criteria: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity (nearness), fecundity, purity, and extent (number affected).
Phronesis
Aristotle: practical wisdom — the intellectual virtue that enables correct moral judgment in particular situations. Not a formula but a cultivated capacity developed through experience.
Euthyphro Dilemma
Is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? Either horn undermines divine command theory as the foundation of ethics.
Informed Consent
The ethical and legal requirement that patients receive adequate information about their diagnosis, proposed treatment, alternatives, and risks — and that they voluntarily consent without coercion. Grounded in autonomy.
Doctrine of Double Effect
An action with both good and harmful effects is permissible if: (1) the act is not intrinsically wrong, (2) only the good effect is intended, (3) harm is not the means to the good, and (4) proportionate reason exists.
Difference Principle
Rawls: social and economic inequalities are just only if they benefit the least-advantaged members of society. Chosen from behind the veil of ignorance because rational agents might end up as the least-advantaged.
General Will (Volonté Générale)
Rousseau: the genuine common good of the political community — not merely the aggregate of private interests but what is truly beneficial for all citizens as a whole.
Therapeutic Privilege
A physician's withholding of information from a patient on the grounds that disclosure would harm the patient. Once broadly accepted, now largely discredited — distress is not incapacity, and the physician's prediction of harm cannot override the patient's right to information needed for autonomous decision-making.
Surrogate Decision-Making
Decision-making on behalf of a patient who lacks decision-making capacity. Standard applies the substituted judgment standard (what would the patient have decided?) first; if unavailable, the best interest standard (what would a reasonable person in this situation choose?).
Paternalism
Overriding a person's autonomous choice for their own good. Soft paternalism: intervening when autonomy is impaired (accepted). Hard paternalism: overriding competent autonomous choice for the person's benefit (generally condemned in modern bioethics).
Moral Residue
W.D. Ross: when a prima facie duty is overridden by a stronger competing duty, an obligation remains — you owe something to the party whose claim you set aside. Moral residue accounts for the feeling of regret or obligation that remains after a genuinely difficult moral choice.
Medically Futile Treatment
Treatment that cannot achieve its intended physiological goal, or that offers negligible benefit relative to burden. Distinguishes physiological futility (the treatment cannot work) from normative futility (the treatment could work but is not worth doing given the patient's values and condition).
Natural Law Theory
The view that moral norms are grounded in human nature and accessible through reason — not dependent on divine command or social convention. Associated with Aquinas: actions are morally good when they accord with natural human ends (life, reproduction, knowledge, social life). Foundation of the doctrine of double effect.
Substituted Judgment
A standard for surrogate decision-making requiring the surrogate to make the decision the patient would have made — not what the surrogate thinks is best. Respects the patient's prior autonomous self even when current incapacity prevents direct expression.
Moral Distress
The experience of knowing the ethically correct action but being constrained from taking it — by institutional policy, power differentials, legal requirements, or resource limitations. A recognized occupational hazard in nursing; associated with burnout, compassion fatigue, and staff turnover. Moral distress is a signal that ethical analysis is needed, not a sign of personal weakness.

Landmark Bioethics Cases
Tuskegee Syphilis Study (1932–1972)
U.S. Public Health Service · 40 years · 399 Black men
Research Ethics · Informed Consent · Justice · Racial Exploitation
The U.S. government studied untreated syphilis in Black men in rural Alabama without informed consent, and without treating them after penicillin became standard care in 1947. Exposed in 1972. Outcome: the Belmont Report, National Research Act, and federal IRB requirements. The foundational case for why informed consent and ethics oversight are non-negotiable.
Karen Ann Quinlan (1975)
New Jersey Supreme Court · In re Quinlan
Autonomy · Surrogate Decision-Making · Withdrawing Treatment
21-year-old Quinlan suffered severe brain damage and was placed on a ventilator in a persistent vegetative state. The NJ Supreme Court (1976) ruled her right to privacy included the right to refuse treatment, exercisable by a surrogate. Established the legal right to withdraw life-sustaining treatment and the foundation of advance directive law.
The Belmont Report (1979)
National Commission · Direct response to Tuskegee
Respect for Persons · Beneficence · Justice · IRB Framework
Established three foundational principles for ethical research: Respect for Persons, Beneficence, and Justice. Created the IRB oversight framework. Beauchamp and Childress later expanded the Belmont principles into the four-principle clinical ethics framework. The direct ancestor of modern bioethics.
Henrietta Lacks and HeLa Cells (1951)
Johns Hopkins Hospital · Ongoing ethical debate
Autonomy · Tissue Ownership · Racial Equity in Research
Lacks, a Black woman who died of cervical cancer in 1951, had cells cultured without consent that became the first immortal human cell line. HeLa cells contributed to the polio vaccine, cancer research, and HIV treatment. Her family received nothing while the cells generated enormous commercial value. Foundational for questions of informed consent, tissue ownership, and racial equity in research.
"The woman is immortal. Her family struggles to afford healthcare." — Rebecca Skloot
Terri Schiavo (1990–2005)
Florida · 15-year legal and political battle
Autonomy · Surrogate Decision-Making · Persistent Vegetative State
Schiavo suffered cardiac arrest in 1990 and was left in a persistent vegetative state. A 15-year battle involving Florida courts, U.S. Congress, and the Supreme Court ended with feeding tube removal in 2005. Autopsy confirmed persistent vegetative state. Catalyzed a national surge in advance directive completion — Five Wishes documents and living wills increased dramatically after 2005.
Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California (1976)
California Supreme Court · Duty to Warn
Confidentiality · Duty to Protect · Non-Maleficence vs. Privacy
Poddar told his therapist he intended to kill Tarasoff. Campus police released him. Two months later, he murdered her. The California Supreme Court: "the protective privilege ends where the public peril begins." Created the duty-to-warn doctrine: when a patient reveals a credible, specific threat against an identifiable third party, confidentiality yields to the obligation to protect.
Cruzan v. Director, Missouri (1990)
U.S. Supreme Court · First right-to-die case
Autonomy · Constitutional Right to Refuse Treatment
The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that competent persons have a constitutionally protected right to refuse medical treatment. Accelerated the Patient Self-Determination Act (1990), requiring Medicare and Medicaid facilities to inform patients of their advance directive rights. Cruzan established that the right to refuse treatment is constitutional, not merely a matter of professional ethics.
Oregon Death with Dignity Act (1997)
First U.S. physician-assisted death law · Upheld by Supreme Court 2006
Autonomy · Non-Maleficence · Conscientious Objection
Permits terminally ill Oregon residents with less than six months to live to request a lethal medication dose (self-administered). Requires two oral requests separated by 15 days and one written request. Physicians may conscientiously object. Distinguishes physician-assisted death from euthanasia. The most direct intersection of autonomy, non-maleficence, and professional conscience in American healthcare law.

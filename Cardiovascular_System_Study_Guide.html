ⓘ What you'll learn in this unit

The cardiovascular system is the body's transport network. It has three parts that work as one team: blood (the fluid that carries oxygen, nutrients, wastes, and defenders), the heart (the double pump that keeps blood moving), and blood vessels (the closed system of pipes that delivers blood to every cell and brings it back). Master how these three connect and the whole system makes sense.

💓 The Big Picture: The Double Loop

Right heart pumps deoxygenated blood (blue) to the lungs. Left heart pumps oxygenated blood (red) to the body. Blood vessels carry it all; blood is the cargo.
TOPIC 1 🩸 Blood Composition, Functions & Plasma MUST KNOW▼

Blood is a fluid connective tissue. Like all connective tissue it has living cells suspended in a nonliving matrix — here the matrix is liquid (plasma) instead of solid. If you spin blood in a centrifuge it separates into two parts:

💧 Plasma (~55%)
The liquid matrix. 90% water, 8% plasma proteins, 2% other solutes (electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, gases, wastes).		 🩸 Formed Elements (~45%)
The cells & fragments: Erythrocytes (RBCs), Leukocytes (WBCs), and Thrombocytes (platelets). This % is the hematocrit.

Physical Characteristics

  • Sticky, opaque fluid; scarlet (oxygen-rich) to dark red (oxygen-poor).
  • pH 7.35–7.45 (slightly alkaline); about 38°C (1° warmer than body surface).
  • ~8% of body weight. Average volume: 5–6 L in males, 4–5 L in females (testosterone stimulates RBC production, so males have more).

The 3 Jobs of Blood

🚚 Transport
O&sub2; & nutrients to cells; CO&sub2; & wastes to lungs/kidneys; hormones to target organs.		 🌡️ Regulation
Maintains body temperature, normal pH (buffers), and adequate fluid volume.		 🛡️ Protection
Antibodies, complement, & WBCs fight infection; platelets & plasma proteins clot to prevent blood loss.

Blood Plasma in Detail

Plasma is 90% water. The 8% plasma proteins are made mostly by the liver and stay in the blood (they aren't taken up by cells):

  • Albumin (60%) — the main carrier protein; acts as a buffer and is the biggest contributor to blood osmotic pressure (holds water in vessels).
  • Globulins (36%) — include antibodies and transport proteins.
  • Fibrinogen (4%) — the clotting protein; converts to fibrin during coagulation.

The final 2% is everything else: wastes (urea, creatinine, lactic acid), nutrients (glucose, amino acids), electrolytes (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻), respiratory gases (O&sub2;, CO&sub2;), hormones, and enzymes.

💡 Mnemonic: Plasma proteins = "Albumin Goes First" (Albumin 60% > Globulins 36% > Fibrinogen 4%). And remember plasma proteins are made in the liver — so liver disease → low albumin → fluid leaks out (edema).
⚠️ Common confusion: Plasma still contains fibrinogen (clotting protein). Serum is plasma after clotting — same fluid but with the clotting proteins removed. Plasma = serum + clotting factors.

🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with advanced liver disease develops swelling in the legs and abdomen (edema). Their blood albumin is very low. Explain the connection.
Show answer
The liver makes albumin. With liver failure, albumin production drops. Albumin normally holds water inside vessels via osmotic pressure, so when it falls, fluid leaks out of the blood into the tissues → edema and ascites.

TOPIC 2 🚀 Erythrocytes & Hemoglobin MUST KNOW▼

Erythrocytes (red blood cells, RBCs) are the body's oxygen taxis. They are a beautiful example of "structure fits function."

Structural Features — and Why They Matter

Biconcave disc shape
Huge surface area relative to volume → fast gas exchange. Flexible (thanks to the protein spectrin) so it can squeeze through tiny capillaries.		 No nucleus, no organelles
More room for hemoglobin (>97% of cell content, minus water). No mitochondria → ATP made anaerobically, so RBCs don't "use up" the O&sub2; they carry.

RBCs are the major contributor to blood viscosity. Each RBC holds about 250 million hemoglobin molecules.

Hemoglobin (Hb) Structure

Globin = 4 protein chains. Each chain holds a heme group, and each heme has one iron (Fe) atom at its center. Each iron binds one O&sub2;, so one hemoglobin carries four O&sub2;.

Hemoglobin's Three "Looks"

  • Oxyhemoglobin — O&sub2; loaded in the lungs; bright ruby red.
  • Deoxyhemoglobin (reduced Hb) — O&sub2; unloaded in tissues; dark red.
  • Carbaminohemoglobin — CO&sub2; bound to the globin (amino-acid) portion; carries ~20% of blood CO&sub2;. (Note: CO&sub2; binds globin, not the iron.)
💡 Mnemonic: "4 chains, 4 hemes, 4 irons, 4 O&sub2;" — everything about hemoglobin comes in fours. And O&sub2; rides the iron; CO&sub2; rides the globin.
⚠️ Common confusion: Most CO&sub2; is actually carried in plasma as bicarbonate (~70%), not on hemoglobin. Only about 20% rides on Hb as carbaminohemoglobin, and ~7–10% is dissolved in plasma.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. How does the biconcave shape help RBC function?
    Show answer
    It maximizes surface area for gas exchange and lets the cell bend/flex to pass through narrow capillaries.
  2. Why do RBCs lack mitochondria, and what's the benefit?
    Show answer
    They make ATP anaerobically, so they don't consume the oxygen they're transporting. No nucleus/organelles also frees space for more hemoglobin.
  3. How many O&sub2; molecules can one hemoglobin carry, and why?
    Show answer
    Four — one per heme/iron, and there are 4 globin chains each with one heme group.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. O&sub2; binds to which part of hemoglobin?
    A) The globin amino acids · B) The iron in the heme · C) Spectrin · D) The cell membrane
    Show answer
    B. The iron atom in each heme group.
  2. The protein that gives RBCs their flexibility is:
    A) Fibrin · B) Albumin · C) Spectrin · D) Globin
    Show answer
    C. Spectrin.
  3. Oxygen-rich hemoglobin is called:
    A) Carbaminohemoglobin · B) Deoxyhemoglobin · C) Oxyhemoglobin · D) Methemoglobin
    Show answer
    C. Oxyhemoglobin (ruby red).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient has iron-deficiency anemia. Their RBC count may be near normal but they feel exhausted and short of breath. Why does low iron cause these symptoms?
Show answer
Iron sits at the center of each heme and is the actual O&sub2;-binding site. Without enough iron, hemoglobin can't carry adequate oxygen, so tissues become hypoxic → fatigue and breathlessness even if cell numbers look okay.

TOPIC 3 ♻ Erythropoiesis & RBC Life Cycle ▼

Hematopoiesis (hemopoiesis) is the formation of all blood cells. It happens in red bone marrow (axial skeleton, girdles, and proximal epiphyses of the humerus and femur). The marrow produces about 100 billion new cells every day. Erythropoiesis is specifically red blood cell production.

The Balance Principle

RBC numbers stay constant because production = destruction. This balance depends on hormonal control plus adequate iron, amino acids, and B vitamins (B₁₂ and folate).

Too few RBCs
→ tissue hypoxia (not enough O&sub2; delivered).		 Too many RBCs
→ increased blood viscosity (blood gets thick and hard to pump).

Hormonal Control: Erythropoietin (EPO)

EPO is the direct stimulus for erythropoiesis. It's released by the kidneys in response to hypoxia (low oxygen). Picture a thermostat:

Causes of hypoxia that trigger EPO: hemorrhage or increased RBC destruction, insufficient hemoglobin (e.g., iron deficiency), or reduced O&sub2; availability (e.g., high altitude). EPO speeds maturation of marrow cells and raises the reticulocyte count within 1–2 days. Testosterone also boosts EPO — another reason males run higher RBC counts.

RBC Death & Recycling

RBCs live only 100–120 days. With no nucleus they can't repair themselves, so they grow fragile and hemoglobin degrades. Macrophages in the spleen ("the RBC graveyard") engulf old cells. Then:

  • Iron is salvaged and reused (stored on ferritin/transferrin).
  • Heme is degraded into the yellow pigment bilirubin; the liver secretes it in bile into the intestine.
  • Globin is broken into amino acids and recycled.
💡 Mnemonic: "Kidneys Know when O&sub2; is low" — the Kidney makes EPO. And recycling: Iron saved, Heme → bilirubin, Globin → amino acids.
⚠️ Common confusion: The kidney makes EPO, not the bone marrow. The marrow is just the factory that responds to EPO. This is why kidney failure often causes anemia.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What organ releases EPO and what triggers it?
    Show answer
    The kidneys release EPO in response to hypoxia (low blood oxygen).
  2. Trace what happens to heme, iron, and globin when an old RBC is destroyed.
    Show answer
    Iron is salvaged and reused; heme becomes bilirubin (secreted in bile by the liver); globin is broken into amino acids.
  3. Why might someone at high altitude produce more RBCs?
    Show answer
    Thin air = less available O&sub2; = hypoxia, which triggers EPO release and increased RBC production to carry more oxygen.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The lifespan of a red blood cell is about:
    A) 10–20 days · B) 100–120 days · C) 1 year · D) Indefinite
    Show answer
    B. 100–120 days.
  2. Old red blood cells are mainly destroyed in the:

  3. Chronic kidney disease often causes anemia because:
    A) The kidneys store RBCs · B) Less EPO is produced · C) Iron is lost in urine · D) The marrow shuts down

🩺 Clinical Scenario
A newborn becomes jaundiced (yellow skin and eyes) a few days after birth. The pediatrician explains it relates to RBC breakdown. What pigment is responsible, and why does it build up?
Show answer
Bilirubin, the yellow breakdown product of heme. A newborn's liver is immature and can't clear bilirubin fast enough as fetal RBCs are broken down, so it accumulates and yellows the skin and sclera.

TOPIC 4 🛡️ Leukocytes (White Blood Cells) MUST KNOW▼

Leukocytes (WBCs) are the body's defense force. They make up <1% of blood volume but are the only complete cells in blood (they keep their nucleus and organelles). Key abilities:

  • Diapedesis — squeezing out through capillary walls into tissue.
  • Amoeboid motion & positive chemotaxis — crawling toward chemical "distress signals" from damaged tissue or pathogens.
  • Leukocytosis — WBC count >10,000/mm³; a normal response to infection.

The Two Families

Granulocytes
Visible cytoplasmic granules, lobed nuclei, all phagocytic. Neutrophils, Eosinophils, Basophils.		 Agranulocytes
No visible granules, spherical/kidney-shaped nuclei. Lymphocytes, Monocytes.

The 5 WBCs (most → least abundant)

WBCFamilyJob
Neutrophils (~60%)GranulocyteMost numerous; "bacteria slayers." Very phagocytic; granules hold defensins.
Lymphocytes (~25%+)AgranulocyteImmunity & memory. T cells attack infected/tumor cells; B cells → plasma cells make antibodies.
Monocytes (~5%)AgranulocyteLargest WBC; leave blood → become macrophages; fight chronic infections, activate lymphocytes.
Eosinophils (~2–4%)GranulocyteBi-lobed nucleus; digest parasitic worms; dampen allergic responses.
Basophils (<1%, rarest)GranulocyteRelease histamine (vasodilator) in inflammation/allergy; similar to mast cells.
💡 Mnemonic (most → least common): "Never Let Monkeys Eat Bananas" = Neutrophils > Lymphocytes > Monocytes > Eosinophils > Basophils.
⚠️ Common confusion: Monocytes become macrophages only after leaving the blood and entering tissue — same cell line, different name and location. And note neutrophils are also called PMNs (polymorphonuclear leukocytes) because of their multi-lobed nuclei.

Leukocyte Disorders

  • Leukopenia — abnormally low WBC count (often drug-induced).
  • Leukemia — cancer of WBCs; marrow fills with immature, nonfunctional WBCs. Myelocytic (myeloblasts) vs lymphocytic (lymphocytes); acute (blast cells, mostly children) vs chronic (older adults). Death is from internal hemorrhage and overwhelming infection.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What makes WBCs unique among the formed elements?
    Show answer
    They are the only complete cells in blood (they retain a nucleus and organelles). RBCs have no nucleus; platelets are fragments.
  2. Which WBC is most numerous, and what is its main job?
    Show answer
    Neutrophils — highly phagocytic "bacteria slayers."
  3. What is diapedesis?
    Show answer
    The ability of WBCs to squeeze through capillary walls to reach infected/injured tissue.
  4. Differentiate B lymphocytes from T lymphocytes.
    Show answer
    T cells act directly against virus-infected and tumor cells; B cells become plasma cells that secrete antibodies.

  1. Which WBC releases histamine during allergic reactions?

  3. Monocytes that migrate into tissues become:
    A) Plasma cells · B) Mast cells · C) Macrophages · D) Megakaryocytes

🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient on chemotherapy develops a dangerously low neutrophil count (neutropenia) and is placed in protective isolation. Why does losing neutrophils put them at such high risk?
Show answer
Neutrophils are the most numerous WBC and the front-line phagocytes against bacteria. Without them, even minor bacterial exposure can become a life-threatening infection, so the patient is isolated to limit exposure.

TOPIC 5 🩸 Platelets, Hemostasis & Blood Types MUST KNOW▼

Platelets (thrombocytes) are small fragments of megakaryocytes (not whole cells). Their granules carry serotonin, Ca²⁺, ADP, enzymes, and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF). Their job is hemostasis — stopping bleeding.

Hemostasis: 3 Steps in Rapid Sequence

  1. Vascular spasm — the damaged vessel constricts (triggered by direct injury, chemicals from endothelial cells/platelets, and pain reflexes), reducing blood loss and buying time.
  2. Platelet plug formation — normally platelets slide over smooth endothelium. When it's damaged, prostacyclin and exposed collagen cause platelets to swell, form spikes, and stick at the injury — building a plug. Platelets stick only to damaged areas, keeping the plug local.
  3. Coagulation (clotting) — three phases:
    • Phase I: prothrombin activator (prothrombinase) forms.
    • Phase II: prothrombinase converts prothrombin → thrombin.
    • Phase III: thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen → insoluble fibrin, which forms the mesh that traps cells. The whole clot can form in as little as 3 minutes.

After the Clot

  • Clot retraction — platelet contractile proteins pull fibrin strands, drawing wound edges together and squeezing out serum.
  • Repair — PDGF stimulates smooth muscle and fibroblasts to rebuild the vessel wall.
  • Fibrinolysis — once healed, plasminogen → plasmin (a fibrin-digesting enzyme) dissolves and removes the clot.

ABO & Rh Blood Types

TypeAntigens on RBCAntibodies in plasmaCan receive from
AAanti-BA, O
BBanti-AB, O
ABA and Bnoneall (universal recipient)
Ononeanti-A and anti-BO only (universal donor)

Rh factor: Rh⁺ people have the D antigen; Rh⁻ do not. An Rh⁻ mother carrying an Rh⁺ baby can be sensitized and produce anti-Rh antibodies that attack a future Rh⁺ baby's RBCs — this is hemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN), prevented with RhoGAM.

💡 Mnemonic: Hemostasis = "Spasm, Plug, Clot." For donors: "O gives to all, AB takes from all" (O = universal donor, AB = universal recipient). Clotting cascade end: "fibrinogen (soluble) → fibrin (the net)."
⚠️ Common confusion: Fibrinogen is the soluble protein floating in plasma; fibrin is the insoluble thread that forms the clot mesh. Thrombin converts one into the other. Also: type O has no antigens (donor) but both antibodies; type AB is the reverse.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. List the 3 steps of hemostasis in order.
    Show answer
    Vascular spasm → platelet plug formation → coagulation (clotting).
  2. What enzyme converts fibrinogen to fibrin?
    Show answer
    Thrombin (Phase III of coagulation).
  3. Why is type O⁻ called the universal donor?
    Show answer
    It has no A, B, or Rh antigens on its RBCs, so a recipient's antibodies have nothing to attack.
  4. What process dissolves a clot once the vessel is healed?
    Show answer
    Fibrinolysis — plasminogen is activated to plasmin, which digests fibrin.

🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient on the blood thinner warfarin cuts themselves and the bleeding takes a very long time to stop. Which step of hemostasis is most affected, and why?
Show answer
Coagulation (step 3). Warfarin blocks vitamin K–dependent clotting factors, so the fibrin clot forms slowly. The spasm and platelet plug still occur, but without a strong fibrin mesh the plug isn't reinforced and bleeding persists.

TOPIC 6 ❤️ Heart Anatomy: Wall Layers & Chambers MUST KNOW▼

The heart sits in the mediastinum, pointing its apex down and to the left. It is a double pump — two side-by-side pumps in one organ.

The 3 Wall Layers (outside → inside)

Epicardium
Outer layer = visceral layer of the serous pericardium.		 Myocardium
Thick middle layer of cardiac muscle — the part that actually contracts. Reinforced by the fibrous skeleton.		 Endocardium
Inner lining (simple squamous epithelium), continuous with the lining of blood vessels.

The fibrous skeleton of the heart is crisscrossing connective tissue that: anchors cardiac muscle fibers, supports the great vessels and valves, and limits the spread of action potentials to specific pathways (electrically insulating atria from ventricles).

The 4 Chambers

  • Atria (receiving chambers) — thin-walled; internal walls ridged by pectinate muscles. Right atrium receives deoxygenated blood from the superior & inferior vena cava and coronary sinus; left atrium receives oxygenated blood from the pulmonary veins.
  • Ventricles (discharging chambers) — the muscular pumps; walls ridged by trabeculae carneae. Right ventricle → pulmonary trunk (to lungs); left ventricle → aorta (to body). The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it pumps against the whole body's resistance.

Coronary Circulation

The heart muscle has its own blood supply. Coronary arteries (right & left coronary, marginal, circumflex, anterior interventricular) branch off the aorta and run in the atrioventricular groove. Coronary veins (great, small, anterior cardiac) drain into the coronary sinus → right atrium. Blockage here = heart attack.

💡 Mnemonic: Layers outside-in: "Epi (outer), Myo (muscle/middle), Endo (inner)." Chambers: "Atria Accept, Ventricles eject (V for 'Vamoose')." LEFT is for the LONG trip (whole body), so it's the thickest.
⚠️ Common confusion: The right side carries deoxygenated blood (to lungs); the left side carries oxygenated blood (to body). On standard diagrams the patient's right is on YOUR left. Also: pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood and pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood — the only place where this is "reversed" from the usual artery/vein rule.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Name the 3 layers of the heart wall from outside to inside.
    Show answer
    Epicardium, myocardium, endocardium.
  2. Why is the left ventricle wall the thickest?
    Show answer
    It must generate enough pressure to pump blood through the entire systemic circuit against high resistance.
  3. What are two functions of the heart's fibrous skeleton?
    Show answer
    Anchors muscle fibers and valves/great vessels, and electrically insulates atria from ventricles to direct action potentials along set pathways.

🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient has a blockage in the left anterior descending coronary artery. Even though the heart's chambers are full of blood, part of the myocardium begins to die. Why can't the blood inside the chambers supply that muscle?
Show answer
The thick myocardium is too thick to be nourished by diffusion from chamber blood; it depends on the coronary arteries for its own supply. A coronary blockage starves that muscle of O&sub2; → myocardial infarction (heart attack) in the region the artery fed.

TOPIC 7 🚪 Valves & Blood Flow Pathway MUST KNOW▼

Valves keep blood moving in one direction. They open and close passively in response to pressure differences — no muscle effort required.

The 4 Valves

ValveLocationPrevents backflow into…
Tricuspid (right AV)Right atrium → right ventriclethe right atrium
Pulmonary semilunarRight ventricle → pulmonary trunkthe right ventricle
Bicuspid / Mitral (left AV)Left atrium → left ventriclethe left atrium
Aortic semilunarLeft ventricle → aortathe left ventricle
  • AV valves (tricuspid, bicuspid) prevent backflow into the atria when ventricles contract. Chordae tendineae ("heart strings") anchored to papillary muscles hold the flaps so they don't blow inside-out. They open when ventricles relax.
  • Semilunar valves (pulmonary, aortic) prevent backflow into the ventricles when ventricles relax. Contraction raises pressure and forces them open; relaxation drops pressure and they snap shut.

The Pathway of Blood Through the Heart

Full sequence: Right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary SL valve → pulmonary trunk → pulmonary arteries → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → bicuspid (mitral) valve → left ventricle → aortic SL valve → aorta → body → back to right atrium.

💡 Mnemonic: AV valves — "LAB RAT": Left = Bicuspid, Right = Tricuspid. Picture the path as a figure-8: right side does the short lung loop, left side does the big body loop. "Try (tri) before you buy (bi)" — tricuspid is on the right (you meet it first in blood flow).
⚠️ Common confusion: Chordae tendineae and papillary muscles attach only to the AV valves, NOT the semilunar valves. The semilunars are cusps shaped like half-moons that fill and seal from backflow pressure alone.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Which valve is between the left atrium and left ventricle?
    Show answer
    The bicuspid (mitral) valve.
  2. What do the chordae tendineae and papillary muscles do?
    Show answer
    They anchor the AV valve flaps so they don't evert (blow backward into the atria) when the ventricles contract.
  3. Trace blood from the right atrium to the aorta, naming each valve.
    Show answer
    RA → tricuspid → RV → pulmonary SL → lungs → LA → bicuspid → LV → aortic SL → aorta.

TOPIC 8 ⚡ Conduction System & ECG MUST KNOW▼

The heart beats on its own because of the intrinsic cardiac conduction system — a network of noncontractile autorhythmic cells that spontaneously generate and spread electrical impulses to coordinate contraction. (The nervous system only speeds it up or slows it down; it doesn't start the beat.)

The Conduction Pathway

  • SA node (pacemaker) — in the right atrial wall; fires ~75×/min (sinus rhythm); depolarizes fastest, so it sets the pace.
  • AV node — delays the impulse ~0.1 s (so atria finish emptying before ventricles contract); intrinsic rate ~50/min.
  • AV bundle (bundle of His) — the only electrical connection between atria and ventricles (the fibrous skeleton insulates the rest).
  • Right & left bundle branches — run down the interventricular septum toward the apex.
  • Purkinje fibers — spread the impulse through the ventricular walls; intrinsic rate ~30/min. Ventricles contract from the apex up, wringing blood toward the outflow valves.

Pacemaker potential: autorhythmic cells never rest — slow Na⁺ leak channels drift them toward threshold; then Ca²⁺ channels open for depolarization. This is why the heart self-excites.

The ECG (EKG): 3 Waves

  • P wave — atrial depolarization (atria about to contract).
  • QRS complex — ventricular depolarization (atrial repolarization is hidden inside it).
  • T wave — ventricular repolarization.

Heart Sounds

The "lub-dup" is the sound of valves closing. "Lub" (S1) = AV valves closing at the start of ventricular systole. "Dup" (S2) = semilunar valves closing at the start of ventricular diastole. Heart murmurs are abnormal sounds usually from faulty valves.

💡 Mnemonic: Conduction order = "SA → AV → His → Branches → Purkinje" (think: "Some Athletes Have Big Purkinje"). ECG: "Push (atria), QRS squeeze (ventricles), T = time to reset." Rates fall down the chain: 75 → 50 → 30.
⚠️ Common confusion: There's no separate wave for atrial repolarization — it's buried within the larger QRS complex. Also, the SA node sets the rate only because it depolarizes fastest; if it fails, the AV node (~50/min) or Purkinje fibers (~30/min) take over at a slower pace.

TOPIC 9 💙 Cardiac Cycle & Cardiac Output MUST KNOW▼

The cardiac cycle is all the events of one complete heartbeat. Two key words: systole (contraction) and diastole (relaxation).

Phases of the Cardiac Cycle

  1. Ventricular filling (mid-to-late diastole) — AV valves open; 80% of blood flows in passively, then atrial systole tops off the last 20%. The volume now in each ventricle is the end diastolic volume (EDV), ~120 mL.
  2. Ventricular systole — atria relax, ventricles contract. Rising pressure slams the AV valves shut (isovolumetric contraction — all valves closed). When pressure exceeds the arteries, SL valves open and blood is ejected (ejection phase). What's left behind is the end systolic volume (ESV), ~50 mL.
  3. Isovolumetric relaxation (early diastole) — ventricles relax; backflow toward the ventricles closes the SL valves, creating the dicrotic notch (a small blip in aortic pressure).

Cardiac Output (CO)

CO = HR × SV
Cardiac Output = Heart Rate × Stroke Volume
At rest: 75 beats/min × 70 mL/beat = ~5.25 L/min (roughly your whole blood volume every minute!)

Stroke volume (SV) = EDV − ESV. Example: 120 mL − 50 mL = 70 mL/beat. Cardiac reserve = the difference between resting and maximal CO (up to 4–5× in non-athletes, ~35 L/min in trained athletes).

3 Factors That Change Stroke Volume

Preload
Degree of stretch before contraction (Frank-Starling law): more venous return → more stretch → stronger contraction → more SV.		 Contractility
Strength independent of stretch. Positive inotropes (Ca²⁺, epinephrine, thyroxine) increase it; negative inotropes (Ca-channel blockers) decrease it.		 Afterload
Resistance the ventricle pushes against. Higher afterload (e.g., hypertension) → harder work, less SV.

Regulation of Heart Rate

  • Sympathetic (fight-or-flight) — norepinephrine makes the SA node fire faster and boosts contractility. Positive chronotropic (rate) and inotropic (force) effect.
  • Parasympathetic (vagus nerve) — acetylcholine hyperpolarizes pacemaker cells, slowing the heart. At rest the heart shows vagal tone.
  • Hormones/ions — epinephrine and thyroxine raise HR; abnormal K⁺ or Ca²⁺ cause irregular rhythms.
  • Other factors — age, sex, exercise, body temperature.

Imbalances: Tachycardia = HR >100 bpm; bradycardia = HR <60 bpm (can be normal/healthy in trained athletes). Congestive heart failure (CHF) = CO so low that circulation can't meet tissue needs (causes include coronary atherosclerosis, chronic hypertension, repeated MIs, dilated cardiomyopathy).

💡 Mnemonic: "CO = HR × SV" is the master equation. SV factors = "Pre-Con-After" (Preload, Contractility, Afterload). Chrono = clock (rate), Ino = intensity (force). Frank-Starling: "the more it fills, the harder it spills."
⚠️ Common confusion: Preload = stretch before contraction (related to EDV/venous return); afterload = resistance the heart pushes against during ejection. Don't mix them up. And systole = squeeze, diastole = dilate/fill.

TOPIC 10 🌐 Blood Vessel Structure MUST KNOW▼

Blood travels in a closed system of vessels that begins and ends at the heart. Three major types:

Arteries
Carry blood away from the heart. Thick, muscular, elastic walls (high pressure).		 Capillaries
Microscopic exchange vessels; one cell thick; contact tissue cells directly.		 Veins
Carry blood toward the heart. Thinner walls, larger lumens, valves (low pressure).

The 3 Tunics (wall layers of arteries & veins)

From inside out: tunica intima (endothelium, smooth lining), tunica media (smooth muscle + elastin — controls vasoconstriction/vasodilation), and tunica externa (collagen, anchors the vessel). Capillaries have only the tunica intima (one cell thick) for fast exchange.

Artery Types

  • Elastic (conducting) arteries — largest (aorta & major branches); lots of elastin; act as pressure reservoirs that expand and recoil to smooth out the pulsing flow.
  • Muscular (distributing) arteries — deliver blood to organs; thick muscular media; active in vasoconstriction.
  • Arterioles — smallest arteries; control flow into capillary beds via vasodilation/constriction (the main resistance vessels).

Capillary Types

  • Continuous — least permeable; in skin, muscle, CNS. In the brain, tight junctions form the blood-brain barrier.
  • Fenestrated — have pores; more permeable; for absorption/filtration (small intestine, kidneys, endocrine glands).
  • Sinusoidal — "leaky," large gaps; let big molecules and cells pass (liver, bone marrow, spleen).

Capillary beds have a vascular shunt plus true capillaries; precapillary sphincters regulate how much blood enters the true capillaries.

Veins as Blood Reservoirs

Veins are capacitance vessels — they hold up to 65% of the body's blood at any moment. Their large lumens offer little resistance, and valves (most abundant in the limbs) prevent backflow against gravity.

💡 Mnemonic: "Arteries Away" from the heart; veins return. Tunics from inside out: "Intima, Media, Externa" (I'M Excited). Capillary permeability low→high: "Continuous < Fenestrated < Sinusoidal" (Cautious, Fairly leaky, Super leaky).
⚠️ Common confusion: "Artery" does NOT mean "oxygenated." Arteries carry blood away from the heart — the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood. Define arteries/veins by direction, not oxygen content.

  3. Why are veins called capacitance vessels?

TOPIC 11 📊 Blood Pressure, Resistance & Flow MUST KNOW▼

Three Key Terms

Blood Flow
Volume passing a point per minute (mL/min). For the whole system it equals cardiac output.		 Blood Pressure (BP)
Force per unit area on the vessel wall (mm Hg). The gradient from high to low keeps blood moving.		 Resistance (PR)
Friction opposing flow. Mostly in the periphery → peripheral resistance.

3 Sources of Resistance

  • Blood viscosity — "thickness." More formed elements/proteins → more resistance. (Constant normally.)
  • Vessel length — longer vessel → more resistance. (Constant normally; rises with weight gain.)
  • Vessel diameter — the big one. Resistance changes with the 4th power of the radius, so small changes in arteriole diameter dramatically change resistance. Arterioles are the major resistance vessels.
Blood Flow ∝ ΔPressure  ÷  Resistance
Flow rises with a bigger pressure gradient and falls when resistance goes up.

Systemic Blood Pressure

The heart's pumping generates flow; pressure appears when flow meets resistance. BP is highest in the aorta, declines along the path, and is ~0 mm Hg at the right atrium. The steepest drop occurs across the arterioles (because they have the most resistance).

Arterial Pressure Terms

  • Systolic pressure — peak pressure during ventricular contraction (top number, ~120).
  • Diastolic pressure — lowest pressure during ventricular relaxation (bottom number, ~80).
  • Pulse pressure = systolic − diastolic (e.g., 120 − 80 = 40).
  • Mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≈ diastolic + ⅓(pulse pressure); the pressure that actually drives blood to tissues.

Capillary pressure is low (~17 mm Hg) on purpose — high pressure would rupture their thin walls; low pressure gently pushes filtrate into tissues. Venous pressure is low and steady (~15 mm Hg gradient).

💡 Mnemonic: Resistance sources = "Viscosity, Vessel length, Vessel diameter" (3 V's) — diameter wins because of the radius⁴ effect. BP numbers: "Systolic = Squeeze (top)," diastolic = relax (bottom). MAP "drives the blood home."
⚠️ Common confusion: A small vessel gives high resistance (it's inverse!). And the steepest pressure drop is across the arterioles, not the capillaries — arterioles are the adjustable "faucets" of the system.

TOPIC 12 💧 Capillary Exchange & Venous Return ▼

Capillaries are where the whole system earns its keep — this is where blood actually serves the cells. Tissue perfusion is blood flow through tissues, supplying O&sub2;/nutrients and removing wastes.

Why Capillary Flow Is Slow (and That's Good)

Blood velocity is slowest in the capillaries. Even though each capillary is tiny, there are billions of them, so their total cross-sectional area is enormous — like a river slowing as it spreads into a wide delta. Slow flow gives time for diffusion and exchange between blood and tissue.

Factors Aiding Venous Return

Venous pressure is too low to push blood back to the heart on its own (especially uphill from the legs). Three helpers:

🦵 Muscular pump
Contracting skeletal muscles squeeze ("milk") veins; one-way valves keep blood moving toward the heart.		 💨 Respiratory pump
Breathing changes pressures — abdominal veins are squeezed as thoracic veins expand, pulling blood upward.		 🎓 Venoconstriction
Sympathetic stimulation narrows veins, pushing stored blood back toward the heart.

Autoregulation: Local Control of Flow

Autoregulation automatically matches blood flow to each tissue's needs by changing local arteriole diameter:

  • Metabolic controls — low O&sub2; or rising metabolic wastes (CO&sub2;, H⁺, K⁺) trigger vasodilation, sending more blood to active tissue (e.g., working muscle).
  • Angiogenesis — long-term, the body grows more vessels (or enlarges existing ones) where demand is chronically high (e.g., the heart of an endurance athlete, or a tumor).
💡 Mnemonic: Venous return helpers = "Muscles, Breathing, Veins" (Muscular pump, Respiratory pump, Venoconstriction). Capillaries: "Slow flow = great exchange." Autoregulation: "busy tissue calls for more blood" (low O&sub2; → vasodilate).
⚠️ Common confusion: Slow capillary velocity is NOT a sign of poor circulation — it's by design, to maximize exchange time. Velocity is lowest in capillaries because their total cross-sectional area is greatest, even though each one is microscopic.

TOPIC 13 ⚖️ Regulation of Blood Pressure MUST KNOW▼

Blood pressure must stay in a narrow window. The body controls it with the master equation:

BP = CO × PR
Blood Pressure = Cardiac Output × Peripheral Resistance  (and CO depends on blood volume)

Three organs cooperate under the brain's supervision: the heart (sets CO), blood vessels (set PR), and kidneys (set blood volume). Change one variable and the others compensate.

Short-Term Control (seconds to minutes) — alters resistance

  • Neural — baroreceptor reflex: Baroreceptors in the carotid sinuses and aortic arch sense stretch. The vasomotor center (medulla) adjusts vessel diameter to maintain vasomotor tone. If BP drops, it triggers vasoconstriction + faster HR; if BP rises, the opposite.
  • Hormonal: Epinephrine/norepinephrine (adrenal medulla) → vasoconstriction + ↑CO. ADH → intense vasoconstriction in severe hypotension. ANP (from the atria) → vasodilation + lowers blood volume/BP.

Long-Term Control (hours to days) — alters blood volume

The kidneys regulate BP by controlling blood volume:

  • Direct mechanism: high BP/volume → kidneys excrete more urine → volume and BP fall. Low BP/volume → kidneys conserve water → BP rises.
  • Indirect (renin-angiotensin-aldosterone): low BP → kidneys release renin → angiotensin II (a potent vasoconstrictor) + aldosterone (retains Na⁺ and water) → BP rises.
💡 Mnemonic: "BP = CO × PR." Short-term = nerves & hormones change resistance (fast). Long-term = kidneys change volume (slow). Baroreceptors live in the "neck and arch" (carotid sinus + aortic arch). RAAS: "Renin Raises pressure."
⚠️ Common confusion: ANP lowers BP (it's the "anti-pressure" hormone — promotes water/salt loss and vasodilation), while ADH, aldosterone, and angiotensin II raise BP. Don't lump ANP in with the pressure-raising hormones.

    Show answer
    In the carotid sinuses and aortic arch; they detect stretch (a proxy for blood pressure).
  3. How do the kidneys raise a low blood pressure?
    Show answer
    They conserve water (directly) and release renin → angiotensin II (vasoconstriction) + aldosterone (Na⁺/water retention), raising blood volume and BP.

    B. Carotid sinus and aortic arch.

  • Pulse pressure — systolic − diastolic.
  • MAP — mean arterial pressure; drives tissue perfusion.
  • Baroreceptors — stretch sensors in carotid sinus & aortic arch.
  • Autoregulation — local adjustment of flow to match tissue needs.
  • Angiogenesis — growth of new blood vessels.

🎯 Final Study Tips

  • Draw the heart and trace blood flow through all 4 chambers and valves, labeling oxygenated (red) vs deoxygenated (blue). If you can do this from memory, the heart chapter is yours.
  • Memorize the conduction sequence and the WBC order. "Some Athletes Have Big Purkinje" and "Never Let Monkeys Eat Bananas."
  • Master the three master equations: CO = HR × SV, SV = EDV − ESV, and BP = CO × PR.
  • Connect structure to function. Why is the left ventricle thickest? Why are capillaries one cell thick? Why do veins have valves? The "why" sticks better than the "what."
  • Use the clinical scenarios. The exam loves applying concepts (anemia, MI, varicose veins, ACE inhibitors). Practice the "so what?"
  • Quiz with the hidden answers — active recall beats rereading. Use the cheat sheet the night before AND the morning of the exam.

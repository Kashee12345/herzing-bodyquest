ⓘ What you'll learn in this unit

Unit 6 covers the body's two great processing systems. The urinary system filters the blood, balances water and electrolytes, and makes urine to clear wastes. The digestive system breaks food down into absorbable nutrients and eliminates the residue. One handles what goes out of the blood; the other handles what comes in. Learn how each is built and how it works, step by step, and it all clicks.

🧬 The Big Picture: In and Out

TOPIC 1 🧴 Kidney Functions & Anatomy MUST KNOW▼

The kidneys are the body's master filtration and balancing organ. The rest of the urinary system just transports and stores what the kidneys make.

Functions of the Kidneys

  • Regulate water volume & solute concentration in body fluids.
  • Regulate ion balance in the extracellular fluid (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, etc.).
  • Long-term acid-base balance (adjusting H⁺ and HCO₃⁻ in urine).
  • Excrete metabolic wastes, toxins, and drugs.
  • Produce hormones: renin (regulates blood pressure) and erythropoietin/EPO (regulates RBC production).
  • Activate vitamin D and perform gluconeogenesis if needed.
Heads up: the slides swap the labels — correctly, renin regulates blood pressure and erythropoietin (EPO) regulates RBC production.

The Other Urinary Organs

Ureters (carry urine kidney → bladder), urinary bladder (temporary storage), urethra (carries urine out of the body).

External Anatomy

  • Retroperitoneal (behind the peritoneum), in the superior lumbar region. The adrenal (suprarenal) gland sits atop each kidney.
  • Concave medial surface has the renal hilum — where ureters, vessels, and nerves enter/exit.
  • Three supportive layers (out to in): renal fascia (anchors), perirenal fat capsule (cushion), fibrous capsule (prevents infection spread).

Internal Anatomy & Urine Flow

Three regions: renal cortex (outer, granular), renal medulla (cone-shaped renal pyramids separated by renal columns), and the renal pelvis (funnel to the ureter). Urine drains:

renal pyramid (papilla) → minor calyx → major calyx → renal pelvis → ureter

Blood Flow Through the Kidney

Renal artery → segmental → interlobar → arcuate → cortical radiate (interlobular) arteries → afferent arteriole → glomerulus → efferent arteriole → peritubular capillaries/vasa recta → (veins reverse the order) → renal vein. Note: the glomerulus is unusual — fed AND drained by arterioles.

💡 Mnemonic: Urine flow = "Papilla, minor, major, pelvis, ureter" (small cups → big cups → funnel → tube). Kidney layers protect: "Fat cushions, Fascia anchors, Fibrous fights infection." Glomerulus = "Afferent Arrives, Efferent Exits."
⚠️ Common confusion: The kidneys are retroperitoneal (behind the peritoneal lining), not inside the peritoneal cavity. And the glomerulus is fed and drained by arterioles (afferent in, efferent out) — unlike normal capillary beds drained by venules.

Show answer

TOPIC 2 🔬 The Nephron MUST KNOW▼

The nephron is the structural and functional unit of the kidney that actually forms urine. There are over 1 million per kidney. Each has two main parts:

Part 1: The Renal Corpuscle (the filter)

  • Glomerulus — a tuft of fenestrated (porous) capillaries built for high-volume filtration.
  • Glomerular (Bowman's) capsule — cup that surrounds the glomerulus. Its visceral layer has branching podocytes whose filtration slits let filtrate pass into the capsular space; the parietal layer is simple squamous.

Part 2: The Renal Tubule (processes the filtrate)

A single layer of epithelium, but each region has unique histology and function:

Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) → Nephron loop (loop of Henle) → Distal convoluted tubule (DCT) → collecting duct

Collecting ducts fuse and drain through the papillae into the calyces.

Two Classes of Nephrons

Type% & role
Cortical nephrons85%; mostly in the cortex; routine filtration.
Juxtamedullary nephrons15%; long loops dipping deep into the medulla; essential for producing concentrated urine.
💡 Mnemonic: Tubule order = "Proud Lions Drink Carefully" → PCT, Loop, DCT, Collecting duct. Podocytes have feet (pedicels) with filtration slits. Juxtamedullary = "next to medulla" → deep loops → concentrate urine.
⚠️ Common confusion: The renal corpuscle = glomerulus + Bowman's capsule (the filter). The renal tubule = the rest (PCT, loop, DCT). Together they make the nephron. The collecting duct is technically shared by multiple nephrons (not part of one nephron).

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What are the two main parts of a nephron?
    Show answer
    The renal corpuscle (glomerulus + glomerular/Bowman's capsule) and the renal tubule (PCT, nephron loop, DCT).
  2. What's special about the glomerular capillaries?
    Show answer
    They are fenestrated (have pores), allowing high-volume filtration of plasma.
  3. Which nephron type makes concentrated urine, and why?
    Show answer
    Juxtamedullary nephrons — their long loops dip deep into the medulla to build the osmotic gradient needed to concentrate urine.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The structural and functional unit of the kidney is the:
    A) Calyx · B) Nephron · C) Renal column · D) Pyramid
    Show answer
    B. The nephron.
  2. Filtrate passes into the capsular space through:
    A) Goblet cells · B) Filtration slits of podocytes · C) Microvilli · D) The renal pelvis
    Show answer
    B. Filtration slits between podocyte foot processes.
  3. The correct order of the renal tubule is:
    A) DCT → loop → PCT · B) PCT → loop → DCT · C) Loop → PCT → DCT · D) PCT → DCT → loop
    Show answer
    B. PCT → nephron loop → DCT → collecting duct.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient's urinalysis shows protein and blood cells in the urine. Given the filtration membrane normally blocks these, what part of the nephron is likely damaged?
Show answer
The filtration membrane in the renal corpuscle (glomerulus/podocyte slits). Normally it keeps proteins and blood cells in the blood; when it's damaged (e.g., glomerulonephritis), these leak into the filtrate and appear in urine (proteinuria/hematuria).

TOPIC 3 💧 Urine Formation & GFR MUST KNOW▼

The kidneys process a staggering 180 L of fluid daily but produce only ~1.5 L of urine — they filter the entire plasma volume ~60 times a day! Urine forms through three processes.

The 3 Steps of Urine Formation

  1. Glomerular filtration — a passive process (no ATP). Blood pressure (hydrostatic pressure) forces fluid and small solutes through the filtration membrane into the capsule. The result, filtrate, is basically plasma minus proteins and cells.
  2. Tubular reabsorption — the tubules reclaim ~99% of the filtrate (water, glucose, ions, amino acids) and return it to the blood.
  3. Tubular secretion — moves extra substances from blood into filtrate: K⁺, H⁺, NH₄⁺, creatinine, drugs. Helps control blood pH and clear drugs/wastes.

Final urine is <1% of the original filtrate — mostly metabolic wastes and unneeded substances.

Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR)

GFR = volume of filtrate formed per minute by both kidneys. Normal = 120–125 mL/min.
  • GFR below 60 mL/min for 3+ months = chronic kidney disease.
  • GFR below 15 mL/min = end-stage renal failure (dialysis/transplant needed).
💡 Mnemonic: The 3 steps = "Filter, Reabsorb, Secrete." Direction trick: Reabsorption = filtrate → blood (Return); Secretion = blood → filtrate (Send out). Filtration is the only passive, pressure-driven step. GFR normal = "120ish"; under 60 = disease; under 15 = end-stage.
⚠️ Common confusion: Reabsorption returns substances TO the blood; secretion adds substances FROM the blood into the filtrate. They go opposite directions. Also, filtration is passive (driven by blood pressure), not active transport.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Name the 3 processes of urine formation and the direction of each.
    Show answer
    Glomerular filtration (blood → filtrate), tubular reabsorption (filtrate → blood, ~99%), tubular secretion (blood → filtrate).
  2. Why is glomerular filtration called passive?
    Show answer
    It requires no ATP — blood (hydrostatic) pressure alone forces fluid and solutes across the filtration membrane.
  3. What is a normal GFR, and what value indicates end-stage renal failure?
    Show answer
    Normal GFR is 120–125 mL/min; below 15 mL/min indicates end-stage renal failure.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. About what percent of filtrate is reabsorbed back into the blood?
    A) 50% · B) 75% · C) 99% · D) 10%
    Show answer
    C. ~99% (only <1% becomes urine).
  2. Moving creatinine and drugs from blood into the filtrate is:
    A) Filtration · B) Reabsorption · C) Secretion · D) Diffusion
    Show answer
    C. Tubular secretion.
  3. A GFR under 60 mL/min for 3 months indicates:
    A) Normal kidneys · B) Chronic kidney disease · C) Dehydration only · D) Diabetes
    Show answer
    B. Chronic kidney disease.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient becomes severely dehydrated and their blood pressure drops sharply. Predict what happens to GFR and urine output, and why.
Show answer
Filtration depends on blood (hydrostatic) pressure in the glomerulus. A big drop in blood pressure lowers GFR, so less filtrate forms and urine output falls (oliguria) — the body conserves fluid. If pressure stays too low, kidney injury can result.

TOPIC 4 ⚒️ Regulation: JGA, RAAS & ADH MUST KNOW▼

The Juxtaglomerular Apparatus (JGA)

The JGA sits where the distal tubule touches the afferent arteriole. It regulates the rate of filtrate formation and blood pressure using two cell types:

CellRole
Macula densaChemoreceptors — sense the NaCl content of the filtrate.
Granular (JG) cellsMechanoreceptors — sense blood pressure in the afferent arteriole; contain secretory granules of renin.

The RAAS — Raising Blood Pressure

The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) is the main mechanism for raising blood pressure. Renin is released by granular cells in 3 situations:

  • Direct sympathetic nervous system stimulation.
  • The macula densa detects low NaCl in the filtrate.
  • Reduced stretch of granular cells (low blood pressure).

Concentrating Urine: Countercurrent + ADH

The kidney keeps body fluids near 300 mOsm. To concentrate urine, it uses the countercurrent mechanism — fluid (and blood in the vasa recta) flowing in opposite directions through the nephron loop builds an osmotic gradient from 300 mOsm (cortex) to 1200 mOsm (deep medulla). Then hormones decide how much water to reclaim:

SituationHormone & result
DehydratedMaximal ADH → lots of water reabsorbed → small volume of concentrated urine (~1200 mOsm).
OverhydratedADH decreases → little water reabsorbed → large volume of dilute urine (~100 mOsm).

ADH (antidiuretic hormone) makes the collecting ducts more permeable to water; aldosterone drives Na⁺ (and water) reabsorption while dumping K⁺.

💡 Mnemonic: "RAAS Raises pressure." ADH = Add water back (Anti-Diuretic = less urine). Macula densa = "measures salt" (chemoreceptor); granular cells = "gauge pressure" (mechanoreceptor) + make renin. Gradient: 300 up top, 1200 down deep.
⚠️ Common confusion: ADH conserves water (concentrated urine when dehydrated) — "anti-diuretic" means less urine. Don't confuse it with aldosterone, which mainly conserves Na⁺ (water follows). The macula densa senses salt; granular cells sense pressure.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What do the macula densa and granular cells each sense?
    Show answer
    Macula densa = NaCl content of filtrate (chemoreceptor); granular/JG cells = blood pressure in the afferent arteriole (mechanoreceptor) and they secrete renin.
  2. What does ADH do, and when is it maximal?
    Show answer
    ADH increases water reabsorption in the collecting ducts; it's maximal during dehydration, producing small amounts of concentrated urine (~1200 mOsm).
  3. What is the medullary osmotic gradient range, and what builds it?
    Show answer
    300 mOsm (cortex) to 1200 mOsm (deep medulla), built by the countercurrent multiplier (nephron loop) and exchanger (vasa recta).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The main hormone system for raising blood pressure via the kidney is:
    A) ADH · B) RAAS · C) ANP · D) Insulin
    Show answer
    B. Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.
  2. ADH causes urine to become:
    A) More dilute · B) More concentrated · C) More acidic · D) Larger in volume
    Show answer
    B. More concentrated (it reabsorbs water).
  3. Renin is secreted by:
    A) Macula densa · B) Podocytes · C) Granular (JG) cells · D) Goblet cells
    Show answer
    C. Granular (juxtaglomerular) cells.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient is prescribed an "ACE inhibitor" for high blood pressure, which blocks the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II. Using the RAAS, explain how this lowers blood pressure.
Show answer
Angiotensin II is a powerful vasoconstrictor and triggers aldosterone (which retains Na⁺ and water). Blocking its formation causes vasodilation and less fluid retention, lowering blood pressure — the kidney's RAAS is the target.

TOPIC 5 💊 Urine Characteristics & Urinalysis ▼

Chemical Composition

Urine is 95% water and 5% solutes. The main solutes are nitrogenous wastes:

  • Urea — from amino acid (protein) breakdown; the largest solute component.
  • Uric acid — from nucleic acid metabolism.
  • Creatinine — from creatine phosphate in muscle.

Physical Characteristics

PropertyNormal & notes
ColorPale to deep yellow from urochrome (pigment from hemoglobin breakdown). Deeper yellow = more concentrated. Abnormal (pink, brown, smoky) = foods, blood, bile, drugs.
TransparencyClear when fresh; cloudy may indicate a urinary tract infection (UTI).
OdorSlightly aromatic fresh; develops an ammonia smell as bacteria break down urea on standing.
pHSlightly acidic (~6; range 4.5–8.0). High-protein diet lowers pH; vegetarian diet, vomiting, or UTI raises it.
Specific gravity1.003–1.032 (how concentrated it is vs water).

Urinalysis: What Should NOT Be in Urine

A urinalysis screens for disease. Normally absent — their presence signals a problem:

Abnormal findingMay indicate
Glucose (glycosuria)Diabetes mellitus (blood glucose exceeds reabsorption capacity).
Protein (proteinuria)Damaged glomerular filtration membrane.
Blood (hematuria)Infection, stones, trauma, or tumor.
Ketones (ketonuria)Uncontrolled diabetes or starvation (fat breakdown).
WBCs / leukocyte esterase / nitritesUrinary tract infection.
BilirubinLiver disease or bile duct obstruction.
💡 Mnemonic: "95/5" — 95% water, 5% solutes; Urea is the biggest waste (from protein). Color from urochrome ("uro-chrome = urine color"). Things that shouldn't be there: "Glucose, Protein, Blood, Ketones" each point to a disease.
⚠️ Common confusion: Glucose in urine = diabetes red flag, not normal — healthy kidneys reabsorb all filtered glucose. And the ammonia smell of old urine comes from bacteria breaking down urea, not from the fresh urine itself.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What is the largest solute (nitrogenous waste) in urine, and where does it come from?
    Show answer
    Urea, from the breakdown of amino acids (protein metabolism).
  2. Why is glucose in the urine a concern?
    Show answer
    Normally all filtered glucose is reabsorbed; glucose in urine suggests blood glucose is so high it exceeds reabsorption capacity, as in diabetes mellitus.
  3. What might cloudy urine indicate?
    Show answer
    A urinary tract infection (UTI).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The yellow pigment of urine is:
    A) Bilirubin · B) Urochrome · C) Stercobilin · D) Melanin
    Show answer
    B. Urochrome.
  2. Protein in the urine most likely indicates damage to the:
    A) Bladder · B) Urethra · C) Glomerular filtration membrane · D) Ureter
    Show answer
    C. The glomerular filtration membrane.
  3. Urine is approximately what percent water?
    A) 75% · B) 85% · C) 95% · D) 99%
    Show answer
    C. ~95% water (5% solutes).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient's urinalysis shows glucose and ketones. The provider suspects uncontrolled diabetes. Explain why both appear.
Show answer
In uncontrolled diabetes, blood glucose is so high it overwhelms tubular reabsorption, so glucose spills into urine (glycosuria). Because cells can't use that glucose, the body burns fat for energy, producing ketones that also appear in urine (ketonuria). Together they're a classic sign of poorly controlled diabetes.

TOPIC 6 🚽 Ureters, Bladder, Urethra & Micturition MUST KNOW▼

Ureters

Slender retroperitoneal tubes that carry urine from the kidneys to the bladder. Gravity isn't enough — urine is pushed by peristalsis (smooth muscle waves), whose rate matches urine production. Three wall layers (inside → out): mucosa (transitional epithelium), muscularis (smooth muscle), adventitia (fibrous connective tissue).

Urinary Bladder

  • A muscular sac for temporary urine storage, on the pelvic floor behind the pubic symphysis. (Males: prostate sits below it; females: anterior to the vagina/uterus.)
  • Wall layers: mucosa (transitional epithelium), detrusor muscle (three smooth-muscle layers), and adventitia.
  • The trigone is a smooth triangle marked by the two ureter openings and the urethral opening — a common site of infection.
  • When empty it collapses into folds (rugae); it expands during filling without much pressure rise (thanks to transitional epithelium + rugae).

Urethra & the Two Sphincters

SphincterType & control
Internal urethral sphincterSmooth muscle at the bladder-urethra junction; involuntary.
External urethral sphincterSkeletal muscle in the pelvic floor; voluntary (the one you consciously control).

Male urethra (18–20 cm) carries both urine AND semen; female urethra (3–4 cm) carries only urine. The short female urethra explains why UTIs are more common in women.

Micturition (Urination)

Three events must happen together to void:

  1. Detrusor muscle contracts (ANS).
  2. Internal urethral sphincter opens (ANS, involuntary).
  3. External urethral sphincter opens (somatic nervous system, voluntary).

Voluntary control matures with the pontine control centers around ages 2–3 (why toilet training happens then).

💡 Mnemonic: Sphincters: "Internal = Involuntary (smooth); External = your Effort (skeletal/voluntary)." Ureter/bladder walls: "Mucosa, Muscularis/detrusor, Adventitia." Short female urethra → more UTIs. Trigone = triangle of 3 openings.
⚠️ Common confusion: The internal sphincter is involuntary (smooth muscle); the external is voluntary (skeletal muscle) — the one you consciously hold. Urine moves through ureters by peristalsis, not gravity alone.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. How does urine move through the ureters?
    Show answer
    By peristalsis (smooth muscle waves) — gravity alone is not enough.
  2. Differentiate the internal and external urethral sphincters.
    Show answer
    Internal = smooth muscle, involuntary (ANS); external = skeletal muscle, voluntary (somatic NS).
  3. Why are UTIs more common in women?
    Show answer
    The female urethra is much shorter (3–4 cm vs 18–20 cm), so bacteria reach the bladder more easily.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The triangular region of the bladder between the ureter and urethral openings is the:
    A) Detrusor · B) Trigone · C) Rugae · D) Hilum
    Show answer
    B. The trigone.
  2. Which sphincter is under voluntary control?
    A) Internal urethral · B) External urethral · C) Pyloric · D) Detrusor
    Show answer
    B. External urethral sphincter (skeletal muscle).
  3. The muscular wall of the bladder is the:
    A) Detrusor muscle · B) Trigone · C) Mucosa · D) Adventitia
    Show answer
    A. The detrusor muscle.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A toddler isn't yet reliably toilet-trained, while their older sibling is. Using micturition control, explain the developmental difference.
Show answer
Voluntary control of the external urethral sphincter depends on the pontine control centers, which mature around ages 2–3. Before that, voiding is largely reflexive (the involuntary internal sphincter and detrusor). The older sibling has developed the voluntary control the toddler is still acquiring.

TOPIC 7 👂 Digestive Overview & the Mouth MUST KNOW▼

Two Groups of Digestive Organs

GroupOrgans & job
Alimentary canal (GI tract)Mouth → pharynx → esophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine. Food actually passes through these.
Accessory organsTeeth, tongue, salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, pancreas. Produce secretions that help digestion; food does NOT pass through them.

Mechanical vs. Chemical Digestion

  • Mechanical digestion — physically breaking food up: mastication (chewing), churning in the stomach, segmentation in the intestine.
  • Chemical digestion — enzymes break complex molecules into building blocks (e.g., starch → sugars).

The Mouth & Saliva

The mouth handles ingestion, begins mechanical + chemical digestion, and starts swallowing. Three pairs of salivary glands — parotid, submandibular, sublingual — secrete saliva (controlled mainly by the parasympathetic system). Saliva is:

  • 97–99.5% water, slightly acidic.
  • Salivary amylase — begins starch (carbohydrate) digestion.
  • Mucin — forms mucus to lubricate and bind food into a bolus.
  • Lysozyme + IgA + defensins — antibacterial protection.

Deglutition (Swallowing)

Once food touches the pharynx, swallowing switches from voluntary to involuntary. Peristalsis (wavelike muscle contractions) pushes the bolus down the esophagus; the lower esophageal sphincter relaxes to let it into the stomach.

💡 Mnemonic: Salivary glands: "Pa-Su-Su" (Parotid, Submandibular, Sublingual). Amylase = starts starch ("A for Amylase, A for cArbs"). Swallowing: Buccal = you Begin (voluntary); Pharyngeal = Phinished by reflex (involuntary). Bolus = ball of chewed food.
⚠️ Common confusion: Carbohydrate digestion begins in the mouth (salivary amylase), NOT the stomach. The parasympathetic system increases saliva ("rest and digest"); sympathetic activation causes dry mouth.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Differentiate the alimentary canal from accessory organs.
    Show answer
    The alimentary canal (GI tract) is the continuous tube food passes through (mouth to large intestine); accessory organs (teeth, tongue, salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, pancreas) aid digestion but food doesn't pass through them.
  2. What enzyme in saliva begins digestion, and of what nutrient?
    Show answer
    Salivary amylase, which begins carbohydrate (starch) digestion.
  3. Which swallowing phase is voluntary and which is involuntary?
    Show answer
    The buccal phase (mouth) is voluntary; the pharyngeal-esophageal phase is involuntary (brainstem-controlled).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Salivation is primarily controlled by the:
    A) Sympathetic NS · B) Parasympathetic NS · C) Somatic NS · D) Endocrine system
    Show answer
    B. The parasympathetic division.
  2. A rounded mass of chewed food mixed with saliva is a:
    A) Chyme · B) Bolus · C) Feces · D) Bilirubin
    Show answer
    B. A bolus (chyme forms later, in the stomach).
  3. Food is moved down the esophagus by:
    A) Gravity only · B) Segmentation · C) Peristalsis · D) Mastication
    Show answer
    C. Peristalsis.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A nervous student gives a speech and notices their mouth is very dry. Using the control of salivation, explain why.
Show answer
Anxiety activates the sympathetic ("fight-or-flight") nervous system, which strongly inhibits watery saliva production (and dehydration contributes too). Since salivation is normally driven by the parasympathetic system, sympathetic dominance produces dry mouth.

TOPIC 8 🫊 Stomach: Cells & Gastric Juice MUST KNOW▼

The stomach is a temporary storage tank that does both mechanical digestion (churning) and chemical digestion (begins protein breakdown). Food mixed with gastric juice becomes a creamy paste called chyme.

Key transition: bolus (from the mouth) + gastric juice → chyme.

The Cells of the Stomach & What They Make

CellSecretes & function
Mucous neck cellsAcidic mucus — protects the stomach lining from acid.
Parietal cellsHydrochloric acid (HCl) (pH 1.5–3.5) — denatures proteins, kills bacteria, breaks down plant cell walls. Also intrinsic factor — needed for vitamin B₁₂ absorption in the small intestine.
Chief cellsPepsinogen — activated by HCl into pepsin, the protein-digesting enzyme. (Pepsin then activates more pepsinogen.)
Enteroendocrine cellsHormones like gastrin (stimulates HCl and stomach contractions).

Why Pepsin Is Made as Pepsinogen

Chief cells release the inactive form pepsinogen so the enzyme doesn't digest the cells that make it. HCl from parietal cells converts pepsinogen → active pepsin only once it's safely in the stomach lumen. This "make it inactive, activate it later" theme repeats in the pancreas (trypsinogen → trypsin).

💡 Mnemonic: "Parietal = Acid + Intrinsic factor" (Parietal Produces the Powerful acid). "Chief makes the Chemical (pepsinogen)." Intrinsic factor → B₁₂ ("I.F. = I Find B₁₂"). Bolus becomes chyme in the stomach.
⚠️ Common confusion: Parietal cells make HCl + intrinsic factor; chief cells make pepsinogen. Students often swap them. And protein digestion (not carbohydrate) begins in the stomach — carbs started back in the mouth.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Which stomach cell makes HCl and intrinsic factor, and what is intrinsic factor for?
    Show answer
    Parietal cells; intrinsic factor is required for vitamin B₁₂ absorption in the small intestine.
  2. How is pepsin activated, and why is it secreted in inactive form?
    Show answer
    Chief cells secrete inactive pepsinogen; HCl converts it to active pepsin in the lumen. Inactive form prevents pepsin from digesting the cells that make it.
  3. What is chyme?
    Show answer
    The creamy paste of partially digested food mixed with gastric juices in the stomach.

  1. Hydrochloric acid in the stomach is produced by:

    Show answer
    B. Parietal cells.
  2. Digestion of which nutrient begins in the stomach?
    A) Carbohydrate · B) Protein · C) Fat · D) Fiber
    Show answer
    B. Protein (via pepsin).
  3. Intrinsic factor is necessary for absorption of:
    A) Iron · B) Vitamin B₁₂ · C) Calcium · D) Vitamin D
    Show answer
    B. Vitamin B₁₂.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with an autoimmune condition that destroys parietal cells develops pernicious anemia (B₁₂ deficiency). Connect the lost cells to the anemia.
Show answer
Parietal cells make intrinsic factor, which is required to absorb vitamin B₁₂ in the small intestine. Destroying parietal cells removes intrinsic factor, so B₁₂ can't be absorbed → B₁₂ deficiency → pernicious anemia (B₁₂ is needed for RBC maturation).

TOPIC 9 ⏱️ Gastric Secretion & Emptying MUST KNOW▼

Gastric juice release is controlled by neural and hormonal signals. Parasympathetic input increases secretion; sympathetic decreases it. It unfolds in three phases.

The 3 Phases of Gastric Secretion

  • Cephalic phase — happens before food arrives. The aroma, taste, sight, or thought of food triggers the hypothalamus → vagus nerve → stomach glands "prime the pump."
  • Gastric phase — when food enters the stomach; provides ~2/3 of gastric juice. Stretch receptors and peptides trigger more secretion and gastrin (which boosts HCl, enzymes, and muscle contraction). High acidity then inhibits gastrin (negative feedback).
  • Intestinal phase — when chyme enters the duodenum. The enterogastric reflex INHIBITS gastrin and gastric contractions, and tightens the pyloric sphincter to slow chyme release — protecting the duodenum from too much acid at once.

Gastric Emptying

The stomach usually empties in about 4 hours. Carbohydrate-rich meals pass quickly; fatty meals are slowest (they form an oily layer and can stay up to 6 hours). Stretch and chemical signals in the duodenum control the rate — the gut won't accept more than it can handle.

💡 Mnemonic: Phases = "Head, Stomach, Intestine" (Cephalic = head). Gastric phase = the Giant contributor (~2/3). Intestinal phase Inhibits (enterogastric reflex slows things down). Fat is slow; carbs are quick.
⚠️ Common confusion: The intestinal phase mostly INHIBITS gastric activity (via the enterogastric reflex), while the cephalic and gastric phases stimulate it. And gastrin stimulates acid/contractions, but high acidity shuts gastrin off (negative feedback).

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Name the 3 phases of gastric secretion and their triggers.
    Show answer
    Cephalic (smell/taste/sight/thought of food), gastric (food entering stomach — stretch/peptides), intestinal (chyme entering duodenum).
  2. What does gastrin do?
    Show answer
    Stimulates HCl and enzyme release and increases stomach/intestinal muscle contraction. High acidity inhibits it.
  3. Which meal type empties the stomach slowest, and why?
    Show answer
    Fatty meals — fats form an oily layer that is digested slowly and can remain up to 6 hours.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Smelling food and starting to secrete gastric juice before eating is the:
    A) Gastric phase · B) Intestinal phase · C) Cephalic phase · D) Enterogastric reflex
    Show answer
    C. The cephalic phase.
  2. The enterogastric reflex primarily:
    A) Increases gastrin · B) Inhibits gastric activity · C) Opens the pyloric sphincter · D) Speeds gastric emptying
    Show answer
    B. Inhibits gastric activity (and tightens the pyloric sphincter to slow chyme entry).
  3. Which phase provides about two-thirds of gastric juice?
    A) Cephalic · B) Gastric · C) Intestinal · D) Oral
    Show answer
    B. The gastric phase.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
After a very greasy fast-food meal, a patient feels uncomfortably full for hours, far longer than after a pasta meal. Using gastric emptying, explain the difference.
Show answer
Fat is digested slowly and forms an oily layer on the chyme, so a fatty meal can remain in the stomach up to 6 hours. The duodenum also triggers the enterogastric reflex to slow emptying so fat can be properly emulsified. Carbohydrate-rich meals (pasta) pass quickly, so the full feeling resolves faster.

TOPIC 10 🫇 Small Intestine & Accessory Organs MUST KNOW▼

The Small Intestine — Main Site of Absorption

The longest part of the GI tract (7–13 ft in life). It uses segmentation (rhythmic local contractions that mix chyme) and is where absorption (passage of nutrients to blood/lymph) mainly happens. Three divisions:

DivisionKey facts
DuodenumFirst & shortest (~10 in); receives secretions from the liver and pancreas.
JejunumMiddle; site of most chemical digestion and nutrient absorption.
IleumFinal segment; joins the large intestine.

Lining cells: absorptive cells (columnar), goblet cells (mucus), and enteroendocrine cells (hormones secretin and cholecystokinin/CCK).

Liver & Gallbladder

  • Liver — produces bile, a fat emulsifier (bile breaks large fat globules into tiny droplets so enzymes can work — mechanical, not chemical, breakdown of fat).
  • Gallbladder — stores and concentrates bile. When fatty chyme enters the duodenum, CCK is released and causes the gallbladder to contract, sending bile through the cystic duct → common bile duct → duodenum.
  • Bile is an alkaline solution with bile salts (cholesterol derivatives that emulsify fats) and bilirubin (the chief bile pigment, a heme breakdown product; broken down to stercobilin, which gives feces its brown color).

Pancreas — Exocrine AND Endocrine

PartSecretes
Acinar cells (exocrine)Pancreatic juice — digestive enzymes + bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) that neutralizes acidic chyme entering the duodenum.
Islets of Langerhans (endocrine)α cells → glucagon; β cells → insulin (blood sugar control).

Pancreatic protein enzymes are released inactive for safety: trypsinogen → trypsin (in the duodenum), which then activates the others. (Same "safety" theme as pepsinogen.)

💡 Mnemonic: Small intestine order = "Dow Jones Industrial" → Duodenum, Jejunum, Ileum. Bile emulsifies fat ("bile breaks the blob"). CCK = Call for the gallbladder to Contract (Cholecystokinin = "move the gallbladder"). Pancreas: "β = insulin, α = glucagon" (B before A: insulin lowers sugar).
⚠️ Common confusion: The liver makes bile; the gallbladder only stores it. Bile emulsifies fat (mechanical) — it is NOT an enzyme and doesn't chemically digest fat (lipase does that). Pancreatic bicarbonate neutralizes acid; it doesn't digest food.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Name the 3 divisions of the small intestine in order.
    Show answer
    Duodenum, jejunum, ileum.
  2. What does bile do, and where is it made vs stored?
    Show answer
    Bile emulsifies fats (breaks large globules into droplets). It's made by the liver and stored/concentrated in the gallbladder.
  3. What does pancreatic bicarbonate do?
    Show answer
    Neutralizes the acidic chyme arriving from the stomach, protecting the duodenum and giving enzymes the right pH.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Bile is produced by the:
    A) Gallbladder · B) Pancreas · C) Liver · D) Duodenum
    Show answer
    C. The liver (the gallbladder stores it).
  2. CCK causes the gallbladder to:
    A) Make bile · B) Contract and release bile · C) Absorb water · D) Secrete insulin
    Show answer
    B. Contract and expel bile into the duct toward the duodenum.
  3. Insulin is secreted by which pancreatic cells?
    A) Acinar cells · B) α cells · C) β cells · D) Goblet cells
    Show answer
    C. β (beta) cells of the islets of Langerhans.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient who had their gallbladder removed is told to limit fatty meals. Using bile's role, explain why fatty foods now cause them trouble.
Show answer
The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile and releases a big bolus of it (via CCK) when fatty chyme arrives. Without it, bile drips continuously from the liver but can't be delivered in concentrated bursts, so large fatty meals aren't emulsified well → poor fat digestion, bloating, and diarrhea. Smaller, lower-fat meals are easier to handle.

TOPIC 11 🧬 Chemical Digestion & Absorption MUST KNOW▼

Each major nutrient is broken down by specific enzymes into absorbable building blocks. Know where digestion starts and which enzyme handles each nutrient.

Digestion by Nutrient

NutrientStarts whereEnzymes → building blocks
CarbohydratesMouthSalivary amylase, then pancreatic amylase → monosaccharides (e.g., glucose).
ProteinsStomachPepsin (stomach), then trypsin/chymotrypsin/carboxypeptidase (pancreas) → amino acids.
Fats (lipids)Small intestineBile emulsifies, then lipase → fatty acids + monoglycerides.
Nucleic acidsSmall intestineNucleases → nucleotides.

Key Pancreatic Enzymes

  • Pancreatic amylase — carbohydrates.
  • Trypsin & chymotrypsin — proteins (cleave into smaller peptides); carboxypeptidase trims amino acids one at a time.
  • Lipases — fats.
  • Nucleases — nucleic acids (DNA/RNA).
  • Bicarbonate — neutralizes acid (not an enzyme).

Absorption — Where Nutrients Go

Most absorption happens in the small intestine (especially jejunum) through the columnar absorptive cells:

  • Sugars and amino acids → absorbed into the blood capillaries.
  • Fats → absorbed into lacteals (lymph) as they're too big for blood capillaries initially — that's the link back to the lymphatic system!

The hormones secretin (stimulates pancreatic bicarbonate) and CCK (stimulates pancreatic enzymes + gallbladder contraction) coordinate this.

💡 Mnemonic: Where digestion starts: "Carbs in the mouth, Protein in the stomach, Fat in the small intestine." Enzyme → nutrient: "Amylase = cArbs, Protease/pepsin/trypsin = Protein, Lipase = Lipids, Nuclease = Nucleic acids." Fats take the lacteal (lymph) route.
⚠️ Common confusion: Fats are absorbed into lymph (lacteals), while sugars and amino acids go straight into blood. And remember bile is not an enzyme — it emulsifies fat so lipase (the enzyme) can do the chemical digestion.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Where does digestion of each macronutrient begin?
    Show answer
    Carbohydrates in the mouth, proteins in the stomach, fats in the small intestine.
  2. Match the enzymes: amylase, lipase, trypsin, nuclease to their nutrients.
    Show answer
    Amylase = carbohydrates; lipase = fats; trypsin = proteins; nuclease = nucleic acids.
  3. How are digested fats absorbed, and how is that different from sugars/amino acids?
    Show answer
    Fats are absorbed into lacteals (lymphatic vessels); sugars and amino acids are absorbed directly into blood capillaries.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The enzyme that digests fats is:
    A) Amylase · B) Trypsin · C) Lipase · D) Nuclease
    Show answer
    C. Lipase.
  2. Protein digestion begins with which enzyme in the stomach?
    A) Trypsin · B) Pepsin · C) Amylase · D) Lipase
    Show answer
    B. Pepsin.
  3. Digested fats are absorbed into:
    A) Blood capillaries · B) Lacteals (lymph) · C) The stomach · D) The liver directly

    B. Lacteals (lymphatic capillaries).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with cystic fibrosis has blocked pancreatic ducts and can't deliver pancreatic enzymes to the gut. They lose weight and have greasy, fatty stools. Explain the connection.
Show answer
Without pancreatic enzymes (lipase, amylase, proteases) reaching the small intestine, fats, carbs, and proteins aren't fully digested or absorbed. Undigested fat passes into the stool (steatorrhea — greasy stools), and poor nutrient absorption causes weight loss. Patients often take enzyme replacements with meals.

TOPIC 12 🧸 Large Intestine & Defecation MUST KNOW▼

Subdivisions of the Large Intestine

In order: cecum → (vermiform appendix) → colon → rectum → anal canal → anus. The colon itself runs ascending → transverse → descending → sigmoid.

What the Large Intestine Does

  • Absorbs most of the remaining water from indigestible residue (turning liquid chyme into solid feces).
  • Temporarily stores the residue; contents remain 12–24 hours.
  • Houses bacterial flora (normal gut bacteria) that ferment residue (releasing ~500 mL of gas/flatus daily) and make vitamin K and some B vitamins.

Feces = ~75% water, ~5% bacteria, plus indigestible material, inorganic matter, and sloughed epithelial cells.

The Two Anal Sphincters

SphincterType & control
Internal anal sphincterSmooth muscle — involuntary.
External anal sphincterSkeletal muscle — voluntary (you control it).

Same pattern as the urinary sphincters: internal = involuntary, external = voluntary.

The Defecation Reflex

Stretching of the rectal wall starts the reflex: the sigmoid colon and rectum contract and the internal (involuntary) sphincter relaxes. Signals reach the brain — "is this a good time?" If no, the reflex subsides within seconds (the voluntary external sphincter stays closed). If yes, the external sphincter relaxes, abdominal and diaphragm muscles contract, and feces are expelled.

💡 Mnemonic: Large intestine = "water-recovery + storage." Flora make "K and B" vitamins. Sphincters (just like the bladder): "Internal = Involuntary, External = your Effort (voluntary)." Reflex: "Stretch → signal → brain says go or hold."
⚠️ Common confusion: The large intestine does little digestion — its main jobs are water absorption, vitamin production by flora, and storage. Most nutrient absorption already happened in the small intestine. The external sphincter is the one you voluntarily control.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What are the main functions of the large intestine?
    Show answer
    Absorb remaining water, house bacterial flora (which make vitamin K and B vitamins), and temporarily store feces before elimination.
  2. What do the gut bacterial flora produce?
    Show answer
    Vitamin K and several B vitamins, plus gas (flatus) from fermentation.
  3. Which anal sphincter is voluntary?
    Show answer
    The external anal sphincter (skeletal muscle).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The primary function of the large intestine is to:
    A) Digest protein · B) Absorb water & form feces · C) Emulsify fat · D) Secrete bile
    Show answer
    B. Absorb remaining water and compact feces.
  2. Gut bacterial flora synthesize:
    A) Vitamin C · B) Vitamin K and B vitamins · C) Insulin · D) Bile salts
    Show answer
    B. Vitamin K and B vitamins.
  3. The defecation reflex is initiated by:
    A) Stretching of the rectal wall · B) Eating fat · C) Gastrin release · D) The cephalic phase
    Show answer
    A. Stretching of the rectal wall.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient on long-term broad-spectrum antibiotics develops a bleeding tendency (low vitamin K). Using the large intestine's flora, explain the link.
Show answer
Normal gut bacterial flora produce vitamin K, which the body needs to make clotting factors. Broad-spectrum antibiotics kill off much of this flora, reducing vitamin K synthesis and impairing clotting — leading to a bleeding tendency.

🎯 Exam Cheat Sheet

🧴 URINARY SYSTEM (Ch. 23)
  • Kidney functions: filter blood, balance water/ions/pH, excrete wastes, make renin (BP) & EPO (RBCs), activate vitamin D.
  • Urine flow: papilla → minor calyx → major calyx → renal pelvis → ureter. Kidneys are retroperitoneal.
  • Blood: afferent arteriole → glomerulus → efferent arteriole (fed AND drained by arterioles).
  • Nephron = renal corpuscle (glomerulus + Bowman's capsule) + renal tubule (PCT → loop → DCT → collecting duct). Juxtamedullary nephrons (15%) concentrate urine.
  • 3 urine-forming steps: filtration (passive, blood→filtrate), reabsorption (99%, filtrate→blood), secretion (blood→filtrate).
  • GFR normal 120–125 mL/min; <60 (3 mo) = CKD; <15 = end-stage.
  • JGA: macula densa senses NaCl; granular cells sense BP + make renin. RAAS raises BP.
  • ADH = conserve water (concentrated urine when dehydrated). Medullary gradient 300→1200 mOsm.
  • Urine: 95% water; urea (biggest waste); yellow from urochrome. Shouldn't contain glucose, protein, blood, or ketones.
  • Sphincters: internal = involuntary (smooth); external = voluntary (skeletal). Female urethra short → more UTIs.
🫊 DIGESTIVE SYSTEM (Ch. 25)
  • Alimentary canal (food passes through) vs accessory organs (teeth, tongue, salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, pancreas).
  • Mouth: salivary amylase starts carbs; bolus forms. Saliva controlled by parasympathetic NS.
  • Swallowing: buccal phase (voluntary) → pharyngeal-esophageal (involuntary, peristalsis).
  • Stomach cells: Parietal = HCl + intrinsic factor (B₁₂); Chief = pepsinogen→pepsin (protein). Bolus → chyme.
  • 3 secretion phases: Cephalic (head/smell) → Gastric (food in stomach, ~2/3 of juice, gastrin) → Intestinal (enterogastric reflex INHIBITS).
  • Gastric emptying ~4 hr; carbs fast, fats slowest (~6 hr).
  • Small intestine (Duodenum, Jejunum, Ileum) = main absorption; segmentation mixes.
  • Liver makes bile (emulsifies fat); gallbladder stores it; CCK → gallbladder contracts.
  • Pancreas: acinar (enzymes + bicarbonate neutralizes acid); islets (β=insulin, α=glucagon).
  • Digestion starts: carbs=mouth, protein=stomach, fat=small intestine. Enzymes: amylase=carb, protease/pepsin/trypsin=protein, lipase=fat, nuclease=nucleic acid.
  • Absorption: sugars/amino acids → blood; fats → lacteals (lymph).
  • Large intestine: absorbs water, flora make vitamin K + B, stores feces. Internal anal sphincter involuntary, external voluntary.

📚 Glossary

Urinary Terms
  • Nephron — structural/functional unit of the kidney.
  • Glomerulus — capillary tuft where filtration occurs.
  • Bowman's (glomerular) capsule — cup that collects filtrate.
  • Filtrate — plasma minus proteins/cells, formed by filtration.
  • GFR — glomerular filtration rate (normal 120–125 mL/min).
  • Reabsorption / Secretion — filtrate→blood / blood→filtrate.
  • JGA — juxtaglomerular apparatus (regulates filtration & BP).
  • Renin / RAAS — enzyme/system that raises blood pressure.
  • ADH — antidiuretic hormone; conserves water.
  • Urea — main nitrogenous waste (from protein).
  • Micturition — urination/voiding.
  • Detrusor — bladder wall muscle; trigone — bladder triangle of 3 openings.
Digestive Terms
  • Alimentary canal — the continuous GI tube food passes through.
  • Bolus / Chyme — chewed food ball / creamy stomach mixture.
  • Mastication / Deglutition — chewing / swallowing.
  • Peristalsis / Segmentation — propulsion waves / mixing contractions.
  • Amylase / Pepsin / Lipase / Nuclease — carb / protein / fat / nucleic-acid enzymes.
  • Parietal cells — make HCl + intrinsic factor; chief cells — make pepsinogen.
  • Intrinsic factor — needed for vitamin B₁₂ absorption.
  • Gastrin / Secretin / CCK — gut hormones (acid; bicarbonate; bile + enzymes).
  • Bile — liver-made fat emulsifier; stored in the gallbladder.
  • Bilirubin / Stercobilin — bile pigment / brown fecal pigment.
  • Lacteal — lymphatic capillary that absorbs digested fat.
  • Bacterial flora — gut bacteria that make vitamin K and B vitamins.

🎯 Final Study Tips

  • Trace the two pathways end to end: follow a drop of filtrate from glomerulus to urine, and follow a bite of food from mouth to feces. If you can narrate both, you know the unit.
  • Memorize the ordered lists: urine flow (papilla→calyces→pelvis→ureter), tubule (PCT→loop→DCT), small intestine (DJI), and the 3 gastric phases (cephalic, gastric, intestinal).
  • Lock in the "who makes what" pairs: parietal=HCl/intrinsic factor, chief=pepsinogen; macula densa=salt, granular cells=renin; liver=bile, gallbladder=stores it; β=insulin, α=glucagon.
  • Know the direction words: reabsorption (back to blood) vs secretion (into filtrate); ADH conserves water; fats go to lymph (lacteals), sugars/amino acids to blood.
  • Use the clinical scenarios — the exam loves applications (CKD anemia, pernicious anemia, gallbladder removal, antibiotics & vitamin K).
  • Quiz with the hidden answers — active recall beats rereading. Use the cheat sheet the night before AND the morning of the exam.

ⓘ What you'll learn in this unit

This unit covers the body's framework and its movers. The skeletal system (206 bones) provides support, protection, movement, blood-cell formation, and mineral storage — you'll learn bone markings and the major bones of the axial and appendicular skeleton. Muscle tissue then powers movement — you'll learn the three muscle types, how a skeletal muscle is built, and the physiology of how it contracts (the sliding filament model, the neuromuscular junction, and energy metabolism).

🦴 The Big Picture: Framework & Movers

TOPIC 1 🦴 Skeletal Overview & Bone Markings MUST KNOW▼

Two Divisions of the Skeleton (206 bones total)

DivisionBones & what's included
Axial (80 bones)The body's central axis: skull, vertebral column, sternum & ribs (head, neck, torso).
Appendicular (126 bones)The appendages: upper & lower limbs plus the shoulder (pectoral) and pelvic girdles.

Bone Markings — the Vocabulary

Bones have surface features ("markings") that fall into three groups: projections (sticking out), depressions/openings (holes & dents), and articulating surfaces (where bones meet).

MarkingMeaning
ForamenRound hole for blood vessels/nerves.
FossaDepression that another bone fits into (forms a joint).
MeatusCanal/tube-like opening (e.g., external auditory meatus).
SinusCavity within a bone.
Condyle / HeadRounded projections that form joints (head = end of a long bone).
Trochanter / Tuberosity / Tubercle / Epicondyle / Spine / CrestProjections where muscles attach (trochanter = large bump, tuberosity = small bump, epicondyle = bump above a condyle).
FacetFlat joint surface (common in the spine).
💡 Mnemonic: "Axial = Axis (center line: skull, spine, ribs); Appendicular = Appendages (limbs)." Holes: Foramen = hole, Fossa = shallow dish, Meatus = canal. Bumps for muscles: "Tro-Tube-Tuber-Epi" all = muscle attachment sites.
⚠️ Common confusion: A foramen is a hole (passage), while a fossa is a depression (a dish another bone sits in). A condyle/head forms a joint; a trochanter/tuberosity is for muscle attachment (not a joint).

❓ Practice Questions

  1. How many bones are in the axial vs appendicular skeleton?
    Show answer
    Axial = 80, appendicular = 126 (206 total).
  2. What's the difference between a foramen and a fossa?
    Show answer
    A foramen is a round hole for vessels/nerves; a fossa is a depression that another bone fits into to form a joint.
  3. Name two bone markings where muscles attach.
    Show answer
    Any two of: trochanter, tuberosity, tubercle, epicondyle, spine, crest.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The skull, vertebrae, and ribs belong to the:
    A) Appendicular skeleton · B) Axial skeleton · C) Pectoral girdle · D) Pelvic girdle
    Show answer
    B. Axial skeleton.
  2. A large rounded projection that fits into another bone to form a joint is a:
    A) Foramen · B) Tuberosity · C) Head · D) Crest
    Show answer
    C. Head (a rounded articular end of a long bone).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
On an X-ray report, a radiologist notes a fracture through the "greater trochanter of the femur." Using bone-marking vocabulary, what kind of structure was broken and what attaches there?
Show answer
A trochanter is a large projection for muscle attachment (only the femur has them). The greater trochanter is a key attachment site for hip muscles, so a fracture there affects hip movement and stability.

TOPIC 2 💀 The Skull: Cranial Bones MUST KNOW▼

The skull has 28 bones: 8 cranial (surround the brain), 14 facial, plus 6 ear bones. The 8 cranial bones form the protective braincase.

The 8 Cranial Bones & Key Markings

BoneLocation & key markings
Frontal (1)Forehead, roofs of orbits; frontal sinuses, supraorbital foramen.
Parietal (2)Superior sides/roof of cranium.
Temporal (2)Lateral walls; mastoid process, external auditory meatus, styloid process, mandibular fossa, zygomatic process.
Occipital (1)Posterior & base; foramen magnum (spinal cord passes here), occipital condyles.
Sphenoid (1)"Keystone of the cranium" (anchors all others); sella turcica (holds pituitary gland), optic foramen, sphenoid sinuses.
Ethmoid (1)Between nose & sphenoid; cribriform plate (olfactory nerve passes), crista galli, perpendicular plate (nasal septum), superior/middle conchae.
💡 Mnemonic: 8 cranial bones = "PEST OF 6": Parietal(2), Ethmoid, Sphenoid, Temporal(2), Occipital, Frontal. Foramen Magnum = "Magnum = big hole" (spinal cord). Sella turcica = "Turkish saddle" holds the pituitary. Sphenoid = keystone (touches all the others).
⚠️ Common confusion: The sphenoid is the "keystone of the cranium" (anchors cranial bones); the maxilla is the "keystone of the face." The cribriform plate (ethmoid) transmits the smell (olfactory) nerve; the foramen magnum (occipital) transmits the spinal cord.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. Which cranial bone contains the foramen magnum, and what passes through it?
    Show answer
    The occipital bone; the spinal cord passes through the foramen magnum.
  2. Which bone is the "keystone of the cranium," and what gland sits in its sella turcica?
    Show answer
    The sphenoid bone; the pituitary gland sits in the sella turcica.
  3. What structure passes through the cribriform plate?
    Show answer
    The olfactory nerve (smell), via tiny holes in the ethmoid bone's cribriform plate.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The mastoid process and external auditory meatus are found on the:
    A) Occipital · B) Temporal · C) Sphenoid · D) Frontal bone
    Show answer
    B. Temporal bone.
  2. How many cranial bones are there?
    A) 6 · B) 8 · C) 14 · D) 22
    Show answer
    B. Eight (frontal, 2 parietal, 2 temporal, occipital, sphenoid, ethmoid).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with a head injury loses their sense of smell (anosmia). Which cranial bone and structure might be damaged, and why?
Show answer
The ethmoid bone's cribriform plate, which the olfactory nerve fibers pass through. A blow to the head can shear these delicate fibers as they cross the plate, causing loss of smell.

TOPIC 3 😶 Facial Bones, Sutures & Fontanelles ▼

The 14 Facial Bones

BoneNotes
Maxillae (2)Upper jaw; "keystone of the face"; form most of the hard palate (palatine process); maxillary sinuses (largest).
Mandible (1)Lower jaw; largest/strongest facial bone; the only movable skull bone. Mandibular condyle forms the jaw joint.
Zygomatic (2)Cheekbones.
Nasal (2)Bridge of the nose.
Lacrimal (2)Medial walls of orbits (near tear ducts).
Palatine (2)Posterior hard palate.
Inferior conchae (2)Lower nasal "ledges" (warm/humidify air).
Vomer (1)Lower part of the nasal septum.

The hyoid bone (in the neck) is unique: it's the only bone that doesn't form a joint with another bone — it's slung by ligaments and anchors tongue muscles. The ear ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes × 2) are tiny bones in the temporal bones.

Sutures (immovable skull joints)

  • Coronal — frontal ↔ parietals. Sagittal — between the two parietals. Lambdoid — parietals ↔ occipital. Squamous — temporal ↔ parietal.

Fontanelles (infant "soft spots")

Areas where ossification is incomplete at birth. They let the skull compress during childbirth and allow the brain to grow. They later close as bones meet to form sutures.

💡 Mnemonic: "Maxilla = keystone of the face; Mandible = only movable skull bone." Hyoid = "no joint" floater. Ear bones = "MIS" (Malleus, Incus, Stapes — stapes is the smallest bone in the body). Fontanelles = "soft spots = squeeze through birth + room to grow."
⚠️ Common confusion: The maxilla is the keystone of the FACE (vs sphenoid = keystone of the cranium). The hyoid is the one bone that articulates with NO other bone. Sutures are immovable; the only movable skull joint is the temporomandibular joint (mandible).

❓ Practice Questions

  1. Which facial bone is the largest and the only movable one?
    Show answer
    The mandible (lower jaw).
  2. What's unique about the hyoid bone?
    Show answer
    It's the only bone that doesn't form a joint with any other bone (held by ligaments/muscles).
  3. What is the purpose of fontanelles?
    Show answer
    They allow skull compression during birth and room for brain growth; they later ossify into sutures.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The "keystone of the face" is the:
    A) Mandible · B) Maxilla · C) Zygomatic · D) Vomer
    Show answer
    B. Maxilla.
  2. The suture between the two parietal bones is the:
    A) Coronal · B) Sagittal · C) Lambdoid · D) Squamous
    Show answer
    B. Sagittal suture.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A pediatrician checks a newborn's "soft spot" and explains it should close by about 18–24 months. Why are fontanelles important at birth, and what happens if they close too early?
Show answer
Fontanelles let the skull bones overlap/compress during delivery and give the rapidly growing brain room to expand. Premature closure (craniosynostosis) can restrict brain growth and raise intracranial pressure, so it's monitored and sometimes surgically corrected.

TOPIC 4 🧊 Vertebral Column & Thoracic Cage MUST KNOW▼

The Vertebral Column (26 bones)

RegionNumber & location
Cervical7 — neck. C1 = atlas (holds up the head), C2 = axis (has the dens that the atlas pivots on).
Thoracic12 — chest; articulate with the ribs.
Lumbar5 — lower back (largest, weight-bearing).
Sacrum5 fused → 1 wedge-shaped bone.
Coccyx4–5 fused → tailbone.

Each vertebra has a body (weight-bearing), a spinous process & transverse processes (muscle attachment), and a vertebral foramen — stacked together these form the spinal canal that houses the spinal cord.

The Thoracic Cage

  • Sternum (breastbone): manubrium (top), body (middle), xiphoid process (bottom tip).
  • Ribs: 12 pairs. 7 pairs "true ribs" (attach directly to sternum via costal cartilage). 5 pairs "false ribs" — the upper 3 attach indirectly (to rib 7's cartilage), the last 2 are "floating ribs" (no sternal attachment). All ribs attach to the thoracic vertebrae posteriorly.
💡 Mnemonic: Vertebrae counts = "breakfast 7, lunch 12, dinner 5" (Cervical 7, Thoracic 12, Lumbar 5). Atlas holds the world (head); Axis lets you say "no" (rotation on the dens). Ribs: "True 7, False 5 (3 share + 2 float)."
⚠️ Common confusion: C1 = atlas (nods "yes"), C2 = axis (rotates "no" on the dens). Floating ribs (11 & 12) attach to NO part of the sternum, while the other false ribs attach indirectly. The sacrum and coccyx are fused vertebrae.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. How many vertebrae are in each region (cervical, thoracic, lumbar)?
    Show answer
    Cervical 7, thoracic 12, lumbar 5 (plus sacrum and coccyx).
  2. What's special about C1 and C2?
    Show answer
    C1 = atlas (supports the skull, "yes" nod); C2 = axis with the dens, which the atlas rotates around ("no").
  3. Which ribs are "floating," and why?
    Show answer
    Pairs 11 and 12 — they don't attach to the sternum at all (only to the thoracic vertebrae).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The lowest tip of the sternum is the:
    A) Manubrium · B) Body · C) Xiphoid process · D) Costal cartilage
    Show answer
    C. Xiphoid process.
  2. How many pairs of true ribs are there?
    A) 5 · B) 7 · C) 10 · D) 12
    Show answer
    B. Seven pairs (attach directly to the sternum).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
During CPR training, students are taught to place their hands on the sternum but avoid the very bottom tip. Which part should they avoid, and why?
Show answer
The xiphoid process — it's small, partly cartilaginous, and can break off and injure the liver or diaphragm if compressed. Compressions are centered on the body of the sternum instead.
☑ Self-check: Can you give the vertebral counts, explain atlas vs axis, name the 3 sternum parts, and classify true/false/floating ribs? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 5 👋 Upper Limb & Shoulder Girdle MUST KNOW▼

Shoulder (Pectoral) Girdle

Clavicle (collarbone) + scapula (shoulder blade) = the shoulder girdle, which anchors the arm to the axial skeleton. The scapula's glenoid cavity is the shallow socket that holds the humeral head (the shoulder joint).

Bones of the Upper Limb (proximal → distal)

BoneKey facts & markings
HumerusUpper arm; head fits the glenoid cavity; distal capitulum & trochlea, medial/lateral epicondyles.
RadiusForearm, thumb side; disk-shaped head; styloid process (distal/lateral).
UlnaForearm, pinky side; olecranon process (the elbow); trochlear notch.
Carpals (8)Wrist bones.
Metacarpals (5)Palm; their heads = the knuckles.
Phalanges (14)Fingers (3 each) & thumb (2).
💡 Mnemonic: Forearm: "Radius = thumb side (R for 'Rotate', thumb); Ulna = pinky side (and the olecranon = your elbow)." Shoulder girdle = "Clavicle + Scapula." Hand order: "Carpals → Metacarpals → Phalanges" (wrist → palm → fingers).
⚠️ Common confusion: The radius is on the thumb side and the ulna on the pinky side (in anatomical position, palms forward). The olecranon (ulna) is the bony point of the elbow. The glenoid cavity is on the scapula, not the humerus.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What two bones make up the shoulder girdle?
    Show answer
    The clavicle and the scapula.
  2. Which forearm bone is on the thumb side, and which on the pinky side?
    Show answer
    Radius = thumb side; ulna = pinky side.
  3. What is the bony point of the elbow called, and which bone is it on?
    Show answer
    The olecranon process, on the ulna.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The head of the humerus articulates with the:
    A) Acetabulum · B) Glenoid cavity · C) Olecranon · D) Trochlea
    Show answer
    B. Glenoid cavity of the scapula.
  2. The bones of the palm are the:
    A) Carpals · B) Metacarpals · C) Phalanges · D) Tarsals
    Show answer
    B. Metacarpals.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A child falls on an outstretched hand and the doctor says the radius is broken near the wrist (a common "FOOSH" injury). On which side of the forearm is the radius, and why is it commonly involved?
Show answer
The radius is on the thumb side. In a fall on an outstretched hand (FOOSH), force travels up the thumb-side of the forearm to the distal radius, making distal radius fractures (like a Colles' fracture) very common.

TOPIC 6 🦵 Lower Limb & Pelvic Girdle MUST KNOW▼

Pelvic Girdle

Each hip (coxal) bone — the broadest bone in the body — is three fused bones: ilium (largest, upper flare), ischium (strongest, lower), and pubis (anterior). The acetabulum is the deep hip socket that holds the femoral head. Two coxal bones + sacrum + coccyx = the bony pelvis (shape differs in males vs females).

Bones of the Lower Limb (proximal → distal)

BoneKey facts & markings
FemurLongest, strongest bone; head fits the acetabulum; greater & lesser trochanters, medial/lateral condyles & epicondyles.
PatellaKneecap.
TibiaShin bone (larger, medial, weight-bearing); tibial tuberosity; medial malleolus (inner ankle bump).
FibulaThin lateral leg bone (not weight-bearing); lateral malleolus (outer ankle bump).
Tarsals (7)Ankle/heel; calcaneus (heel bone), talus (most proximal).
Metatarsals (5) & Phalanges (14)Foot & toes (3 per toe except big toe = 2).
💡 Mnemonic: Hip bones = "ILIum (Ilium up top), ISChium (Sit on it), PUBis (front)." Leg: "Tibia = Thick/inner (shin); Fibula = Fine/outer." Ankle bumps: "Medial Malleolus = tibia (inner); Lateral Malleolus = fibula (outer)." Calcaneus = heel.
⚠️ Common confusion: The tibia is the big medial weight-bearing shin bone; the fibula is the thin lateral one (you can break it and still walk). Medial malleolus = tibia, lateral malleolus = fibula. The femoral head fits the acetabulum (hip), mirroring the humeral head in the glenoid (shoulder).

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What are the three fused bones of the coxal (hip) bone?
    Show answer
    Ilium, ischium, and pubis.
  2. Which leg bone bears weight, and which ankle bump belongs to it?
    Show answer
    The tibia bears weight; its medial malleolus forms the inner ankle bump.
  3. What is the heel bone called?
    Show answer
    The calcaneus.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The femoral head fits into the:
    A) Glenoid cavity · B) Acetabulum · C) Olecranon fossa · D) Obturator foramen
    Show answer
    B. Acetabulum of the coxal bone.
  2. The lateral malleolus is part of the:
    A) Tibia · B) Femur · C) Fibula · D) Talus
    Show answer
    C. Fibula.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
An athlete fractures their fibula but is told they can still bear some weight, whereas a tibia fracture would not allow it. Why the difference?
Show answer
The tibia is the weight-bearing bone of the lower leg; the fibula is thin and mainly for muscle attachment and ankle stability, bearing little weight. So an isolated fibula fracture often still allows limited weight-bearing, while a tibia fracture does not.

TOPIC 7 💪 Muscle Types & Characteristics MUST KNOW▼

The 3 Types of Muscle Tissue

TypeControlNucleiStriations
SkeletalVoluntaryMultinucleatedStriated
CardiacInvoluntaryUninucleatedStriated
SmoothInvoluntaryUninucleatedNOT striated

Only skeletal muscle is voluntary. Only smooth muscle lacks striations. Only skeletal is multinucleated.

The 4 Special Characteristics of Muscle

PropertyMeaning
Excitability (responsiveness)Can receive and respond to a stimulus.
ContractilityCan shorten when stimulated.
ExtensibilityCan be stretched.
ElasticityCan recoil back to resting length.

Muscle Functions

Movement of bones & fluids, maintaining posture/position, stabilizing joints, and generating heat (especially skeletal muscle — why you shiver when cold).

💡 Mnemonic: The 4 properties = "Every Cat Eats Eggs" (Excitability, Contractility, Extensibility, Elasticity). Types: "Skeletal = Striated & Steered (voluntary); Smooth = Smooth (no stripes) & aSleep (involuntary); Cardiac = striated but auto."
⚠️ Common confusion: Cardiac muscle is striated but involuntary — striation does NOT equal voluntary. Only skeletal muscle is both striated AND voluntary AND multinucleated. Smooth muscle is the only one without stripes.

TOPIC 8 🧸 Skeletal Muscle Structure MUST KNOW▼

Connective Tissue Sheaths (outside → inside)

SheathWraps
EpimysiumThe entire muscle ("epi" = upon/outside).
PerimysiumA fascicle (bundle of muscle fibers) ("peri" = around).
EndomysiumEach individual muscle fiber ("endo" = within).

How Muscles Attach to Bone

  • Direct attachment — epimysium fuses straight to the bone's periosteum.
  • Indirect attachment — the connective tissue extends beyond the muscle as a ropelike tendon or sheet-like aponeurosis (more common, e.g., biceps).

Inside a Muscle Fiber (cell)

A skeletal muscle fiber is a long, multinucleated cell packed with: myofibrils (the contractile rods made of sarcomeres), sarcoplasmic reticulum (stores Ca²⁺), T tubules (carry the signal inward), many mitochondria, glycosomes (glycogen storage), and myoglobin (stores O&sub2;). Its cell membrane is the sarcolemma. The repeating dark A bands and light I bands create the striped (striated) look.

💡 Mnemonic: Sheaths outside-in: "Epi (Entire), Peri (Pack/fascicle), Endo (Each fiber)." Muscle-cell parts use "sarco-" (= flesh): sarcolemma (membrane), sarcoplasm, sarcoplasmic reticulum. Myoglobin = O&sub2; storage (myo + globin like hemoglobin).
⚠️ Common confusion: Match the sheath to its level: Epimysium = whole muscle, Perimysium = fascicle, Endomysium = single fiber. A tendon connects muscle to bone (a ligament connects bone to bone — don't mix them up).

❓ Practice Questions

  1. Name the 3 connective tissue sheaths from outside to inside and what each wraps.
    Show answer
    Epimysium (whole muscle), perimysium (fascicle), endomysium (individual fiber).
  2. What's the difference between a tendon and an aponeurosis?
    Show answer
    Both attach muscle indirectly; a tendon is ropelike/cordlike, an aponeurosis is a flat sheet.
  3. What does myoglobin do in a muscle fiber?
    Show answer
    Stores oxygen inside the muscle cell.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The connective tissue surrounding a fascicle is the:
    A) Epimysium · B) Perimysium · C) Endomysium · D) Sarcolemma
    Show answer
    B. Perimysium.
  2. The muscle cell membrane is called the:
    A) Sarcomere · B) Sarcolemma · C) Myofibril · D) T tubule
    Show answer
    B. Sarcolemma.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
An athlete "tears their hamstring," damaging the tendon where the muscle attaches to bone. Using muscle structure, explain what a tendon is and why this limits movement.
Show answer
A tendon is the ropelike connective-tissue extension (continuous with the muscle's sheaths) that anchors muscle to bone. It transmits the muscle's pulling force to the bone, so a torn tendon breaks that connection — the muscle can contract but can't effectively move the joint, causing weakness and pain.

TOPIC 9 🧬 Sarcomere & Sliding Filament Model MUST KNOW▼

The Sarcomere — the Contractile Unit

The sarcomere is the smallest functional (contractile) unit of a muscle fiber — the region between two Z discs. It's built from two myofilaments:

FilamentMade of & features
Thick (myosin)Has myosin heads with binding sites for actin and ATP; runs the length of the A band.
Thin (actin)Has binding sites for myosin; regulated by tropomyosin & troponin; spans the I band.

Key landmarks: Z disc (anchors thin filaments, the sarcomere boundary), A band (dark, full length of thick filament), I band (light, thin filament only), H zone (center, thick only — no overlap), M line (holds thick filaments together).

The Sliding Filament Model

The filaments do NOT shorten — the myosin heads grab actin, pull (power stroke), release, and re-grab, sliding the thin filaments toward the center (M line). As they slide, the H zone and I band shrink, the Z discs pull closer, and the whole muscle shortens. (The A band stays the same width.)

💡 Mnemonic: "Thick = Myosin (both have a 'y'? no — thick = m for myosin/'meaty'); Thin = Actin." Bands: "A band = dArk (thick); I band = lIght (thin)." Sliding filament: "filaments slide, they don't shrink" — the H zone and I band disappear, A band stays.
⚠️ Common confusion: In contraction the filaments themselves don't get shorter — they slide past each other, so the sarcomere shortens. The A band width never changes; it's the I band and H zone that narrow. Thick = myosin, thin = actin.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What is the sarcomere, and what marks its boundaries?
    Show answer
    The smallest contractile unit of a muscle fiber; it's the region between two Z discs.
  2. Which filament is thick and which is thin?
    Show answer
    Thick = myosin (with heads); thin = actin (regulated by troponin/tropomyosin).
  3. During contraction, do the filaments shorten? What happens to the H zone and I band?
    Show answer
    No — the filaments slide past each other. The H zone and I band shorten/disappear while the A band stays the same.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The thick filament is made of:
    A) Actin · B) Myosin · C) Troponin · D) Tropomyosin
    Show answer
    B. Myosin.
  2. During contraction, which stays the same width?
    A) I band · B) H zone · C) A band · D) Sarcomere
    Show answer
    C. The A band (thick filament length doesn't change).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
In severe muscle-stretch injuries, sarcomeres are pulled so far apart the thick and thin filaments barely overlap, and the muscle generates little force. Using the sliding filament model, explain why overlap matters.
Show answer
Force comes from myosin heads binding actin and pulling. If the sarcomere is overstretched, there's little overlap, so few cross-bridges can form and little force is produced. (Maximum tension occurs near resting length, ~80–120%, where overlap is optimal.)
☑ Self-check: Can you define the sarcomere, identify thick vs thin filaments and the bands, and explain that filaments slide (not shrink)? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 10 ⚡ Neuromuscular Junction & E-C Coupling MUST KNOW▼

For a skeletal muscle to contract, two things must happen: a nerve must signal it (at the neuromuscular junction), and that signal must trigger calcium release inside the fiber (excitation-contraction coupling).

The Neuromuscular Junction (NMJ)

Skeletal muscle is stimulated by somatic motor neurons. Each axon ending meets a single muscle fiber at the NMJ. The sequence:

Excitation-Contraction Coupling

The link between the electrical signal and the mechanical contraction:

  1. The action potential spreads along the sarcolemma and dives in via the T tubules.
  2. This triggers the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release Ca²⁺ — the final trigger for contraction.
  3. Ca²⁺ binds troponin, which moves tropomyosin off the actin binding sites, so myosin heads can grab actin → the power stroke (sliding filament).
  4. When stimulation stops, Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the SR and the muscle relaxes.

The Motor Unit

A motor unit = one motor neuron + all the muscle fibers it controls. Small motor units (few fibers) allow fine control (eyes, fingers); large motor units (hundreds of fibers) power big movements (thighs, hips). Units fire asynchronously (taking turns) to delay fatigue.

💡 Mnemonic: NMJ chemical = ACh (acetylcholine). "Calcium is the final trigger" — no Ca²⁺, no contraction. Ca²⁺ "uncovers actin" (moves troponin/tropomyosin). Small motor units = fine control; big units = brute force.
⚠️ Common confusion: Acetylcholine (ACh) is the NMJ neurotransmitter; the final trigger inside the cell is Ca²⁺ from the SR. Calcium doesn't bind actin directly — it binds troponin, which then moves tropomyosin to expose actin's binding sites.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What neurotransmitter is released at the neuromuscular junction?
    Show answer
    Acetylcholine (ACh).
  2. What is the "final trigger" for contraction, and where is it released from?
    Show answer
    A rise in intracellular Ca²⁺, released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
  3. What is a motor unit, and which has smaller units — eye muscles or thigh muscles?
    Show answer
    A motor neuron plus all fibers it controls; eye muscles have small motor units (for fine control).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Calcium triggers contraction by binding:
    A) Actin · B) Myosin · C) Troponin · D) ATP
    Show answer
    C. Troponin (which moves tropomyosin off actin).
  2. Muscle fibers controlled by one motor neuron make up a:
    A) Sarcomere · B) Fascicle · C) Motor unit · D) Myofibril

🩺 Clinical Scenario
The toxin in botulism blocks acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, causing paralysis. Using NMJ physiology, explain why blocking ACh stops muscle contraction.
Show answer
ACh is the signal that crosses the NMJ to depolarize the muscle's sarcolemma. Without ACh release, no action potential spreads into the fiber, the SR never releases Ca²⁺, and the myosin-actin cross-bridges can't form — so the muscle can't contract, causing flaccid paralysis.

TOPIC 11 🏋 Contractions & Muscle Responses MUST KNOW▼

Isometric vs. Isotonic Contractions

TypeWhat happensExample
IsometricTension rises but length doesn't change (no movement).Holding a plank; pushing a wall.
Isotonic — ConcentricMuscle shortens to move a load.Lifting a dumbbell up (biceps curl).
Isotonic — EccentricMuscle lengthens while contracting (controls a load).Lowering the dumbbell slowly.

The Muscle Twitch (3 phases)

A twitch is the response to a single brief stimulus (recorded as a myogram):

  1. Latent period — excitation-contraction coupling (no force yet).
  2. Contraction phase — cross-bridges form, tension rises.
  3. Relaxation phase — Ca²⁺ pumped back into SR, tension falls to zero.

Grading Muscle Force (how contractions get stronger)

  • Recruitment (multiple motor unit summation) — activating more motor units = more force.
  • Wave (temporal) summation — stimulating again before relaxation adds twitches together (more Ca²⁺ released).
  • Tetanus — stimuli so rapid that twitches fuse into one smooth, sustained, maximal contraction.
  • Treppe — the "staircase": warmed-up muscle contracts a bit more strongly with each of the first few stimuli.
💡 Mnemonic: "Isometric = same metric (length); Isotonic = same tone (tension), length changes." Concentric = "Comes together (shortens)"; Eccentric = "Extends (lengthens)." Twitch = "Late-Contract-Relax." More force = "recruit more units + summate faster → tetanus."
⚠️ Common confusion: Isometric = no length change (you still generate tension, just no movement). Eccentric contractions still "contract" even though the muscle is lengthening (it's resisting/controlling a load). Tetanus here is the smooth fused contraction — not the disease.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. Differentiate isometric from isotonic contractions.
    Show answer
    Isometric = tension increases but length stays the same (no movement); isotonic = the muscle changes length (concentric = shortens, eccentric = lengthens).
  2. Name the 3 phases of a muscle twitch.
    Show answer
    Latent period, contraction phase, relaxation phase.
  3. What is recruitment?
    Show answer
    Increasing contraction force by activating more motor units.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Lowering a heavy weight slowly is what kind of contraction?
    A) Isometric · B) Concentric · C) Eccentric · D) Tetanic
    Show answer
    C. Eccentric (the muscle lengthens while contracting).
  2. A smooth, sustained, maximal contraction from rapid stimulation is:
    A) A twitch · B) Treppe · C) Tetanus · D) Latent period
    Show answer
    C. Tetanus (fused contraction).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
After a hard downhill hike (lots of lowering/braking with the legs), a person is very sore the next day. Which contraction type dominates downhill walking, and why is it linked to soreness?
Show answer
Eccentric contractions — the quadriceps lengthen under load to control descent. Eccentric work causes more micro-damage to muscle fibers than concentric work, which is why downhill/braking activity often produces the most delayed-onset muscle soreness.

TOPIC 12 ⚡ Muscle Metabolism & Fiber Types MUST KNOW▼

ATP is the only fuel used directly for contraction — but the cell only stores ~4–6 seconds' worth, so it must constantly regenerate it three ways.

3 Ways to Regenerate ATP

MethodSpeed & duration
1. Creatine phosphateDirect phosphorylation of ADP. Fastest, but only ~15 seconds of energy (short bursts).
2. Anaerobic glycolysisNo oxygen needed; faster than aerobic but yields little ATP and produces lactic acid. For moderate, short activity.
3. Aerobic respirationNeeds oxygen; slow but huge ATP yield. Supplies ~95% of ATP for rest/moderate activity (endurance).

Oxygen debt: after intense exercise, extra O&sub2; is needed to restore ATP/creatine phosphate and convert lactic acid back to glucose/glycogen (in the liver). Muscle fatigue happens when ATP production can't keep up with use (plus ionic imbalances).

Muscle Fiber Types

FiberFeatures & use
Type I (slow oxidative, red)Lots of mitochondria, myoglobin, capillaries; aerobic; fatigue-resistant — endurance (marathons, posture).
Type IIA (fast oxidative, pink)Fast + fairly fatigue-resistant; sustained power (soccer, basketball).
Type IIB/IIx (fast glycolytic, white)Few mitochondria; anaerobic; powerful but fatigue quickly — short bursts (sprints, max lifts).
💡 Mnemonic: Energy order = "CP first (instant), Glycolysis next (anaerobic), Aerobic for the long haul." Fibers: "Type I = I run far (slow, red, endurance); Type II = II fast (white, sprints, tire fast)." Red = rich in myoglobin/O&sub2;.
⚠️ Common confusion: Creatine phosphate is the fastest energy source but lasts only seconds; aerobic respiration is slowest but makes the most ATP. Type I = slow, red, endurance; Type II = fast, white, fatigue quickly — don't reverse them.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What is the only molecule used directly to power contraction, and how long do stored amounts last?
    Show answer
    ATP; stored ATP lasts only about 4–6 seconds.
  2. Which ATP source is fastest, and which produces the most ATP?
    Show answer
    Creatine phosphate is fastest (~15 s); aerobic respiration produces the most ATP (for endurance).
  3. Which fiber type suits a marathon runner vs a sprinter?
    Show answer
    Type I (slow, fatigue-resistant) for the marathoner; Type II (fast, powerful, fatigues fast) for the sprinter.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The fastest source of ATP for a short burst is:
    A) Aerobic respiration · B) Anaerobic glycolysis · C) Creatine phosphate · D) Fat oxidation
    Show answer
    C. Creatine phosphate.
  2. Type I (slow oxidative) fibers are best for:
    A) Sprinting · B) Endurance · C) Max lifts · D) Jumping
    Show answer
    B. Endurance activities.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
After sprinting all-out, a runner keeps breathing hard for several minutes even though they've stopped. Using muscle metabolism, explain this "oxygen debt."
Show answer
During the sprint, the runner relied on anaerobic glycolysis (producing lactic acid) and used up ATP and creatine phosphate. Afterward, the body needs extra oxygen to regenerate ATP/creatine phosphate and to convert lactic acid back to glucose/glycogen in the liver — so heavy breathing continues to "repay" the oxygen debt.

🎯 Exam Cheat Sheet

🦴 SKELETAL SYSTEM (Ch. 8)
  • 206 bones: Axial 80 (skull, spine, ribs) + Appendicular 126 (limbs, girdles).
  • Markings: foramen = hole, fossa = depression, meatus = canal, sinus = cavity; condyle/head = joint; trochanter/tuberosity/tubercle/epicondyle = muscle attachment.
  • 8 cranial bones (PEST OF 6): Parietal(2), Ethmoid, Sphenoid, Temporal(2), Occipital, Frontal. Foramen magnum = occipital (spinal cord); sella turcica = sphenoid (pituitary); cribriform plate = ethmoid (smell). Sphenoid = keystone of cranium.
  • Face: Maxilla = keystone of face; Mandible = only movable. Hyoid = no joint. Sutures: coronal, sagittal, lambdoid, squamous. Fontanelles = infant soft spots (birth + brain growth).
  • Spine (26): Cervical 7 (C1 atlas, C2 axis/dens), Thoracic 12, Lumbar 5, sacrum (5 fused), coccyx. Ribs: 7 true, 5 false (last 2 floating). Sternum = manubrium/body/xiphoid.
  • Upper limb: clavicle + scapula (glenoid cavity); humerus → radius (thumb) + ulna (pinky, olecranon) → carpals/metacarpals/phalanges.
  • Lower limb: coxal = ilium+ischium+pubis (acetabulum); femur (longest) → patella → tibia (shin, medial malleolus) + fibula (lateral malleolus) → tarsals (calcaneus)/metatarsals/phalanges.
💪 MUSCLE TISSUE (Ch. 11)
  • 3 types: Skeletal (voluntary, striated, multinucleated), Cardiac (involuntary, striated), Smooth (involuntary, no striations).
  • 4 properties: Excitability, Contractility, Extensibility, Elasticity ("Every Cat Eats Eggs").
  • Sheaths: Epimysium (whole muscle) → Perimysium (fascicle) → Endomysium (fiber). Tendon = muscle-to-bone.
  • Sarcomere = contractile unit (Z disc to Z disc). Thick = myosin (A band, dark); Thin = actin (I band, light) + troponin/tropomyosin. H zone & M line in center.
  • Sliding filament: filaments slide (don't shrink); I band & H zone narrow, A band stays.
  • NMJ: ACh → AP along sarcolemma/T tubules → SR releases Ca²⁺ (final trigger) → Ca binds troponin → cross-bridges. Motor unit = 1 neuron + its fibers.
  • Contractions: Isometric (no length change), Isotonic concentric (shortens) / eccentric (lengthens). Twitch = latent/contraction/relaxation. Force ↑ via recruitment, summation, tetanus.
  • Energy: ATP only direct fuel (4–6 s stored). Sources: creatine phosphate (fastest, ~15 s) → anaerobic glycolysis (lactic acid) → aerobic (most ATP). Fibers: Type I (slow, red, endurance) vs Type II (fast, white, quick fatigue).

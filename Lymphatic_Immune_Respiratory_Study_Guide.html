Master how each system is built and how it works, and the whole picture comes together.

🧬 The Big Picture: Defense & Gas Exchange

TOPIC 1 💧 Lymphatic System Overview MUST KNOW▼

The lymphatic system is the body's drainage and surveillance network. It has two jobs: (1) return leaked fluid and plasma proteins to the blood, and (2) house the immune cells that protect the body.

Three Parts of the System

💧 Lymph
Interstitial fluid once it enters lymphatic vessels. Carries proteins, debris, WBCs, pathogens, even cancer cells — back to the blood.		 🧮 Lymphatic vessels
A one-way system carrying lymph toward the heart: capillaries → collecting vessels → trunks & ducts.		 🍁 Lymph nodes
Bean-shaped filters scattered along the vessels (covered in Topic 2).

Lymphatic Capillaries

  • Very permeable — take up cell debris, pathogens, and cancer cells. Have minivalves for one-way entry, held open by collagen filaments that prevent collapse.
  • Absent from: bones, teeth, bone marrow, and the CNS.
  • Lacteals — special lymphatic capillaries in the intestinal lining that absorb digested fat, forming milky chyle.

The Two Big Ducts

  • Right lymphatic duct — drains the right upper limb and right side of head and thorax only.
  • Thoracic duct — arises from the cisterna chyli and drains the entire rest of the body (the much larger territory).
  • Each empties into venous circulation at the junction of the internal jugular and subclavian veins.

How Lymph Moves (no pump!)

There's no heart for lymph, so it's propelled by: pulsations of nearby arteries, contraction of smooth muscle in vessel walls, the muscular pump (skeletal muscle squeezing), and the respiratory pump (pressure changes from breathing). Valves keep it moving one way.

💡 Mnemonic: "Lymph goes one way — toward the heart." The thoracic duct drains "the most" (everything except the right-upper quadrant). Lacteals = Latte (absorb fatty/milky chyle). Lymph moves the same way veins do: muscular + respiratory pumps + valves.
⚠️ Common confusion: The right lymphatic duct drains only a small region (right arm + right head/thorax); the thoracic duct drains everything else. Also, lymphatic vessels are a one-way system (toward the heart) — unlike blood, lymph doesn't circulate in a loop.

🩺 Clinical Scenario

Show answer

TOPIC 2 🍁 Lymphoid Cells, Tissues & Organs MUST KNOW▼

Lymphoid Cells

  • Lymphocytes — the main immune warriors. T cells manage the response and attack foreign cells; B cells become plasma cells that secrete antibodies.
  • Macrophages — phagocytize foreign material and help activate T cells.
  • Dendritic cells — capture antigens and deliver them to lymph nodes (a type of antigen-presenting cell).
  • Reticular cells — produce the stroma (supportive mesh) that holds other cells in lymphoid organs.

Lymph Nodes — the Principal Lymphoid Organ

Lymph nodes cluster in the inguinal (groin), axillary (armpit), and cervical (neck) regions. Two jobs: filter lymph (macrophages destroy microbes/debris) and act as an immune activation site (lymphocytes mount attacks). Lymph enters via afferent vessels and leaves via fewer efferent vessels at the hilum — the bottleneck slows flow so immune cells have time to work.

Memory aid: Afferent = Arrive; Efferent = Exit.

The Other Lymphoid Organs

OrganKey facts
SpleenLargest lymphoid organ. Cleanses blood of old RBCs/platelets/debris, stores RBC breakdown products (iron) & platelets, immune surveillance. White pulp = immune (lymphocytes); red pulp = RBC disposal (macrophages).
ThymusSite of T-cell maturation (become immunocompetent). Most active in childhood, then atrophies. The "odd one out" — it does NOT directly fight antigens.
TonsilsSimplest lymphoid organs; ring the pharynx. Tonsillar crypts trap bacteria. Palatine (back of mouth), lingual (tongue base), pharyngeal/adenoid (nasopharynx).
Peyer's patches & appendixLymphoid follicle clusters in the intestine; destroy bacteria and generate memory lymphocytes.

MALT

Mucosa-Associated Lymphatic Tissue (MALT) = lymphoid tissue guarding mucous membranes: tonsils, Peyer's patches, and appendix (digestive tract) plus lymphoid nodules in the bronchi (respiratory tract). It protects the openings where pathogens try to enter — this is the overlap with the respiratory system!

💡 Mnemonic: T cells mature in the Thymus (both start with T); B cells mature in Bone marrow (both start with B). Spleen: "White = fight (immune), Red = dead (old RBC disposal)." Lymph node flow: Afferent Arrives, Efferent Exits.
⚠️ Common confusion: The thymus is unusual — it only matures T cells and does NOT directly attack antigens. Also don't confuse the spleen's two pulps: white pulp = immune function, red pulp = filtering/destroying old RBCs.

  1. Where do B cells and T cells mature?

  2. What are the two functions of lymph nodes?
    Show answer
    Filter lymph (macrophages destroy microbes/debris) and serve as a site of immune activation (lymphocytes attack antigens).
  3. Differentiate white pulp from red pulp in the spleen.
    Show answer
    White pulp = lymphocytes/immune function; red pulp = macrophages disposing of worn-out RBCs and bloodborne pathogens.
  4. What does MALT protect, and name two examples.
    Show answer
    It protects the mucous membranes of the digestive and respiratory tracts. Examples: tonsils, Peyer's patches, appendix, bronchial nodules.

    A) Thymus · B) Spleen · C) Tonsil · D) Lymph node

  2. Which organ functions strictly in T-cell maturation?

  3. Lymph exits a lymph node through which vessels?
    A) Afferent · B) Efferent · C) Venous · D) Lacteals

🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient who had their spleen removed (splenectomy) after trauma is told they're now more prone to certain infections. Why does losing the spleen affect immunity and blood?
Show answer
The spleen filters bloodborne pathogens (white pulp immune surveillance) and removes old RBCs (red pulp). Without it, the body is less able to clear certain encapsulated bacteria from the blood, raising infection risk; other organs partly compensate for RBC recycling.

TOPIC 3 🛡️ Innate Immunity I: 1st & 2nd Lines of Defense MUST KNOW▼

Immunity is resistance to disease. There are two big systems: innate (nonspecific) defenses that respond the same way to any threat, and adaptive (specific) defenses that target particular invaders (Topics 5–6). The innate system has the 1st and 2nd lines; adaptive is the 3rd line.

1st Line: Surface Barriers

  • Skin — physical barrier; acidic, keratinized (resists acids, bases, enzymes, toxins).
  • Mucous membranes — trap pathogens. The ciliary escalator sweeps dust/mucus out of the airways.
  • Chemical barriers — lysozyme (in tears/saliva) kills microbes; acidic sebum, sweat (dermcidin), gastric HCl, acidic urine and vaginal secretions all inhibit microbes.

2nd Line: Internal Cellular & Chemical Defenses

  • Phagocytes — macrophages (from monocytes; e.g., Kupffer cells in liver, microglia in brain, alveolar macrophages in lungs) and neutrophils (become phagocytic at infection sites). Eosinophils attack parasitic worms.
  • Natural Killer (NK) cells — "police" the blood/lymph; lyse cancer cells and virus-infected cells before adaptive immunity kicks in. Nonspecific, NOT phagocytic; kill by releasing perforins.
  • Plus inflammation, antimicrobial proteins, and fever (Topic 4).

Phagocyte Mobilization (4 steps)

  1. Leukocytosis — more WBCs (neutrophils) released into blood.
  2. Margination — neutrophils cling to capillary walls.
  3. Diapedesis — they flatten and squeeze through the wall.
  4. Chemotaxis — they follow the chemical trail to the injury.
💡 Mnemonic: "1st = barriers, 2nd = cells & chemicals, 3rd = specific." Phagocyte steps = "Lonely Macrophages Don't Care" → Leukocytosis, Margination, Diapedesis, Chemotaxis. NK = "Natural Killers" lyse with Perforins (poke holes).
⚠️ Common confusion: NK cells are innate and nonspecific (they don't need prior exposure and aren't phagocytic) — don't confuse them with cytotoxic T cells, which are adaptive and antigen-specific. Both kill infected cells, but only T cells "remember."

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Which lines of defense are innate, and which is adaptive?
    Show answer
    1st (surface barriers) and 2nd (internal cellular/chemical) are innate; the 3rd line (B & T cells) is adaptive.
  2. What do NK cells do, and how do they kill?
    Show answer
    They lyse cancer cells and virus-infected cells (nonspecifically) by releasing perforins, before adaptive immunity is active.
  3. List the 4 steps of phagocyte mobilization in order.
    Show answer
    Leukocytosis → margination → diapedesis → chemotaxis.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The most important mechanism of the 2nd line of defense is:
    A) Skin · B) Inflammation · C) Antibodies · D) T cells
    Show answer
    B. Inflammation.
  2. The enzyme in tears and saliva that kills microbes is:
    A) Pepsin · B) Lysozyme · C) Perforin · D) Surfactant

  3. Neutrophils squeezing through capillary walls is called:

🩺 Clinical Scenario
A smoker's "ciliary escalator" is paralyzed by smoke, and they get frequent respiratory infections. Connect the damaged 1st-line defense to the infections.
Show answer
The ciliary escalator normally sweeps trapped dust and pathogen-laden mucus up and out of the airways. Smoke paralyzes the cilia, so mucus and microbes pool in the lower airways, leading to repeated infections — a failure of a 1st-line barrier.

TOPIC 4 🔥 Innate Immunity II: Inflammation, Interferons, Complement & Fever MUST KNOW▼

The Inflammatory Response

Triggered whenever tissue is injured or infected. Benefits: prevents spread of damaging agents, disposes of debris and pathogens, and sets the stage for repair. The 4 cardinal signs are redness, heat, swelling, and pain (sometimes + loss of function).

  • Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on macrophages recognize classes of microbes and trigger cytokine release.
  • Inflammatory mediators — histamine (from mast cells), kinins, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and complement.
  • These cause vasodilation (→ hyperemia/redness/heat) and increased permeability (→ edema). The leaked fluid is exudate, which carries clotting proteins and antibodies and moves debris into lymphatics.

Interferons (IFNs) — the Antiviral Alarm

A virus-infected cell secretes interferons that enter neighboring cells and make them produce antiviral proteins that block viral reproduction. (The infected cell may die, but it warns its neighbors.) IFNs also reduce inflammation and activate macrophages/NK cells; engineered versions treat hepatitis, genital warts, and MS.

Complement — ~20 Blood Proteins

Complement (C1–C9, plus factors) circulates inactive and is the major mechanism for destroying foreign cells. It amplifies inflammation and can directly lyse cells. Two activation pathways:

  • Classical pathway — antibodies bind the invader, then C1 attaches (complement fixation).
  • Alternative pathway — triggered directly on the microbe surface (C3, B, D, P).

Activated complement causes three things: inflammation (C3a), opsonization (C3b tags cells for phagocytosis), and cell lysis via the membrane attack complex (MAC), which punches a hole so water floods in and bursts the cell.

Fever

A systemic response to invaders. Pyrogens (from leukocytes/macrophages) reset the hypothalamic thermostat higher. Benefits: makes the liver and spleen sequester iron and zinc (which microbes need) and speeds up metabolism/repair.

💡 Mnemonic: Cardinal signs = "Red Hot Swollen Painful." Interferon = "Interferes" with viruses (warns neighbors). Complement's 3 jobs = "Lyse, Label, Lure" (lysis via MAC, opsonization/labeling, inflammation that lures cells). Fever = "hide the iron" from microbes.
⚠️ Common confusion: Interferons don't save the already-infected cell — they protect neighboring cells. And complement, inflammation, fever, and interferons are all innate (nonspecific), even though complement can be activated by antibodies (the classical pathway).

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. List the 4 cardinal signs of inflammation.
    Show answer
    Redness, heat, swelling, and pain.
  2. How do interferons protect the body?
    Show answer
    A virus-infected cell secretes IFNs that cause neighboring cells to make antiviral proteins, blocking viral reproduction.
  3. Name complement's three effects.
    Show answer
    Inflammation, opsonization (C3b tagging), and cell lysis (membrane attack complex).
  4. How does fever help fight infection?
    Show answer
    It causes the liver/spleen to sequester iron and zinc (needed by microbes) and increases metabolic rate to speed repair.

🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with a bacterial infection develops fever and the doctor decides not to suppress a mild fever. Using innate immunity, why might a moderate fever be helpful?
Show answer
A moderate fever causes the liver and spleen to sequester iron and zinc that bacteria need to multiply, and it speeds up metabolic/immune activity for faster repair — so a mild fever can actually aid the fight against infection.

TOPIC 5 🧬 Adaptive Immunity: Antigens, B & T Cells MUST KNOW▼

The adaptive (specific) immune system is the 3rd line of defense. It has three defining features: it is specific (targets a particular antigen), systemic (not limited to one site), and it has memory (faster, stronger response next time).

Antigens

An antigen is a substance that provokes an immune response — usually a large, complex molecule the body sees as "nonself." The specific spots immune cells bind to are antigenic determinants (epitopes). Examples of antigens: bacteria/toxins, viruses, mismatched RBCs, cancer cells.

The Two Arms of Adaptive Immunity

Humoral (antibody-mediated)Cellular (cell-mediated)
Main cellB cellsT cells
How it worksB cells become plasma cells that secrete antibodies into body fluids ("humors").T cells directly attack infected/abnormal cells or manage the response.
Best againstFree-floating pathogens (bacteria, toxins, viruses in blood/lymph).Intracellular threats (virus-infected cells, cancer, transplants).

Where the Cells Come From & Mature

All lymphocytes form in red bone marrow. To become immunocompetent, each must learn to recognize its one specific antigen: B cells mature in the bone marrow, T cells mature in the thymus.

Antigen-Presenting Cells (APCs)

APCs — mainly dendritic cells and macrophages — engulf an antigen, digest it, and display a piece on their surface to activate T cells. This is the bridge between innate and adaptive immunity. (Dendritic cells are the most effective APCs.)

💡 Mnemonic: Adaptive = "Specific, Systemic, Smart (memory)." B = Bone marrow + antiBodies (humoral); T = Thymus + aTTack cells (cellular). Humoral fights free pathogens; cellular fights infected cells.
⚠️ Common confusion: Humoral = B cells/antibodies (think "humors" = body fluids), best vs free pathogens. Cellular = T cells, best vs intracellular threats. Both are adaptive. APCs (dendritic cells/macrophages) link innate to adaptive — they don't kill on their own, they present.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What three features define adaptive immunity?
    Show answer
    It is specific, systemic, and has memory.
  2. Differentiate humoral from cellular immunity.
    Show answer
    Humoral = B cells/plasma cells secreting antibodies (against free pathogens); cellular = T cells directly attacking infected/abnormal cells.
  3. What does an antigen-presenting cell do?
    Show answer
    Engulfs and digests an antigen, then displays a fragment on its surface to activate T cells (e.g., dendritic cells, macrophages).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Antibodies are secreted by:

    B. Plasma cells, which develop from B cells.
  2. Cell-mediated (cellular) immunity is carried out by:

  3. The most effective antigen-presenting cells are:

🩺 Clinical Scenario
HIV destroys helper T cells. Using the two arms of adaptive immunity, explain why losing helper T cells cripples the entire adaptive response, not just the cellular arm.
Show answer
Helper T cells "manage the immune response" — they activate both cytotoxic T cells (cellular) AND B cells (humoral). Without them, B cells aren't properly stimulated to make antibodies and T-cell attacks falter, so both arms collapse, leaving the body open to opportunistic infections.

TOPIC 6 🎯 Antibodies & Active vs Passive Immunity MUST KNOW▼

Antibodies (immunoglobulins, Ig) are Y-shaped proteins made by plasma cells. They don't kill directly — they tag, clump, and neutralize antigens so other defenses can finish the job.

The 5 Antibody Classes

ClassKey facts
IgMFirst antibody released in a response; potent agglutinating (clumping) agent. (Largest.)
IgAIn secretions — saliva, sweat, intestinal juices, milk; stops pathogens attaching to epithelial surfaces.
IgDAttached to the B cell surface; functions as a B-cell receptor.
IgGMost abundant (75–85% of plasma antibodies); protects against bacteria, viruses, toxins; crosses the placenta to the fetus.
IgEActive in allergies and parasitic infections; triggers mast cells/basophils to release histamine.

Active vs. Passive Immunity

Active ImmunityPassive Immunity
Who makes the antibodies?Your own body makes themSomeone else's antibodies are given to you
Memory?Yes — long-lastingNo — temporary
Natural exampleGetting the infection & recoveringAntibodies via placenta or breast milk
Artificial exampleVaccine (you make the response)Antibody injection (e.g., antivenom, immune globulin)
💡 Mnemonic: Antibody classes spell "M A D G E." IgM = iMmediate (first); IgA = secretions (sAliva); IgG = Greatest amount + Goes to baby (placenta); IgE = allErgy. Active = you Act (make your own, lasts); Passive = Passed to you (borrowed, temporary).
⚠️ Common confusion: IgM is the first antibody in a response; IgG is the most abundant and the one that crosses the placenta. A vaccine = active (your body builds memory); receiving antibodies (or maternal antibodies) = passive (no memory, temporary).

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Which antibody is released first, and which is most abundant?
    Show answer
    IgM is released first; IgG is the most abundant (and crosses the placenta).
  2. Which antibody is found in secretions like saliva and breast milk?
    Show answer
    IgA.
  3. Differentiate active from passive immunity.
    Show answer
    Active = your body makes its own antibodies and memory (infection or vaccine), long-lasting. Passive = you receive preformed antibodies (placenta, breast milk, injection), temporary, no memory.
  4. Which antibody is involved in allergies?
    Show answer
    IgE (triggers histamine release from mast cells/basophils).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. A vaccine provides which type of immunity?
    A) Natural passive · B) Artificial passive · C) Artificial active · D) Natural active
    Show answer
    C. Artificial active — the vaccine makes YOUR body build its own response and memory.
  2. Antibodies passed from mother to fetus across the placenta provide:
    A) Natural active · B) Natural passive · C) Artificial active · D) No immunity
    Show answer
    B. Natural passive immunity (temporary, no memory).
  3. Which antibody crosses the placenta?
    A) IgA · B) IgE · C) IgG · D) IgM
    Show answer
    C. IgG.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A person bitten by a venomous snake is given antivenom (preformed antibodies), and is also advised to keep their tetanus vaccine current. Which gives active and which gives passive immunity, and why does the timing differ?
Show answer
Antivenom = passive immunity — preformed antibodies work immediately but are temporary (no memory). The tetanus vaccine = active immunity — slower to develop but builds lasting memory. You need the fast borrowed antibodies now (venom) and the durable self-made protection later (tetanus).

TOPIC 7 🫁 Respiratory Anatomy & Zones MUST KNOW▼

The 4 Processes of Respiration

ProcessWhat happens
1. Pulmonary ventilationBreathing — air moves in and out of the lungs.
2. External respirationO&sub2;/CO&sub2; exchange between lungs (alveoli) and blood.
3. TransportO&sub2; and CO&sub2; carried in the blood.
4. Internal respirationO&sub2;/CO&sub2; exchange between blood and tissues.

Respiration takes both the respiratory AND circulatory systems working together.

Major Organs (air's journey down)

Nose / nasal cavity & sinuses → pharynx (throat) → larynx (voice box) → trachea (windpipe) → bronchi and their branches → lungs & alveoli.

Conducting Zone vs. Respiratory Zone

Conducting ZoneRespiratory Zone
JobConduits — carry, warm, humidify & clean air. NO gas exchange.Gas exchange happens here.
StructuresNose → trachea → bronchi → terminal bronchioles.Respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, alveolar sacs & alveoli.

The airways branch ~23 times — the bronchial (respiratory) tree.

Bronchial Branching — a Key Clinical Detail

Trachea → right & left main (primary) bronchi → lobar (secondary) bronchi (3 on the right, 2 on the left — matching the lung lobes). The right main bronchus is wider, shorter, and more vertical — so inhaled objects usually lodge on the right.

💡 Mnemonic: "Conducting = Conduits (no exchange); Respiratory zone = where Respiration (exchange) happens." Lobes: Right has 3, Left has 2 (the left makes room for the heart). Right bronchus = Wider, shorter, vertical → aspiration goes Right.
⚠️ Common confusion: External respiration = lungs↔blood; internal respiration = blood↔tissues (don't reverse them — "external" is near the outside air). And only the respiratory zone exchanges gas; the conducting zone is just plumbing (anatomical dead space).

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Name the 4 processes of respiration.
    Show answer
    Pulmonary ventilation, external respiration, gas transport, internal respiration.
  2. What's the difference between the conducting and respiratory zones?
    Show answer
    Conducting zone moves/cleans air with no gas exchange; respiratory zone (respiratory bronchioles, ducts, alveoli) is where gas exchange occurs.
  3. Why do aspirated objects usually end up in the right lung?
    Show answer
    The right main bronchus is wider, shorter, and more vertical, so gravity tends to direct objects there.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Gas exchange between the lungs and blood is called:
    A) Ventilation · B) External respiration · C) Internal respiration · D) Transport
    Show answer
    B. External respiration (lungs ↔ blood).
  2. How many lobar (secondary) bronchi does the right lung have?
    A) 1 · B) 2 · C) 3 · D) 4
    Show answer
    C. Three (the right lung has 3 lobes; the left has 2).
  3. The main site of gas exchange is the:
    A) Trachea · B) Bronchi · C) Alveoli · D) Pharynx
    Show answer
    C. Alveoli (respiratory zone).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A toddler inhales a small peanut and develops wheezing localized to the right lung. Why is the right lung the more common site for an aspirated object?
Show answer
The right main bronchus is wider, shorter, and more vertical than the left, so inhaled objects follow the straighter path and lodge in the right lung more often.

TOPIC 8 🌪️ Alveoli, Respiratory Membrane & Pleurae MUST KNOW▼

Alveoli — the Gas-Exchange Sacs

There are ~300 million alveoli, accounting for most of the lung's volume and giving a huge surface area (~40× your skin!). Features:

  • Surrounded by elastic fibers and a web of capillaries.
  • Alveolar pores connect adjacent alveoli and equalize air pressure.
  • Alveolar macrophages ("dust cells") keep the surfaces sterile.

The Respiratory Membrane (air-blood barrier)

The respiratory membrane is only ~0.5 µm thick — where alveolar and capillary walls meet. Two cell types line the alveolus:

  • Type I cells — single layer of squamous epithelium forming the wall (thin = fast diffusion).
  • Type II cells — secrete surfactant (reduces surface tension; Topic 10) and antimicrobial proteins.

If the membrane thickens (e.g., pulmonary edema/"waterlogged" lungs), gas exchange becomes inadequate. Emphysema destroys alveolar walls, reducing surface area.

The Pleurae

A thin, double-layered serous membrane around each lung:

  • Parietal pleura — lines the thoracic wall and diaphragm (outer).
  • Visceral pleura — covers the lung surface (inner).
  • Pleural fluid fills the thin pleural cavity: it lubricates (so layers glide) and creates surface tension that holds the lung against the chest wall.

Blood Supply (two circuits)

  • Pulmonary circulation (low pressure, high volume) — pulmonary arteries bring deoxygenated blood to be oxygenated; pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood.
  • Bronchial circulation (systemic, high pressure) — bronchial arteries feed the lung tissue itself with oxygenated blood.
💡 Mnemonic: "Type 1 = thin wall (won = one layer); Type 2 = surfactant (II = "oil")." Pleurae: "Parietal = wall (Parietal = Perimeter); Visceral = lung surface." The membrane is thin on purpose — thick = trouble.
⚠️ Common confusion: Type II cells make surfactant (not Type I, which just form the thin wall). And pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood (toward the lungs) — one of the few places arteries are oxygen-poor.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Which alveolar cell makes surfactant, and which forms the wall?
    Show answer
    Type II cells secrete surfactant; Type I cells form the thin squamous wall.
  2. Name the two pleural layers and the function of pleural fluid.
    Show answer
    Parietal (thoracic wall) and visceral (lung surface); pleural fluid lubricates and provides surface tension holding the lung to the chest wall.
  3. Why does pulmonary edema impair gas exchange?
    Show answer
    Fluid thickens the respiratory membrane, increasing diffusion distance so O&sub2; and CO&sub2; cross less efficiently.

  1. Surfactant is produced by:
    A) Type I cells · B) Type II cells · C) Alveolar macrophages · D) Goblet cells
    Show answer
    B. Type II alveolar cells.
  2. The pleura covering the lung surface is the:
    A) Parietal pleura · B) Visceral pleura · C) Pericardium · D) Peritoneum
    Show answer
    B. Visceral pleura.
  3. Emphysema reduces gas exchange mainly by:

🩺 Clinical Scenario
A premature infant struggles to breathe because their Type II cells aren't making enough surfactant yet (infant respiratory distress syndrome). Why does low surfactant make breathing so hard?
Show answer
Surfactant reduces alveolar surface tension. Without enough, surface tension pulls alveoli closed after each breath, so the baby must use enormous effort to re-inflate collapsed alveoli with every breath — exhausting and inefficient.

TOPIC 9 💨 Mechanics of Breathing & Pressures MUST KNOW▼

Breathing is all about pressure and volume. Air always flows from high pressure to low pressure, and (Boyle's Law) when a container's volume increases, its pressure decreases. The lungs exploit this.

The Key Pressures

PressureWhere & behavior
AtmosphericAir around the body; 760 mm Hg at sea level. The reference point.
Intrapulmonary (intra-alveolar)Pressure in the alveoli; rises and falls with breathing; equalizes with atmospheric between breaths.
IntrapleuralPressure in the pleural cavity; always negative (less than intrapulmonary) — this keeps the lungs inflated.

Why Intrapleural Pressure Stays Negative

Two inward forces try to collapse the lung (elastic recoil of the lung + alveolar surface tension), and one outward force (elasticity of the chest wall) pulls the thorax out. The tug-of-war creates a negative intrapleural pressure that holds the lungs open against the chest wall.

Inspiration vs. Expiration

  • Inspiration is ACTIVE — inspiratory muscles (diaphragm + external intercostals) contract, thoracic volume increases, intrapulmonary pressure drops below atmospheric, and air rushes in.
  • Quiet expiration is PASSIVE — muscles relax, the elastic lungs recoil, volume decreases, pressure rises above atmospheric, and air flows out. (Forced expiration is active, using abdominal + internal intercostal muscles.)

When Pressure Is Lost: Atelectasis & Pneumothorax

Atelectasis = partial/complete lung collapse (e.g., a plugged bronchiole or a chest wound). A pneumothorax — air entering the pleural cavity through a wound — destroys the negative intrapleural pressure, so the lung collapses.

💡 Mnemonic: Boyle's Law: "bigger box = lower pressure." Inspiration = In takes effort (active); quiet expiration is easy (passive). Intrapleural is ALWAYS negative — lose that negativity (pneumothorax) and the lung collapses.
⚠️ Common confusion: Inspiration is always active; quiet expiration is passive (just recoil). It's the reverse of what people guess. And remember intrapleural pressure is always negative relative to intrapulmonary — that gradient is what keeps lungs inflated.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. During inspiration, what happens to thoracic volume and intrapulmonary pressure?
    Show answer
    Volume increases; intrapulmonary pressure drops below atmospheric (negative), so air flows in.
  2. Why is intrapleural pressure always negative?
    Show answer
    Two inward forces (lung elastic recoil + alveolar surface tension) oppose one outward force (chest wall elasticity), netting a negative pressure that holds lungs open.
  3. Is quiet expiration active or passive? What about forced expiration?
    Show answer
    Quiet expiration is passive (elastic recoil); forced expiration is active (abdominal + internal intercostal muscles).

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Air flows into the lungs when intrapulmonary pressure is:
    A) Equal to atmospheric · B) Greater than atmospheric · C) Less than atmospheric · D) Zero
    Show answer
    C. Below atmospheric (negative), so air moves in.
  2. Air entering the pleural cavity causing lung collapse is a:
    A) Atelectasis from mucus · B) Pneumothorax · C) Pleurisy · D) Pulmonary embolism
    Show answer
    B. Pneumothorax (a cause of atelectasis).
  3. The primary muscle of quiet inspiration is the:
    A) Diaphragm · B) Rectus abdominis · C) Internal intercostals · D) Sternocleidomastoid
    Show answer
    A. The diaphragm.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A stabbing victim has a chest wound that opens the pleural cavity to outside air, and that lung collapses. Using pressure relationships, explain exactly why the lung deflates.
Show answer
The lung stays inflated because intrapleural pressure is negative. The wound lets outside air into the pleural cavity (pneumothorax), equalizing intrapleural pressure with atmospheric. With the negative gradient gone, the lung's natural elastic recoil pulls it inward and it collapses (atelectasis).

TOPIC 10 🧤 Ventilation Factors & Lung Volumes ▼

3 Factors That Make Breathing Harder

Inspiratory muscles spend energy overcoming three things:

FactorWhat it means
Airway resistanceFriction in the airways. Constriction (e.g., asthma) raises resistance and can stop ventilation. Epinephrine dilates bronchioles and lowers resistance.
Alveolar surface tensionWater molecules pull inward, trying to collapse alveoli. Surfactant (from Type II cells) reduces it and prevents collapse.
Lung complianceHow easily lungs stretch. High = good. Reduced by fibrosis (scar tissue), low surfactant, or a stiff chest wall.

Respiratory Volumes & Capacities

TermMeaning
Tidal volume (TV)Air moved in/out in a normal quiet breath (~500 mL).
Inspiratory reserve (IRV)Extra air you can forcibly inhale beyond a normal breath.
Expiratory reserve (ERV)Extra air you can forcibly exhale beyond a normal breath.
Residual volume (RV)Air that always remains in the lungs (keeps alveoli open).
Vital capacity (VC)Total exchangeable air = TV + IRV + ERV.

A capacity is just two or more volumes added together.

Nonrespiratory Air Movements

Reflex actions that move air for other reasons: cough, sneeze, crying, laughing, hiccups, and yawns.

💡 Mnemonic: 3 hindrances = "Resistance, surface tension, compliance." High compliance = easy breathing (compliant lungs cooperate). Surfactant fights surface tension. Residual volume = the air that Refuses to leave.
⚠️ Common confusion: High compliance is GOOD (lungs stretch easily); fibrosis lowers compliance (stiff lungs, hard to inflate). Don't confuse residual volume (air that can't be exhaled) with expiratory reserve (extra air you can force out).

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. Name the 3 factors that hinder ventilation.
    Show answer
    Airway resistance, alveolar surface tension, and lung compliance.
  2. What is lung compliance, and what reduces it?
    Show answer
    How easily the lungs stretch; reduced by fibrosis (scar tissue), low surfactant, and a stiff/inflexible thoracic cage.
  3. Define vital capacity.
    Show answer
    The total exchangeable air: tidal volume + inspiratory reserve + expiratory reserve.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Epinephrine improves airflow during an asthma attack by:
    A) Increasing surface tension · B) Dilating bronchioles · C) Lowering compliance · D) Constricting airways
    Show answer
    B. It dilates bronchioles, reducing airway resistance.
  2. Air that always remains in the lungs is the:
    A) Tidal volume · B) Vital capacity · C) Residual volume · D) Expiratory reserve
    Show answer
    C. Residual volume.
  3. Pulmonary fibrosis affects the lungs by:
    A) Increasing compliance · B) Decreasing compliance · C) Adding surfactant · D) Widening airways
    Show answer
    B. Scar tissue stiffens the lungs, decreasing compliance.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
During an asthma attack a patient struggles to move air, and an inhaler (a bronchodilator like albuterol) provides relief. Which of the 3 ventilation factors is the problem, and how does the inhaler help?
Show answer
The problem is increased airway resistance from bronchoconstriction. A bronchodilator (like epinephrine/albuterol) relaxes and widens the bronchioles, lowering resistance so air moves more easily.

TOPIC 11 🔥 Gas Exchange & Transport MUST KNOW▼

Two Gas Laws

LawWhat it says
Dalton's Law (partial pressures)Total pressure of a gas mixture = sum of each gas's pressure. Each gas's partial pressure is proportional to its % in the mix.
Henry's Law (solubility)Each gas dissolves in liquid in proportion to its partial pressure and solubility. CO&sub2; is ~20× more soluble in water than O&sub2;.

Gases Move Down Their Pressure Gradients

Gas always diffuses from high partial pressure to low:

  • O&sub2; — steep gradient in the lungs: alveolar Po&sub2; (104 mm Hg) > venous blood (40 mm Hg), so O&sub2; rushes into the blood.
  • CO&sub2; — gentler gradient: venous blood (45) > alveolar (40), so CO&sub2; moves into the alveoli. The gradient is small, but CO&sub2;'s high solubility lets it diffuse in equal amounts.

External respiration (lungs): O&sub2; loads, CO&sub2; unloads. Internal respiration (tissues): the reverse. Good exchange needs ventilation-perfusion coupling — air reaching alveoli (ventilation) must match blood flow (perfusion).

Oxygen Transport on Hemoglobin

~98% of O&sub2; is carried bound to hemoglobin (Hb). Hb is 100% saturated when all 4 heme groups carry O&sub2;. Binding is cooperative — as O&sub2; binds, Hb's affinity rises; as it releases, affinity falls. Factors that make Hb release more O&sub2; to tissues (the Bohr effect):

  • ↑ temperature, ↑ H⁺ (lower pH), ↑ CO&sub2;, ↑ BPG.
  • These all occur in active, metabolizing tissues — so busy tissues automatically get more O&sub2;. Smart design!

Carbon Dioxide Transport (3 ways)

  • 70% as bicarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻) in plasma — the main method; also buffers blood pH.
  • 20% bound to globin of hemoglobin (carbaminohemoglobin) — binds the protein, not the iron.
  • 10% dissolved in plasma.
💡 Mnemonic: CO&sub2; transport = "70 / 20 / 10" (Bicarbonate > Blood protein > "Bare" dissolved). Bohr effect: "hot, acidic, CO&sub2;-rich tissue → drop off more O&sub2;." O&sub2; binds iron; CO&sub2; binds globin.
⚠️ Common confusion: Most CO&sub2; (70%) travels as bicarbonate, NOT on hemoglobin. And the Bohr effect means lower pH / higher CO&sub2; decreases Hb's affinity for O&sub2; — that's helpful, because it unloads O&sub2; exactly where it's needed.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. State Dalton's Law and Henry's Law in one line each.
    Show answer
    Dalton: total pressure = sum of partial pressures (each ∝ its %). Henry: each gas dissolves in proportion to its partial pressure and solubility.
  2. List the 3 ways CO&sub2; is transported and their percentages.
    Show answer
    70% as bicarbonate, 20% on hemoglobin (carbaminohemoglobin), 10% dissolved in plasma.
  3. What conditions cause hemoglobin to release more O&sub2; (Bohr effect)?
    Show answer
    Increased temperature, increased H⁺ (lower pH), increased CO&sub2;, and increased BPG — all features of active tissue.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Most carbon dioxide is transported in the blood as:
    A) Dissolved CO&sub2; · B) Carbaminohemoglobin · C) Bicarbonate ions · D) Carbonic acid gas
    Show answer
    C. Bicarbonate ions (~70%).
  2. Hemoglobin is fully saturated when:
    A) One heme carries O&sub2; · B) Two hemes carry O&sub2; · C) All four hemes carry O&sub2; · D) It binds CO&sub2;
    Show answer
    C. All four heme groups carry O&sub2; (100% saturated).
  3. The Bohr effect describes how O&sub2; unloading increases when blood is:
    A) Cold and alkaline · B) Warm and acidic with high CO&sub2; · C) Low in CO&sub2; · D) High in pH
    Show answer
    B. Warm, acidic, high-CO&sub2; conditions weaken the Hb–O&sub2; bond, releasing O&sub2;.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
During intense exercise, a muscle is hot, acidic, and full of CO&sub2;. Explain how the Bohr effect makes sure that exact muscle gets more oxygen.
Show answer
Active muscle produces heat, H⁺ (lower pH), and CO&sub2;. These conditions reduce hemoglobin's affinity for O&sub2; (Bohr effect), so Hb releases more O&sub2; right where metabolism is highest — an elegant, automatic supply-and-demand match.

TOPIC 12 🫀 Control of Breathing & Respiratory Disorders MUST KNOW▼

The Respiratory Control Centers (in the medulla)

  • Ventral respiratory group (VRG) — the rhythm generator; sets normal breathing (eupnea, 12–15 breaths/min) and drives the inspiratory muscles via the phrenic (diaphragm) and intercostal nerves.
  • Dorsal respiratory group (DRG) — integrates input from peripheral stretch receptors and chemoreceptors.

Depth of breathing = how strongly the center stimulates the muscles. Rate = how long the inspiratory center stays active. Both adjust to the body's needs.

The #1 Breathing Stimulus — a Surprise

Rising CO&sub2; (not low O&sub2;) is the most powerful respiratory stimulant.
Increased CO&sub2; → more H⁺ (lower pH) in the brain → the medulla drives faster, deeper breathing to blow off CO&sub2;.
  • Hyperventilation — breathing too much removes too much CO&sub2; (hypocapnia), which can cause cerebral vasoconstriction and dizziness.
  • Hyperpnea — the increased ventilation that matches exercise (10–20×) to meet metabolic demand. (Different from hyperventilation — hyperpnea keeps blood gases normal.)

Respiratory Disorders to Know

DisorderKey facts
COPDIncludes chronic bronchitis & emphysema. Irreversible ↓ ability to exhale. ~80% have a smoking history. Features: dyspnea ("air hunger"), chronic cough, frequent infections; can progress to respiratory acidosis.
AsthmaCoughing, dyspnea, wheezing, chest tightness. Active airway inflammation (IgE-driven immune response) precedes bronchospasm. Reversible — the link to the immune system from Ch. 21!
Lung cancerLeading cause of cancer death; ~90% from smoking. Three main types: squamous cell carcinoma (bronchial), adenocarcinoma (peripheral, ~40%), small cell carcinoma (aggressive, metastasizes early).
💡 Mnemonic: "CO&sub2; is the boss of breathing" — rising CO&sub2; drives you to breathe, not low O&sub2;. VRG = Rhythm; eupnea = "you-pnea" = normal 12–15. COPD = Can't Of-load (exhale) air; asthma = immune/IgE wheeze.
⚠️ Common confusion: The strongest breathing driver is high CO&sub2;, not low O&sub2;. Also, hyperpnea (exercise; blood gases stay normal) is NOT the same as hyperventilation (excess breathing that drops CO&sub2; below normal). Asthma ties back to immunity — it's an IgE-driven inflammatory response.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What is the most powerful stimulus to breathe, and why?
    Show answer
    Rising CO&sub2; — it lowers brain pH (more H⁺), which strongly drives the medulla to increase rate and depth of breathing.
  2. Differentiate hyperventilation from hyperpnea.
    Show answer
    Hyperventilation removes too much CO&sub2; (hypocapnia); hyperpnea is increased ventilation that matches metabolic need (exercise) while keeping blood gases normal.
  3. What two conditions make up COPD, and what's the hallmark difficulty?
    Show answer
    Chronic bronchitis and emphysema; the hallmark is an irreversible decrease in the ability to force air OUT (exhale), with dyspnea.

🩺 Clinical Scenario
A panicking patient hyperventilates and becomes lightheaded and tingly. Using CO&sub2; control of breathing, explain what's happening and why breathing into a paper bag can help.
Show answer

Rebreathing exhaled air (paper bag) restores CO&sub2; levels, normalizing pH and symptoms. (Use cautiously — only when hyperventilation is truly the cause.)

🎯 Exam Cheat Sheet

💧 LYMPHATIC SYSTEM & IMMUNITY
  • Lymphatic jobs: return leaked fluid/proteins to blood + house immune cells. Lymph flows ONE way (toward heart).
  • Thoracic duct drains most of body; right lymphatic duct drains right arm + right head/thorax. Lacteals absorb fat.
  • B cells mature in Bone marrow; T cells mature in Thymus. Spleen: white pulp = immune, red pulp = old RBC disposal.
  • MALT = mucosa-associated lymphatic tissue (tonsils, Peyer's patches, appendix, bronchial nodules).
  • 3 lines of defense: 1st = barriers (skin/mucosa), 2nd = innate cells/chemicals (phagocytes, NK, inflammation, complement, fever), 3rd = adaptive (B/T cells).
  • Phagocyte steps: Leukocytosis → Margination → Diapedesis → Chemotaxis.
  • NK cells = innate, nonspecific, lyse virus/cancer cells with perforins.
  • Inflammation signs: redness, heat, swelling, pain. Interferons = antiviral (warn neighbors). Complement: inflammation + opsonization + lysis (MAC). Fever sequesters iron/zinc.
  • Adaptive = specific, systemic, memory. Humoral = B cells/antibodies (free pathogens); Cellular = T cells (infected cells).
  • Antibodies (MADGE): IgM (first), IgA (secretions), IgD (B-cell receptor), IgG (most abundant, crosses placenta), IgE (allergy).
  • Active immunity = your body makes it (infection/vaccine), lasting. Passive = borrowed antibodies (placenta/injection), temporary.
🫁 RESPIRATORY SYSTEM
  • 4 processes: pulmonary ventilation, external respiration (lungs↔blood), transport, internal respiration (blood↔tissues).
  • Conducting zone = no gas exchange; respiratory zone (alveoli) = exchange. ~300 million alveoli. Right lung 3 lobes, left 2.
  • Right main bronchus = wider, shorter, vertical → aspiration goes right.
  • Respiratory membrane ~0.5 µm. Type I cells = wall; Type II cells = surfactant.
  • Pleurae: parietal (chest wall), visceral (lung). Pleural fluid lubricates + holds lung to wall.
  • Boyle's Law: volume up → pressure down. Inspiration = active; quiet expiration = passive.
  • Intrapleural pressure is always negative — lose it (pneumothorax) → lung collapses (atelectasis).
  • 3 ventilation hindrances: airway resistance, surface tension (fought by surfactant), compliance (high = good).
  • Gas laws: Dalton (partial pressures), Henry (solubility; CO&sub2; 20× more soluble than O&sub2;).
  • O&sub2; ~98% on hemoglobin (binds iron). Bohr effect: heat/↓pH/↑CO&sub2; → more O&sub2; released to tissues.
  • CO&sub2; transport: 70% bicarbonate, 20% on hemoglobin (globin), 10% dissolved.
  • VRG sets rhythm (eupnea 12–15/min). Rising CO&sub2; is the #1 breathing stimulus.
  • COPD (bronchitis + emphysema): can't exhale, smoking, dyspnea. Asthma: IgE inflammation + bronchospasm. Lung cancer: ~90% smoking.

📚 Glossary

Lymphatic & Immunity Terms
  • Lymph — interstitial fluid inside lymphatic vessels.
  • Lacteals — lymphatic capillaries that absorb dietary fat.
  • Thoracic duct — drains most of the body's lymph (from cisterna chyli).
  • Immunocompetent — a lymphocyte able to recognize its specific antigen.
  • Antigen — a substance that provokes an immune response (nonself).
  • Opsonization — tagging a pathogen for easier phagocytosis.
  • Diapedesis — WBCs squeezing through capillary walls.
  • NK cell — innate cell that lyses virus-infected/cancer cells with perforins.
  • Interferon — antiviral protein that protects neighboring cells.
  • Complement — ~20 blood proteins causing inflammation, opsonization, and lysis (MAC).
  • Humoral / Cellular immunity — B-cell antibody response / T-cell direct attack.
  • Active / Passive immunity — self-made (lasting) / borrowed antibodies (temporary).
Respiratory Terms
  • External / Internal respiration — gas exchange at lungs / at tissues.
  • Conducting / Respiratory zone — air passages (no exchange) / alveoli (exchange).
  • Surfactant — Type II cell secretion that lowers alveolar surface tension.
  • Respiratory membrane — the thin air-blood barrier (~0.5 µm).
  • Pleura — double serous membrane around the lung (parietal + visceral).
  • Intrapleural pressure — pressure in the pleural cavity; always negative.
  • Atelectasis — lung collapse; pneumothorax — air in pleural cavity.
  • Compliance — how easily the lungs stretch (high = good).
  • Tidal volume / Vital capacity / Residual volume — normal breath / max exchangeable air / air that always remains.
  • Dalton's / Henry's Law — partial pressures / gas solubility.
  • Bohr effect — lower pH/higher CO&sub2; releases more O&sub2; from hemoglobin.
  • Eupnea — normal quiet breathing (12–15/min). Dyspnea — labored breathing.
